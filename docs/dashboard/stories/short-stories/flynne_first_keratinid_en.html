First Keratinid: Flynne Steps Into the Pod

Origin: Flynne's first time in a keratinid pod

744 wordsPeripheral

The keratin smelled faintly of iodine and something older, like the inside of a coin left too long in a pocket. I was sitting in the trailer living room where the carpet had worn through to the plywood in a perfect oval that matched my mother's rocking chair, except the chair was gone and what sat in its place was a pod. A thing. Burton called it a thing.

The thing was a deep marine-blue, almost the color of the swimming pool at the Clanton Motel that had been drained for the season, and it had a seam running down the middle like a closed eye. When Burton tapped the seam with one finger the whole structure resonated and half-lit, a glow seeping up from inside the shell like the bioluminescence you sometimes see in the cut on a fish.

"Don't scratch it," Burton said. He was standing by the door with his arm crossed over the place where the other arm should have been, which was a posture I had learned not to read as protective. It was just how he stood. The Marines had wired it into him and the pod on our living room carpet had nothing to do with it.

"I wasn't going to scratch it."

"You were thinking about scratching it. I could see you thinking about scratching it."

The technician was a woman named Maida of the Quigley who had driven up from Knoxville in a beige van with "Quigley Orthopedic" written on the side in the kind of blue that hospitals use. She was on her knees next to the pod, fitting a length of soft tubing into a port on the side. The tubing was the color of bone.

"Headset first," she said, "then the pod. We don't run the pod with a cold entry. The body needs the buffer of a brief peripheral session before the long one, or the spine rejects the signal and you spend the next three days vomiting."

I picked up the headset from the coffee table. It was the same one Burton had been wearing for two months, a black band with a smooth patch over the temples and a faint seam where the military sensor had been added. It smelled like a fireplace. It smelled like Burton.

"I'll be right here," Burton said.

"You'll be right here, but I won't be right here. I'll be there. Where the headset goes."

"Yeah."

I put it on.

The headset first go so cold.

Not cold like the kitchen floor in January. Cold like a thought you'd been avoiding finally arriving, the entire fridge of the brain opening at once and the chill of every deferred thing pouring out. Then warm, then nothing, then the familiar soft fall into the peripheral's body that I was starting to associate with the smell of hospitals and a particular shade of medical blue.

I was standing in a room that was not the trailer living room.

It was the staging room, the same staging room I had stood in twice before during Burton's test sessions. There was the same backlit panel on the wall, the same kind of chair that doesn't quite exist as a chair but as a suggestion of one. There was the same voice in my ear.

"Flynne."

"Yes, sir."

"You can drop the sir. You're not in the Marines."

"I'm not in anything."

"You're in the pod. That's something. Open the pod."

I opened my eyes.

I was in the trailer.

The pod was open in front of me, the seam pulled apart, the inside lined with what looked like thousands of tiny pearl-gray needles set in a soft mesh. I could feel the places where the needles had pressed into my back. I could not feel my legs.

"Don't try to stand," Maida of the Quigley said, from somewhere to my left. "The first pod exit is always the longest. Give it a minute."

I gave it a minute.

I gave it three minutes.

I could feel my left foot. I could feel my right foot. I could feel, with a kind of embarrassed relief, the carpet under my left foot, the oval of worn plywood, the exact place where the rocking chair had stood and where my mother had sat and where the pod now stood in its place.

The trailer smelled of iodine.

Burton handed me a glass of water.

I drank.

The water tasted like the future.
