The Blue Silk Wall

1448 wordsHardwired (Walter Jon Williams)

Setup

Maurice had a code for the cleaning of the wall.

The code was a small cut of number on a small cut of paper, the cut of paper he kept in the cash register, the cut of paper that the registry had long ago assigned to the cut of the man who ran the military hardware at the Blue Silk. Maurice had been a cutter at Vandenberg. He had come back from the chair on the night of the Rock War with a hard burn on the side of his face and a hard burn in his memory and a hard burn in the place where the Orbits had cut the tears from his face when they had cut the tears from his eyes.

He had cleaned the wall every night for thirty-one years. The wall was a small cut of space above the small cut of mirror behind the small cut of bar. The wall was the place where the lasers sat. The lasers sat above the wall in the way the lasers had been sitting above the wall since the night the Orbits had given Maurice the lasers as a gift and the gift had been a gift and the gift had been a debt and the debt had been the only debt the Orbits had ever offered the dirtboys.

The lasers were not powered tonight. The lasers were not powered any night the door was not closed. The lasers were not powered any night the Orbits did not pay the wall's bill.

He set the cloth to the case.

He was thinking about the man in the brown suit.

Development

It had been three years ago.

Maurice had been cleaning the wall. The Blue Silk had been, that night, almost empty — only two dirtgirls at the far end of the bar, drinking slowly, looking at each other in the mirror, looking away, looking back. The air-conditioning had been off because the Orbits had not paid the wall's bill for two months and Maurice had told the bartender — a young secondman who had come down through the well the year before — to keep the chrome cool, to keep the lasers unlit, to keep the wall quiet.

Then the door had opened.

The man had walked in. The man had walked in the way every Orbital man walks in, in the way Maurice had spent thirty-one years learning to read: at the angle of the wrist, at the angle of the ear, at the angle of the place where the gravity had been different. The man had a brown suit. The man had a small chrome hand on the small chrome of the small chrome of the small chrome of the Orbital. The man had not walked in to drink. The man had walked in to wait.

Maurice had known this, at the door, in the way every man in the Blue Silk knows it.

"She's not here tonight," Maurice had said, very quietly, from behind the bar.

The man had not answered.

"She's in Singapore," Maurice had said. "She has been in Singapore for three weeks."

The man had not answered.

"Sit down," Maurice had said. "Sit down. Or go."

The man had not answered. The man had walked to the end of the bar and sat down and waited.

Maurice had raised his finger to his own code.

Rising Action

Then Sarah had walked in.

Maurice had heard the door before he had seen her. He had heard the small soft sound the door made when the woman who walked in had been the woman who walked in to be waited for. He had heard the small soft sound of her boots on the dark wood of the floor. He had heard the small soft sound of the chrome of her earrings against the small soft sound of the chrome of her earrings.

He had not turned to look.

He had known, at the door, in the way every man in the Blue Silk knows it.

"Maurice," Sarah had said.

"Sarah."

"He's here," Sarah had said. "Cunningham told me to come."

"I know," Maurice had said.

"Did he say anything?"

"He said to sit down or to go."

"He will not go," Sarah had said.

"I know," Maurice had said.

Maurice had raised his finger to his own code.

He had spoken the code for his gun — the old code, the code he had not spoken since the morning of his last flight, the code he had been saving for a night like tonight. The code had been a name: Innes. The name of a cutter who had flown beside Maurice on the morning of the morning, when the city of the Orbital had been a small white star at the small white edge of the small grey chrome of the small grey sky of Vandenberg.

The lasers had not fired. They had been waiting above the mirror. The lasers had been waiting the way the man had been waiting the way Sarah had been waiting. They had been waiting for the word.

The word was Maurice's.

Maurice had not given the word.

Climax

"Tell me what you want," Maurice had said.

The man in the brown suit had finally spoken. His voice had been quiet, calm, almost computer-flat, but with a weight at the end of each sentence that the air-conditioning did not move.

"My Princess is afraid of the dirtgirls of the Florida Free Zone."

Maurice had not spoken.

"He asks me to find her a weapon," the man had said. "A shaped charge. A dirtgirl who knows how to be a dirtgirl, and how to be a weapon, and how to be both at the same time."

Maurice had spoken. "You will not find it here."

The man had spoken. "I have found it here."

Maurice had spoken. "It will not be cheap."

The man had spoken. "I did not come for cheap."

Maurice had not spoken.

The man had risen from the bar. The man had walked to the door. The man had paused at the door and turned, very slowly, to look at Sarah, who had not moved from where she stood, who had not turned to look at the door.

"Her name is Sarah," the man had said.

Maurice had not spoken. The man had heard the words and gone.

The lasers had not fired. The wall had been silent. The mirror had been silent. The door had been silent. The Orbits had been silent.

Maurice had cleaned the wall.

Resolution

He set the cloth to the case. He set the cloth to the case the way he had been setting the cloth to the case every night for thirty-one years and the case had been a case and the case had been a wall and the wall had been the place where the lasers sat and the lasers sat above the wall in the way the man had been sitting above the wall.

The man had been waiting for Sarah. The man had been waiting the way every man in the brown suit had been waiting for every Sarah since the Rock War and the Rock War had been waiting for the Orbits and the Orbits had been waiting for the man in the brown suit.

He had not spoken the word.

The man had heard the words and gone.

Maurice cleaned the wall.

The wall was silent. The man was silent. Sarah was silent. The Orbits were silent.

The Orbits had been silent since the morning.

Maurice stood in the small soft quiet of the bar, and the cloth was in his hand, and the wall was clean, and the lasers were dark, and the small grey wall screen at the back of the bar scrolled, very slowly, a small clean line of data from the small grey console in the small back room of the Blue Silk:

WALL: NOMINAL. LASERS: OFF. ORBITS: SILENT.

Maurice was a man who had been a cutter once, who had been at the chair once, who had been at the mirror once. Maurice was a man who had heard the lasers in his mind for thirty-one years and had never spoken the word.

He set the cloth down on the case. He set the cloth down the way he had been setting the cloth down every night for thirty-one years. The cloth was, in the long quiet of the Blue Silk, the only thing that was, tonight, his.

The wall was clean.

The lasers were dark.

The man had gone.
