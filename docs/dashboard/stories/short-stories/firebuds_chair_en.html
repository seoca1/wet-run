Firebud's Chair

1607 wordsHardwired (Walter Jon Williams)

The chair was Firebud's design — a low white thing, hand-stitched leather at the head and the hands, the kind of small, ordinary throne that any decent body shop in the Florida Free Zone might keep, and that was exactly the point. You couldn't cut a weapon in a chair that looked like a weapon.

She wiped the paring knife with a square of chamois, then set it on the surgical tray beside the laser pen. Her studio smelled like surgical spirit and the faint brine of Tampa Bay at low tide, both of them caught in the air-conditioning and held against the white tile like a memory. The comp terminal in the corner was running its usual loop of low music — Trinidadian steel-pan instrumentals — and the ceiling fans moved the smell around the room without ever really clearing it. The console screen on the wall scrolled a small, patient line of data — heart rate, neural feedback, the soft rise-and-fall of Sarah's breathing — and that was the only data that mattered right now.

Outside, beyond the half-shuttered window, the sun was coming up the color of copper over the bay. Inside, Sarah was sitting in the chair.

Sarah had been sitting in the chair for three hours. She had not moved. She had not spoken. She had sat with her hands flat on the rests at her sides, palms down, like a schoolgirl told to keep still, and her eyes had been fixed on the same point on the wall since Firebud began — a knot in the wood paneling that looked, in the right light, like the crosshairs of an old gunsight. Firebud had hung the panel herself, fifteen years ago, for exactly this reason: give a dirtgirl a place to put her eyes that is not the knife.

The knife was in Firebud's right hand. The laser was in her left.

The scars on Sarah's face were the long ones. Old burn scars from a fire in the camps outside Houston, before the Orbital surrender, when Sarah's mother — Sarah still did not say the woman's name; she had not said it since the morning the Rock War ended — had lit the family tent in a stove fire trying to keep a baby warm and had taken the whole block of canvas up with her. Sarah had been four. The baby had been Sarah. The mother had not come back. The scars had.

Firebud leaned in. She tilted Sarah's face up with two violet-painted fingers. Sarah's lip curled, the way it always did, but she did not pull away. They had been through this before.

"Must we use this girl, Cunningham?"

Firebud asked it without turning her head. She knew where Cunningham was — he was always in the same place when a session began, the same corner of the studio with the same slouch against the same wooden post, his hands in the pockets of the same brown suit, his brown eyes on the same scar. He had been watching her work the way a man watches a chess computer running through its opening book. He had been watching Sarah work the way a man watches the chip in a console light up.

Cunningham's voice came back the way his voice always came back: quiet, calm, almost computer-flat, but with a weight at the end of each sentence that the air-conditioning did not move.

"Our Sarah has style, Firebud. Style and discipline. You are to give it form. Her style must be a weapon, a shaped charge. You will make it, I will point it. And Sarah will punch a hole right where we intend she should."

Cunningham said the words, and the comp terminal's music kept playing, and the fans kept moving the smell, and Sarah kept her eyes on the crosshairs in the wood. Then Cunningham asked, very softly, looking down at Sarah:

"Won't you, Sarah?"

Sarah did not reply. Instead, she looked up at Firebud, drawing back her lips, showing teeth.

"Let me hunt you some night, Firebud. I'll show you style."

Firebud rolled her eyes. "Dirtgirl stuff," she snorted, but she took a step back. Sarah grinned.

"And, Firebud," Cunningham said. The voice did not change pitch. The room did not change temperature. The comp terminal's music did not skip. "Leave the scars alone."

Firebud blinked. The paring knife hovered over Sarah's cheekbone. For one long moment, she was a bodybuilder she knew the answer to: remove the scars, smooth the face, give the Princess a blank canvas to stare at. That was the chrome of the trade, the wetwork any cutter-jock used to do.

"The scars?"

"Leave them."

"They will speak to our Princess. Of this cruel terrestrial reality that she helped create. That she dominates. With which she is already half in love."

Firebud lowered the knife. She looked at the scars. She looked at Cunningham's brown eyes. She looked at Sarah's grin. She thought, what a strange little empire this is, where a man's brown eyes can hold a knife at the face of a dirtgirl and the dirtgirl will be the one made safer for it. She thought, for the first time in nineteen years of cutting, that the trade had finally asked her a question her hands could not answer. She had been cutting Sarah's chin into an arrowhead. She had not been cutting the scar. The scar would have to cut itself, eventually, into the Princess's dream.

"Fine," she said. "The scars stay."

She set the paring knife down. She set the laser down. She had finished the chin — a sharper chin, an arrowhead, the shaped charge that Cunningham demanded — and the rest of Sarah's face was, for the moment, Firebud's to forget. Sarah's body was no longer a body. It was a thing being made. The trade had taken over.

But Cunningham had said leave the scars. So the scars stayed.

She looked at the chair.

The chair was her design. The chair had been her design for nineteen years. The chair had been her design the way her cart had been her cart in the clean room at the high edge of the Florida Free Zone, the morning the Orbits had hired her and the Orbits had given her the chair and the chair had been a chair and the chair had been a profession and the profession had been the only profession in the dirtgirls' trade. She had been a body designer since then. The trade had been her trade. The trade had taken the scars on the cut of the chair.

She had not cut the scars.

The scars were Sarah's scars. The scars were the scars of every dirtgirl the trade had not been able to cure — the scars of the camps outside Houston, the scars of the streets of Caracas, the scars of the favelas outside Singapore, the scars of the long cold walk out of Tampa after the Rock War. The trade had taken every cut it could. The trade had been the only honest employer in the Florida Free Zone.

She looked at the chair. She remembered the morning she had cut her own knuckles on the brass fittings, in her first week with the Orbits, and how the chrome had come off her hands for a week afterward, and how she had thought then that she was going to be a surgeon.

She looked at Sarah's eyes. They were fixed on the crosshairs in the wood paneling. They were fixed on the small, perfect target. They were fixed, and they did not blink, and the comp terminal's data showed the soft steady pulse of a heart that had been sitting in the chair for three hours and was not afraid.

She remembered what it had been to be afraid.

She remembered the first time Cunningham had walked into the studio with Sarah and Sarah had sat in the chair and Cunningham had stood in the corner with his hands in his pockets and his brown eyes on the scar. She had been cutting Sarah since then. She had not been cutting the scar. The scar was the cut of Cunningham's brown eyes, and the brown eyes had been the eyes of the Orbits, and the Orbits had been the eyes of the trade.

Sarah sat in the chair.

Sarah did not speak. Sarah did not move. Sarah sat with her eyes on the crosshairs in the wood, and the laser pen's thin red dot drew itself, faintly, across the chin that Firebud had shaped for her, and the comp terminal's steel-pan music went on playing Trinidad in the corner, and the fans went on moving the smell around the room without ever clearing it. Outside, over Tampa Bay, the sun was finishing its rise. The data on the console scrolled, soft and patient. The chair held Sarah the way the chair had always held Sarah: still, quiet, ready.

Firebud stood in the center of the studio.

The chair was at the center of her studio, the way her studio was at the center of her trade, the way her trade was at the center of her life. The chair was her design. The chair was nineteen years of her work. The chair held the shape that Sarah's body had been made into.

She picked the paring knife up. She picked the laser pen up. She looked at the scars. She thought about Cunningham. She thought about Sarah. She thought, very quietly, I have finished the chin.

She had finished the chin.
