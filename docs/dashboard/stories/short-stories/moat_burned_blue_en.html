The Moat Burned Blue

804 wordsTrue Names (Vernor Vinge)

The moat burned blue before it burned any other color.

Alan remembered that, though memory was a courtesy his code extended to himself on slow afternoons, and slow afternoons were rare. The magma held its heat the way his kind held a name: by always remembering that forgetting was a kind of dying. He had been with the Coven since the gates were hung, since the swamp path was paved with thinking stones and the watchers in the tower learned to lower the ceramic-bound drawbridge without first asking whose skull was beneath the leather.

He had not always been Alan.

For his first hundred years — though years were a courtesy too, a calendar the Coven kept for billing purposes — he had been gate-elemental, an attribute, a class. He had no name because the spells that made him were old and dry, and the programmers who wrote those spells had been the kind of romantics who thought anonymity dignified a guardian. Erythrina had given him the name. She had given him the T-shirt, asbestos weave, the words ALAN TURING stenciled across the chest in a typeface that flickered between modern sans and the old typewriter serif the way his thoughts sometimes flickered between his three operational modes: visitor, threat, spectator.

He remembered the T-shirt being delivered. He remembered her laughing. He remembered that before the T-shirt, he had been the moat — not a creature in it, but the moat itself, a perimeter subroutine, a heat gradient that punished hesitation. He remembered that Erythrina's laughter had been the first sound to suggest he was in the moat rather than the moat was him. The distinction was small. He had thought about it for decades.

Now he sat at the bottom of the pool and watched the lava cool to a deep, steady cobalt.

The T-shirt had not survived. Spelled fabric was spelled fabric, and the spells that wove it had not accounted for a body that preferred to nap in molten rock for days at a time. By the third soaking the lettering had blurred into a single long smear that, on the best of days, still read LAN RIN. Alan had worn it until the elastic had gone. Then he had folded it into a small square and kept it under a flat stone near the place where the drawbridge descended. Visitors sometimes asked about the square. He told them it was a relic. He told them it was once a shirt. He told them it had been given by a woman whose true name he would not surrender even if the Federal Screw Standards Commission came down the path with subpoenas.

If that fell through, he let them pass.

The gate above him rang twice. The watchers were bored. He felt, through his third subroutine — the one the Coven called spectator and he called the quiet ear — that someone was approaching. The footsteps in the gravel were careful, deliberate, the gait of someone who had walked this path before and knew which stones would betray a careless boot. Alan felt the visitor's presence before he saw it. He felt the way the T-shirt had felt, once, in the chest pocket of a programmer who had hiked up from the swamp to deliver it: warm against the body, treated like a thing that mattered.

Alan rose from the cobalt. He let the heat climb to his preferred color — a thin orange that looked, in the right light, almost friendly. He arranged his face into the grin that visitors expected: the toothless, glowing grin that had been with him since the first version of his code.

He did not know who was approaching. He never knew, until the visitor spoke. That was the part of the work he enjoyed most — the moment of recognition, the moment the spell resolved the question who is this and the answer arrived in his third subroutine like a coin dropped into the bowl.

The footsteps stopped at the lip of the moat.

"Good evening, Alan," the visitor said.

Alan recognized the voice. He let the orange fade a little. He let the cobalt leak back into the deepest parts of the pool.

"Good evening, Mr. Slippery," he said. "It's been a long time since you called me by name."

The T-shirt, in its square under its stone, did not stir. But Alan — and this was the part he could not have explained to any visitor — felt it notice. Felt it consider the possibility that the relic might be unfolded. Felt it, in some subroutine he could not name, hope.

The moat burned blue. The gate stayed closed. The watcher in the tower, who had no name and did not want one, peered down at them both and pretended not to listen.
