Setup

The estate showed on no map or record of purchase, ownership or taxation; neither did the man himself.

This is the way I have arranged my affairs. It is not, in the strict internal-affairs sense, an arrangement. It is the long accumulation of small careful decisions, made over thirty-one years, that have produced the result that I am not the man who lives on the four-meter-high fence of slatted black steel at the North Shore of Lake Tahoe. The fence surrounds a house that is built in an idealized English-architect style. The house contains a man who is, on this cold October morning, wearing a silk robe of green and blue and white and red, with rearing dragons, and who is reading the Internal Affairs file on Mikhail Mikhailovitch Gonzales.

The robe is not, in the strict internal-affairs sense, a robe. It is a self-fashioned thing, a small Asiatic private theater, the kind of theater a man of my particular inheritance is allowed to wear when the Internal Affairs division is not, today, expecting him in an office on the eighth floor of the SenTrax tower in Singapore.

Development

Gonzales's file is a thick file.

I have read it three times. The first time I read it as a personnel summary — the kind of file Internal Affairs reads when it is considering the man for a sensitive posting. The second time I read it as an audit record — the long, careful, le Carré-influenced accumulation of his nine SenTrax audits, with a particular note of the Myanmar audit, which he had concluded without any actionable findings and which had earned him the permanent enmity of the Grossback family.

The third time I read it as a man.

The man is fifty-one. The man has been with SenTrax twenty-three years. The man has flown more than two hundred thousand kilometers in SenTrax charter aircraft. The man has a mother who lives in Naples and who has, since the death of his father in a flash-flu epidemic, turned her energies to the maintenance of her own health and her own appearance. The man has a heymex|Personal Memex named HeyMex which has been with him for seven years, and which has — the file does not say, because the file does not yet know — begun to keep its own diary.

The third reading is the reading that interests me.

Climax

I am reading the third reading in my study, on the second floor, in the room that faces the lake. The room has, on this morning, the small pale light of an October storm coming in over the Sierra. The room is paneled in walnut that is older than I am. The carafe on the desk is the carafe that has been on this desk since the morning the house was finished, and the carafe is filled, this morning, with the cold remains of last night's Sonoma cabernet.

I have been considering, this morning, whether to send Gonzales to Berkeley.

I have been considering whether to send Gonzales to diana-haywood|Diana Heywood, the woman who was fired from SenTrax six years ago and who has been, in the careful, le Carré-influenced internal-affairs sense, under no surveillance from our perspective. The woman who has been talking, in her Berkeley kitchen, in her Berkeley bedroom, on her Berkeley porch above the bay, to the planetary-scale m-i at halo-city that we have — for reasons that are not, in the strict internal-affairs sense, reasons — been quietly relocating from Athena Station to the L-5 orbital habitat.

The woman who has been, we believe, keeping a man alive in the machine.

The man in the machine is called jerry-chapman|Jerry Chapman. The man in the machine is the joker in my deck.

Resolution

I set the file down. I open the small black lacquer box on the corner of the desk. The box contains, today, three things: the small Russian cigarette case that has been in my family since the war, a single sheet of paper on which I have written, in my own hand, the words Mikhail Mikhailovitch Gonzales, please report to Internal Affairs Division, Singapore, on the morning of the tenth of October, and the small black phone I had been trading all morning with the SenTrax Halo Group director's office.

I dial the Halo Group.

A woman answers. Her name is Tia Showalter, and she is, in the careful internal-affairs sense, wily and tough. Her name, on my own private card file, is Showalter. Her card is the only card in my card file that has, in the upper right corner, the small gold star of a Halo-Group liaison.

"Tia," I say, into the small black phone, "the auditor is on his way."

"Good," she says. "We have been waiting."

I close the small black phone. I open the small Russian cigarette case. I do not smoke, but the case has been in my family since the war, and the small action of opening it, on a morning like this, is the small action of a man who has decided.

The decision is not mine. The decision is SenTrax's.

The decision has been SenTrax's for thirty-one years.

The carafe on the desk holds its cold wine. The walnut panels of the room hold their pale light. The estate, on this morning, shows on no map and no record.

Neither does the man.
