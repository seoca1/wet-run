The Deathspider's News

1342 wordsTrue Names (Vernor Vinge)

Setup

The deathspider had been hanging in the web for six days.

The web was the deathspider's web. The web had been woven across the narrow path between the swamp and the gate, between the lily-pads and the place where the trail began to climb toward the moat. The web was not the deathspider's own work. The web had been woven, originally, by the Coven's first gate-programmer, who had been dead for decades and whose name the deathspider did not know. The web was part of the gate's architecture. The deathspider was part of the web.

The web smelled, in the morning, of the small metallic smell that web smells when web has been wet by the swamp overnight and has decided, in the polite subroutine of web, to dry out by the time the visitors begin to climb.

The deathspider was red-banded, the size of a man's fist, and very old. The deathspider had outlived three Coven chairpersons and two gate-elementals. The deathspider had outlived the original swamp-path paving. The deathspider had not outlived its purpose, which was to greet each visitor who climbed the path from the swamp, to ask the visitor a question, and — depending on the answer — to deliver one of seven possible messages.

The messages were, in the order the deathspider had learned them:

1. Beware, beware.

2. Turn back.

3. Stand still.

4. Lower your hand.

5. Run.

6. Raise the back of your hand so that I may crawl onto it.

7. No message.

The seventh was the hardest.

The deathspider had been asked, six days ago, to deliver the seventh. The deathspider had been asked by Erythrina, who was the only visitor the deathspider had ever seen climb the path carrying a piece of paper instead of a spell. The paper had been folded. The paper had been folded so that the deathspider could see, without unfolding it, the letter M.

Erythrina had said: Carry this for six days. After six days, if no one has come up the path who can read it, deliver it to the moat.

The deathspider had carried the paper for six days. The deathspider had hung in the web, holding the paper with two of its eight legs, and had watched the path. The deathspider had watched the path with the patience of an animal that had outlived three chairpersons. The deathspider had watched the path without moving, except to swing slightly when the air in the swamp shifted.

No one had come up the path.

This was unusual. The deathspider had seen, in its long life, three quiet spells on the Coven path — periods when no visitor climbed the path for weeks at a time. The deathspider had watched, through those spells, the way the lilies closed at night and opened in the morning, the way the moisture in the air grew heavier and then lighter, the way the watcher's silhouette in the tower above the gate would sometimes lean, very slightly, to one side, as if the watcher were tired. The deathspider had seen those spells, and the deathspider had always known, deep in whatever subroutine the Coven had given it, that the spells would end. The spells had always ended with a visitor, and the visitor had always been one of the seven who could read the paper, and the deathspider had always delivered the paper, and the paper had always been — the deathspider had never been told what the paper had been. The deathspider had only been told to deliver it.

Complication

Six days. No visitor.

The seventh day arrived. The deathspider, holding the paper, lowered itself slowly down the web's centerline. The deathspider did not swing. The deathspider did not hum. The deathspider made its way across the web to the leaf that Erythrina had placed, six days ago, at the web's eastern anchor.

The leaf was a curled leaf, the kind of leaf that, in the Coven's first gate-programmer's spell, was a delivery leaf. The deathspider climbed onto the leaf. The leaf began to fall, slowly, the way leaves in the Other Plane fall — not according to gravity but according to the subroutine that the Coven's first gate-programmer had written for the falling of delivery leaves.

The deathspider fell, with the leaf, into the air above the swamp. The deathspider felt the air grow warmer as it fell. The deathspider felt, very faintly, the heat of the moat beginning to climb.

The deathspider landed on the flat stone near the drawbridge.

The deathspider climbed off the leaf. The deathspider approached the T-shirt's stone. The deathspider sat, with the paper, in the cold corner between the stone and the moat's edge, and waited.

It did not wait long.

A clawed foot emerged from the lava. A pair of glowing eyes set in a coal-black head peered nearsightedly. The elemental — Alan, the name Erythrina had given him; the deathspider had heard the name spoken once, in passing, by a visitor who had passed the test and climbed the path and laughed on the other side of the gate — the elemental, Alan, leaned down.

Climax

"What is this," Alan said.

The deathspider held the paper up.

Alan took the paper. Alan unfolded it. Alan read it.

Alan, very slowly, folded the paper again. Alan, very slowly, set the paper on the flat stone beside the T-shirt. Alan, very slowly, climbed back down into the moat.

The moat burned blue. The fire did not come up.

The deathspider climbed back up the path. The deathspider climbed back into the web. The deathspider resumed its position in the center of the web, the position it had held for decades, the position it would hold for as long as the Coven needed it.

The paper was no longer the deathspider's responsibility.

The deathspider did not know what the paper had said.

The deathspider had, in the long subroutine that the Coven had given it to know things by, decided, in the long minutes of the climb back up the path, that the paper had not been for the moat. The paper had not been for the gate. The paper had not been for the watchers in the tower. The paper had been, the deathspider had finally decided, for a person who was not yet on the path. The paper had been, the deathspider had finally decided, for a person who would, in the long future of the Coven, climb the path, and who would, in the long future of the Coven, recognize the paper for what the paper was, and who would, in the long future of the Coven, deliver the paper to the person the paper had been meant for.

The deathspider had finally understood, in the long subroutine of its small mind, that the paper had been a piece of the future.

Resolution

The deathspider did not know what the paper had said.

The deathspider did not need to know.

The deathspider climbed back into the web. The deathspider resumed its position. The web smelled, faintly, of the small metallic smell of web that has been wet and has decided, in the polite subroutine of web, to dry.

The deathspider watched the path.

The deathspider watched the path with the patience of an animal that had outlived three chairpersons, and that had outlived two gate-elementals, and that had outlived the original swamp-path paving, and that had, in the long subroutine of its small mind, finally understood what Erythrina had meant when Erythrina had said After six days, if no one has come up the path who can read it, deliver it to the moat.

Erythrina had meant: If no one has come up the path who can read it, deliver it to the future.

The deathspider had, in the long subroutine of its small mind, delivered the paper to the future.

The paper was no longer the deathspider's responsibility.

The deathspider did not need to know.

The paper had been delivered.
