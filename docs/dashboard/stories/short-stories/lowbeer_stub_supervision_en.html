The Supervisor's Window: Ainsley Lowbeer on the Eighteenth Stub

Origin: Lowbeer assesses a new stub incoming from the periphery

688 wordsPeripheral

The interface was not a screen. That was the first thing the inspector noted when she sat down at her desk that morning and powered it on. It was not a screen because the screen itself was a category that the long emergency had rendered obsolete. What she had, instead, was a window — a thing whose edges were not edges but were the inspector's trained attention to the edges of probable things — and through it, a stub timeline she had been assigned to supervise was assembling itself out of packet noise.

The eighteenth stub.

The first seventeen she had watched unfold with the dutiful detachment of someone whose profession required her to be detached. The eighteenth was different. The eighteenth had a periphery attached to it, and the periphery had a name, and the name had been entered into the dossier on Lowbeer's desk three weeks earlier — Flynne Fisher, Clanton, West Virginia, twenty-first century, single mother and brother on the periphery test program — and the inspector had read the name once and had not been detached about it since.

The window brightened. A street appeared. The street was not a street she recognized, but the inspector recognized the century from the make of the asphalt and the way the lane markings had been repainted over older markings. Twenty-first century. Late.

"He is online," the desk said.

The inspector did not say anything. She watched the periphery resolve into a figure standing on a corner in what the inspector understood, from satellite cross-referencing, was the high street of a town that had been doing its level best to die quietly for thirty years. The figure was a young woman. She was wearing polkadot sunglasses. She was holding a paper bag in one hand. The other hand was at her side, swinging slightly, in the way that human hands swing when the rest of the body has decided to stay.

She was waiting. She was waiting the way people wait when they have been told to wait and have decided that the person who told them to wait will eventually show.

"He is online and is waiting for the briefing," the desk said.

"I am aware," the inspector said.

"You have not opened the briefing dossier."

"I am aware."

"The operator has been online for nine minutes and is reading a magazine she purchased at the corner shop. The periphery has not yet been dispatched."

The inspector turned toward the window. The street was empty in the way that American small-town streets are empty at five in the afternoon on a Tuesday — emptied by the routing of jobs and the routing of culture and the routing of everything that the jackpot had spared, but had not spared in any particular abundance. The inspector had read about this. She had watched it on seventeen other stubs in various configurations. She had seen it be more empty than this, and she had seen it be less empty than this.

"He is on his way," the inspector said. "Mr. Netherton is on his way."

"The operator's pulse has not changed."

"Mr. Netherton is on his way, and the operator's pulse has not changed, and you are telling me things I know."

The desk did not respond. The desk did not need to. The inspector turned back to the window.

The figure on the street had put down the paper bag. She had taken off the polkadot sunglasses. She had folded them and put them in her shirt pocket. She was looking at something — at a person not yet in the inspector's window — and her face was the kind of face that had decided to be careful about what she was about to be given.

The inspector's hand moved to her teacup. The tea had gone cold. She had not noticed.

The inspector watched the rest of the scene assemble itself, a frame at a time, through a window that was not a screen, in a stub that was the eighteenth, in a periphery she had decided to be less detached about than her profession strictly required.
