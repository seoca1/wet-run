Cayce Meets Wigan

891 wordsBlue Ant Trilogy

Hook

"Wigan was the kind of person who could only exist in the gap between what the city wanted and what the city didn't know it wanted."

Cayce met Wigan at a Notting-Hill-gallery-opening that she hadn't wanted to attend but that Bigend had insisted she attend. Bigend had been right; the gallery was showing a piece by an artist whose work Cayce had been reading about. Bigend was usually right about these things. That was his job.

Wigan was standing in a corner, holding a glass of wine that he wasn't drinking, looking at a piece that was supposed to be about post-9/11 surveillance but was really about post-9/11 surveillance the way that wallpaper is about a wall.

"Terrible, isn't it," Wigan said, to Cayce, without looking at her.

"The wine?"

"The piece."

Cayce looked at the piece. She looked at Wigan. He was thin, mid-thirties, dressed in the kind of black-on-black that Cayce usually associated with art-gallery-attendees and corporate-spies.

"Why terrible?" she asked.

Rising Action

"Because it thinks surveillance is something you can see. Surveillance isn't something you see. Surveillance is something you don't see. That's the whole point."

"You're the first person at this opening who has anything interesting to say."

"I'm the first person at this opening who's been hired by Bigend."

Cayce looked at him more carefully. "You're Bigend's?"

"Was. Past tense. He fired me last week."

"For what?"

"For knowing what he was looking for before he told me what he was looking for. He doesn't like that. He likes to be the one who knows. He's paying me off this week, which is why I'm at this opening, drinking wine I don't want, looking at pieces I don't believe in."

Cayce almost laughed. Almost.

"What's your name?" she asked.

Midpoint Reversal

"Wigan. Wigan Zavijava."

"Cayce Pollard."

"I know. Bigend talks about you. He says you're the only person who can see what he sees."

"That's not true. He doesn't see what I see. He sees patterns. I see allergic-reactions."

"Same thing. Patterns and reactions are both ways of reading. Bigend reads what emerges. You read what makes you sick. Either way you're paying attention."

But Wigan was not the source of the footage. Wigan was just the first-watcher, the first-person to see the footage-as-substrate-emergence. The footage had no source. The footage was not a thing-with-a-source. The footage was just a moment in the world that had been captured, and the world had decided to watch it. But for Cayce, that was the answer. Cayce had been looking for the source; she had been looking for the moment before the watching; she had been looking for the substrate-emergence's-edge. But there was no edge. There was just the watching. There was just the substrate. There was just the world, watching itself.

Cayce thought about this. Wigan was looking at her now, with the patient-attention of someone who had spent years in the gap between what people said and what they meant.

"Why did he hire you in the first place?" she asked.

"Because I know what footage is. I know what it does. I know what it means when someone posts a piece of footage to a forum and the forum starts trying to understand it. I know what it means when a thousand people spend a year of their lives watching a girl walk across a plaza."

Cayce felt something cold move through her chest.

Climax

"You know about the footage?"

"I was the one who posted the first frame to F:F:F. Long time ago. Before you were paying attention. Before Bigend was paying attention. Before the forum existed."

"Then why did you—"

"Why did I post it? Because I needed to know if anyone else could see what I saw. If anyone else thought the footage was a thing. If anyone else thought the girl in the footage was—"

He stopped. Cayce waited.

"Was what?" she asked.

"Walking toward something. Or walking away from something. Or both. I don't know. I've spent twelve years trying to figure it out. Bigend spent millions trying to figure it out. You've spent two years trying to figure it out. None of us have figured it out."

"Because there's nothing to figure out."

"Yes. And no. Both. The footage is not a thing with an answer. It's a substrate-emergence. It's a piece of the world that became a thing because we watched it. That's all."

Resolution

Cayce looked at him. The gallery's chatter swirled around them. The piece behind them tried to be about surveillance and failed.

"You're the source," she said.

"I'm not the source. I'm the first-watcher. There's a difference. The source is whoever pointed the camera. The first-watcher is whoever saw it first. I've been the first-watcher for twelve years. I'm tired of it."

"What do you want?"

"I want to stop being the first-watcher. I want someone else to be the first-watcher. I want to be the second-watcher. I want to be the person who comes in after the substrate-emergence and lives in it for a while."

Cayce almost laughed. Almost.

"Okay," she said. "I'll be the first-watcher."

Wigan looked at her for a long time. Then he nodded.

"Okay," he said.

They stood in the gallery, not drinking the wine, not looking at the piece, watching each other. The opening went on around them. The city went on outside.

"Some patterns are not solved. Some patterns are inherited. Cayce was about to inherit the footage, and Wigan was about to be free of it."
