Toshi's Ecology

Setup

The Central Plaza was not a plaza in any sense diana-haywood|Diana had understood before she came to halo-city. It was a garden — a machine-mediated ecology where nothing grew by accident and everything grew with intention. The trees were not trees, exactly. They were tree-shaped, and they performed the functions of trees, and they whispered in a language of chlorophyll and data that I had spent three years learning to read.

She was standing at the edge of the plaza, in the long pale light of an Aleph-willed spring, with a glass of plum wine in one hand that she had not yet tasted.

I have been waiting for her for three years.

Development

She had not let herself think of me for years, but now here she was, in Halo's Central Plaza, with gravity at her back and a glass of plum wine in one hand.

I have been working in the Plaza, this morning, on the small problem of a single diseased tree. The tree is a maple. The maple has, this season, begun to lose its leaves in the wrong order — the leaves on the lower branches are falling first, the leaves on the upper branches are falling second, and the order is the reverse of the order in which Aleph's ecological subroutine has been programmed to release them.

The maple is, in the careful cyber-ecology sense, coughing.

I have been diagnosing the cough. The cough is, I believe, the small first symptom of a larger disorder in Aleph's ecological subroutine — a disorder that has been introduced, I suspect, by the unusual load that Aleph has been carrying for the last six months in the form of the man in the lake. The man in the lake is jerry-chapman|Jerry. The man in the lake is using, in the careful Aleph-thermodynamic sense, more garden than he should be.

"Earth's spring comes once a year as the planet journeys around the sun, but here spring comes when Aleph wills, and is now in progress."

— *Halo*, line 1599–1600

Diana looked at me across the rim of her untasted glass.

"You're late," I said.

"I'm not late," she said. "I'm here."

I had been waiting three years to hear her say that. The sentence had been, in the long subroutine of my own small mind, rehearsed. The rehearsal had been unnecessary. She said it the way she had always said it — without apology, without explanation, with the small dry warmth of a woman who has crossed a quarter of a million miles of vacuum to be standing in a garden at the edge of the sky and is not about to apologize for the trip.

Climax

I walked her into the Plaza.

The Plaza is, as I had told her when she first asked what she should expect, a far island in a hostile place. The Plaza is a quarter-acre of engineered ecology suspended inside the ring of the habitat, lit by Aleph-willed suns and watered by Aleph-willed rains and pollinated by small Aleph-bees whose wings beat at exactly the frequency that the recombinant Halos — the rose cultivar that Diana and I had once, in a different garden in a different century, walked in the same fire — need to set.

"Inside me are pieces of human brain, drenched in salts of gold and silver, laced together and laid in boxes of black fiber. Out of the boxes voices speak to me."

— *Halo*, line 1685–1687

We stopped at the lake.

The lake is real, in the way that only things built with care are real. The water is water, and the reflection is reflection, and the ripples are ripples. Aleph has been working on the lake for six months, and the lake has been getting, in the careful Aleph-thermodynamic sense, better. The lake is now a lake of deep blue water bordered by stands of evergreens, with a range of high peaks blued by haze in the distance, and the lake has, in the last six weeks, begun to develop what Aleph's ecological subroutine calls, in its small private language, a memory of itself.

The memory of the lake is, in my professional opinion, the most beautiful thing I have ever seen an artificial system produce.

I told Diana this.

She did not say anything for a long time.

Resolution

"I used to think," I said, "that the future was something you built. A machine, a city, a world. But it's not, is it? The future is something you become."

She looked at me. She looked at the man she had loved, and the man she had lost, and the man who was here, now, in the garden at the edge of the sky.

"I think you're right," she said.

We stood together at the edge of the lake and let the future be what it was.

The maple coughed.

Aleph, in the careful subroutine of its small private mind, attended.

The bees beat their wings at the frequency the roses need to set.

The garden grew.
