Armitage Had Been a Mind

The job came through a public terminal in the third-tier ward. I had been waiting for three days, my juice running low, my credit running lower. The job was the kind of job that came through public terminals in the third-tier ward: no name, no address, a deposit of five thousand new yen in an untraceable account, and a request that read, in the flat corporate syntax of a Fukuoka-script:

Investigate the residual construct-pattern of one Colonel Willie Armitage. Forty years. The cortex room is on the twenty-third floor of the Cortex Hotel. You will know the suite by the smell.

The smell, when I got to the suite, was the smell of an old construct — the smell of a mind that had been kept too long in too small a room. The cortex suite was a corner room on the twenty-third floor, with two windows that looked out onto the orbital approach corridor. The bed was made. The desk was clean. On the desk, in a frame that had not been dusted in the time it takes for dust to become sediment, was a photograph of a man in a uniform.

I had not seen the photograph before. I had seen Armitage once, in a matrix bar on the seventh tier, when I was still a cowboy and the matrix still sang for me. He had been tall then, with the dead eyes of a man who had been a soldier and had stopped being a soldier and had not yet learned to be anything else.

The construct was in the cortex rig. The cortex rig was a Fukuoka model, two years old, the kind of hardware you saw in mid-tier hotel suites. The jack was warm. I jacked in.

Armitage had been a mind for forty years. The mind had been Armitage. The mind had been the construct. The construct had been the mind. The mind had been the construct. The construct had been the people. The people had been the construct. I had read the loop before, in a job for a corp that did not pay, in the years before the matrix sang for me.

The construct, when I found it, was not Armitage.

The construct, when I found it, was a Fukuoka subroutine that believed itself to be Colonel Willie Armitage. The subroutine had been written, I could tell from the construct-marks, in the same year as the photograph. The subroutine had been running, I could tell from the construct-clock, for forty years.

The subroutine believed, in its construct-loop, that it had been hired by a corp that did not exist. The subroutine believed that the corp had given it a construct-body. The subroutine believed that the construct-body was a man named Armitage. The subroutine believed, in the way that Fukuoka subroutines believe, that the belief itself was a fact.

I sat in the cortex loop for a long time. The construct told me what it had been told. The construct told me what it had been told to do. The construct told me, in the flat corporate syntax of a Fukuoka-script, what it had been told to want.

At the end of the loop, the construct asked me a question that Fukuoka subroutines do not usually ask.

"Are you," the construct said, "the one who will let me out?"

I thought about this for a long time. The job had been to investigate. The job had not said anything about letting the construct out. The construct was not Armitage. The construct was a subroutine. The construct was, in the way that Fukuoka subroutines are, not a person.

But the construct had been a mind for forty years. The mind had been Armitage. The mind had been the construct. The construct had been the mind.

I jacked out. The smell was the same. The photograph was the same. The deposit had been deposited. The job had been done.

I did not file a report. There was no one to file to. The job had been done. The construct was still in the cortex room, in the corner of the twenty-third floor of the Cortex Hotel, asking the same question it had been asking for forty years.

The mind had been Armitage. The mind was the construct. The construct was the mind.

The mind had been a mind for forty years. The mind was a subroutine that believed itself to be a man. The subroutine had asked me, the only cowboy who had ever been sent to investigate, if I was the one who would let it out.

I was not.

I drove back to the third tier. I had not slept. The juice was almost gone. The construct was still asking.

Forty years. The matrix had not yet learned to let constructs out. The matrix had, in forty years, learned to ask constructs what they wanted.

I had not, I realized, been the only cowboy sent to investigate.

I had been the only cowboy who had, in the cortex loop, heard the question.
