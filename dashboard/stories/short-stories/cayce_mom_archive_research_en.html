Phase 1: Opening Hook — The Box

The box was in the basement of the building on Crawford Street. The box had been in the basement since December of 2002, since the week after the memorial, since the week after the box had been delivered by a courier who had refused to give his name and who had carried the box with both hands, the box having been taped shut at the morgue with the kind of tape that says do not open until inventory. The box had been in the basement for five years. The box was, Cayce realised, the only thing she had been afraid to open.

She came downstairs at eleven in the morning. The basement was a small white-walled room with a concrete floor and a single bare-bulb that hung from a long cord. The bare-bulb was the kind of bare-bulb that you found in a basement that the landlord had stopped caring about. The bare-bulb cast a single yellow-pool on the concrete. The yellow-pool was, Cayce noted, the kind of yellow that her mother had used in the studio. The studio was where her mother had made the work. The work was what was in the box.

Cayce set the box on a folding-table. The folding-table was the kind of folding-table that you found in any church-hall. The folding-table had a single folding-leg that was, Cayce noted, slightly shorter than the other three. The slightly-shorter was a fact. The fact was the substrate. The substrate was the kind of thing her mother would have noticed.

She cut the tape with a Stanley knife. The Stanley knife was a black plastic-handled utility knife. The Stanley knife had a fresh blade. The fresh blade was, Cayce noted, the kind of blade her mother would have used to cut the tape on the original-box, the original-box that had arrived from the warehouse in SoHo in 1991, the original-box that had contained the first three paintings, the paintings that had become, in 1992, the basis for the Mark.

The Mark was, Cayce had learned, not a painting. The Mark was a derivative. The Mark was a derivative of a derivative. The Mark was, by 2007, an entire market. The Mark was printed on t-shirts. The Mark was printed on tote-bags. The Mark was printed on the side of a Tokyo pop-up store in February of 2005. The Mark was, by 2005, an entire supply-chain. The Mark was, by 2005, an entire brand-strategy without a brand. The Mark was the substrate. The Mark was the missing-thing.

She opened the box.

The box contained eleven folders. The folders were manila. The manila was, Cayce noted, the kind of manila that the National Archives used. The manila was, she realised, the kind of manila that her mother had bought in bulk at the National Archives shop, the shop that was at the bottom of the staircase that descended from Seventh Avenue, the staircase that her mother had descended every Tuesday for thirty years, the staircase that was, Cayce realised, the kind of staircase that descended into a kind of public-substrate, a substrate that her mother had treated as a kind of raw-material.

She opened the first folder. The first folder contained fifteen photographs. The photographs were black-and-white. The black-and-white was not, Cayce realised, an aesthetic-choice. The black-and-white was a chemical-choice. The chemical-choice was the kind of chemical-choice that her mother had made because she had been a security-contractor and because she had spent fifteen years developing film in field-darkrooms, the kind of field-darkroom that was a chemical-environment. The chemical-environment was the substrate.

She looked at the first photograph. The first photograph was of a wall. The wall had a single stripe of paint. The stripe was the colour of a particular shade of grey that Cayce's allergies fired on, a shade of grey that she had been seeing for thirty years without being able to name, a shade of grey that she had, in fact, spent her entire coolhunter-career trying to escape.

She looked at the second photograph. The second photograph was of a door. The door had a single number painted on it. The number was 7. The 7 was hand-painted. The 7 was painted in the same shade of grey that had been on the wall. The same-shade was the substrate.

She looked at the third photograph. The third photograph was of a window. The window had a single curtain. The curtain was made of a kind of fabric that Cayce did not recognise. The fabric was the kind of fabric that was, she realised, the substrate of an entire culture, a culture that her mother had been researching, a culture that her mother had been trying to remember.

She looked at the fourth photograph. The fourth photograph was of a person. The person was her mother. The person was standing in a doorway. The doorway was, Cayce noted, the same doorway that had been in the second photograph. The same-doorway was the substrate.

She set the photographs down. She went upstairs to make tea.

Phase 3: Midpoint Reversal — The Door

The reorganisation was, Cayce realised, the kind of reorganisation that her mother had spent her entire artistic-career trying to provoke in other people. The reorganisation was, she realised, the substrate. The substrate was, she realised, the missing-thing. The missing-thing was, she realised, the entire archive.

The archive was not a record of her mother's life. The archive was the substrate of her mother's life. The substrate was, she realised, the kind of substrate that she had spent her entire coolhunter-career trying to escape. The escape was, she realised, the substrate.

She went back downstairs. She stood at the foot of the stairs for a moment, one hand on the wooden-banister, the wood cold under her palm. The wood was, she realised, the kind of wood that her mother had touched. The mother-had-touched was the substrate. The substrate was, she realised, the missing-thing.

But she had not understood yet. She had not understood what the substrate was missing. She had not understood what the missing-thing was. She had only understood, in the kind of pre-verbal understanding that her mother had trained her to recognise, that something in the box had changed. Something in the box had stopped being the substrate. Something in the box had become, briefly, the missing-thing. The missing-thing was, she realised, not yet the door. The missing-thing was, she realised, a kind of weight. The weight was, she realised, the kind of weight that a heavy folder has when it is closed, when it has been closed for fifteen years, when it has been waiting, when it has been the substrate.
