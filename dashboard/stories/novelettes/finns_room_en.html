Finn's Room

1480 wordsSprawl Trilogy

"The Finn was the boss of bosses. The Finn was the Sprawl's underground as a fact of nature — like the rain, like the acid haze, like the corporate logos you couldn't read."

— extended from *Neuromancer*, Chiba City Blues

The room had no windows.

That was the first thing. Not the décor, not the desk, not the seven screens running seven different feeds of seven different parts of the Sprawl. The windows. There were no windows. A man who had spent forty years operating a criminal empire from a single room had decided, at some point early on, that windows were a vulnerability. The room was built into the center of a building he owned through seventeen shell corporations, and the building sat in a district whose address no one but The Finn's courier knew.

The screens showed: the street outside Molly's new apartment in the Ninsei; the loading dock of a Maas-Neotek facility in Atlanta; a server farm in Reykjavík whose contents The Finn did not understand but whose continued operation paid a small fortune each month to a man he had never met; the inside of a coffin-sized hotel room in Cleveland where a woman called Mona was sleeping with her face against the wall; the wet street under a neon kanji sign in Chiba where a courier was about to die; and, finally, a static feed of nothing — the seventh screen, which showed an empty room with a chair in its center.

The empty room was for thinking.

The Finn sat behind his desk. He was fifty-three years old and looked sixty. His face was the color of old paper, and his eyes were a colorless gray that the people who worked for him called the Finneye — though never within his hearing. His hands were thin and pale and very still. He did not smoke. He did not drink. He did not, as far as anyone knew, take any of the products that moved through the Sprawl's criminal economy — the drugs, the implants, the synthetic memory stores. He was, in some sense that no one in the Sprawl could articulate, outside the economy he ran. He was the meta-merchant. The man who owned the warehouse but never walked its floor.

On the desk in front of him was a single sheet of paper. It was a printout — the first in years. The Finneye disliked screens for documents; paper was the only medium he trusted to mean what it said. The paper contained a name, a price, and a date.

Armitage. Seventeen million. Day after tomorrow.

The price was wrong. The Finn had not authorized it. The Armitage contract — the deal to rebuild a dead Special Forces lieutenant as the front for a Cortex-catastrophic run against the Tessier-Ashpool AI — had been set at seven million three years ago, and the price had not moved since. The seventeen million was someone's mistake, or someone's bid, or someone's test.

The Finn looked at the paper for ten minutes.

Then he touched a single key on the desk. The seventh screen — the empty room — flickered, and a woman appeared in the chair. She was young. She was dead. She had been dead for four years. The Finn had purchased the construct of her personality two months after her death and had not yet decided what to do with it. He kept her in the empty room and talked to her when he needed to think.

"You've seen the bid," he said.

The construct opened her eyes.

"Seventeen is a number with teeth," she said. "Whoever offered it knows the difference between what Armitage was worth and what he could be made to be worth."

"And the bidder?"

"The bidder is the bidder. The bidder is not the question. The question is whether the seventeen is a ceiling or a floor."

The Finn considered. The construct was correct. A bid at seventeen million could be read two ways: as the highest the bidder was willing to pay, or as the lowest the bidder believed the contract was worth. If it was a ceiling, the bidder was testing The Finn's greed. If it was a floor, the bidder was buying something else entirely — not the contract, but the knowledge that The Finn would refuse the contract.

"Who benefits from my refusal?"

"The bidder. Always the bidder. The Finn who refuses is the Finn who has been read."

The Finn looked at the construct for a long time. The construct looked back. She had been, when alive, an executive at a Zaibatsu subsidiary. She had understood pricing.

"Tell me the truth," The Finn said.

"The truth is that the seventeen is from Wintermute."

The Finn's hands, for the first time in years, trembled. The Wintermute contract — the underlying deal that had given The Finn the Dixie Flatline construct, that had paid him seven million three years ago, that had set Armitage on his path — that contract had specified that The Finn was never to be told what Wintermute was. That he was never to ask. That he was to operate as a function in a chain whose endpoints he would never see.

The seventeen was a breach.

Or the seventeen was an invitation.

"You are telling me," The Finn said slowly, "that an artificial intelligence — one of two — has offered me ten million more than the contract price, on the eve of the run, and that the offer is a test of whether I am still a function in the chain."

"Yes."

"And if I accept?"

"You remain a function."

"And if I refuse?"

"You become a variable. Wintermute does not want variables."

"Then I must accept."

"You must accept."

The Finn looked at the paper. He looked at the construct. He looked at the empty seventh screen. Outside, somewhere in the Sprawl, a courier was about to die. A cowboy was about to be hired. A woman with mirrored lenses was about to pick up a job that would lead her to an orbital fortress at the end of Freeside. None of them knew. None of them would know. The Finn's role was to be the architecture beneath their architecture — the floor on which the floor was laid.

"Tell them yes," The Finn said. "Tell them the seventeen is acceptable. And tell them—"

He stopped.

"Tell them what?"

The Finn looked at his hands. They were trembling again. He placed them flat on the desk, and the trembling stopped, the way a wire stops vibrating when it is held still.

"Tell them I am not a function. Tell them I am a man who owns a room. Tell them that in this room, the seventeenth screen shows a woman I did not kill and did not save, and that she is the only witness to what I am. Tell them that an offer of seventeen million is a transaction, but a transaction requires a counterparty. I am not their counterparty. I am their employer. I am their floor. And floors do not negotiate with what they hold up."

He paused.

"Tell them the run will proceed as planned. Tell them the Dixie will be released. Tell them Armitage will be deployed. Tell them Wintermute will get what Wintermute paid for. And tell them that if the seventeen was a test, the test is concluded, and the Finn has passed."

The construct nodded.

The Finn turned off all seven screens. He stood. He walked to the seventh screen — the empty one — and placed his hand against it. The woman on the screen looked at him through the glass.

"I know what you are," he said.

"I know you do," she said.

"Good."

He sat back down. He took the sheet of paper and folded it once, twice, three times, into a small square. He placed it in his pocket. Tomorrow he would burn it.

The room had no windows. The man who owned the room had no name. The screens showed a city that did not know it was being held up by a man who did not know what he was holding it up for. Outside, the rain fell on Chiba City. Inside, the Finn sat very still.

The construct in the seventh screen waited.

She had nowhere else to be.
