Bigend Meets Laney

1082 wordsBridge Trilogy

Opening Hook

I had been in Tokyo for ninety days before Bigend found me. Ninety days of falling asleep to the hum of vending machines and waking to the hum of vending machines — the same hum, the same machine, the same vending. In Tokyo, vending is a kind of poetry. In Bay Area it is just vending. I was in Tokyo because Tokyo is where patterns terminate.

I was eating ramen in a Roppongi place whose name I could not pronounce. The broth was a kind of data — alkaline, marine, the ghost of something I had eaten in a previous life. Outside, the district's signage blinked in patterns I could read the way some men read women: superficially, by intent. Below the signage, in the patterns of the sidewalk's foot traffic, I read what I had always read: the shape of the next surprise.

The next surprise walked in.

He was six foot six. He had Northern European vowels. He ordered nothing.

"Colin Laney," he said. "Hubertus Bigend. Blue Ant. I would like to talk to you about a contract."

I knew who he was. I had known who he was since Cayce Pollard had explained, in a SoHo office three thousand miles away, that Blue Ant is a firm that captures the drift of culture. Cayce had said it without irony. Cayce is allergic to branding. Cayce is, in that sense, an instrument of branding — a measuring device for what others cannot see.

I am not allergic to anything. I am sensitive to patterns. This is a different disorder.

Rising Action

"The idoru," Bigend said. "Rei Toei. You know of her."

I knew of her. Rei Toei: the world's first virtual pop idol. A construct, generated by the Loa Oracles' residual architecture and given a body through Rez's industry connections. She had married a rock guitarist in a Tokyo ceremony I had declined to attend. I had declined because my patterns told me the marriage was not symbolic — it was operational. The marriage was the data-flow.

"Rei Toei is the precedent," Bigend said. "A precedent is not a single event. It is the pattern of events that will follow. Rei Toei is the first of a category. The category will produce many more like her. The question Blue Ant asks is not who Rei Toei is, but what she predicts."

I did not like the question. The question was the kind my previous employers had asked, in the days when I worked for the Rez-Lo consortium — a consortium whose payment to me had been deletions, threats, and one near-death in a Seattle parking structure. I had stopped working for them because the patterns told me to stop. I had stopped working for everyone because the patterns told me to stop.

Bigend saw me not liking the question.

"Blue Ant does not pay in deletions," he said. "Blue Ant pays in yen. Blue Ant pays in data. Blue Ant pays in continuation."

"Continuation?"

"You will continue to be Colin Laney. You will continue to be sensitive to patterns. You will continue to be paid for the sensitivity. You will continue, in Tokyo, in Roppongi, in noodle shops whose names you cannot pronounce."

I considered this. The offer was the first in years that did not require my death.

Climax

"What do you want me to profile?" I asked.

"The data-flow that produced Rei Toei," Bigend said. "The architecture of a precedent. Where the precedent will lead. Who will follow. What will follow them."

This was, I realized, the same question the Loa Oracles had answered when they had produced Rei Toei. The Loa had answered the question prophetically — they had produced the precedent because they had seen the category. Bigend wanted the question answered analytically — he wanted a human analyst to confirm what the Loa had already known.

"I cannot profile what the Loa have already produced," I said.

"You can profile what the Loa will produce next," Bigend said. "Rei Toei is the first. The next will be a Cayce Pollard — a coolhunter so sensitive to branding that her body rejects logos. The next-next will be something we do not yet have a name for. A pattern so far below the brand-line that no instrument can read it."

"Cayce is not a construct," I said.

"No," Bigend said. "Cayce is not a construct. Cayce is the next category of analyst. Coolhunter. Brand-sensor. A new human role, produced by the merger of brand and immunity. Blue Ant wants to find her earlier. Blue Ant wants to find her before she finds herself."

I understood. The contract was not to profile Rei Toei. The contract was to find the next Rei Toei. The contract was to find the pattern that produced patterns.

I took the contract.

Resolution

Bigend paid me in yen. The yen was clean. The contract was written. The data-flow began.

I have been working for Blue Ant for ninety-seven days. The patterns I read are no longer only Tokyo's. They are London, Moscow, New York, the Sprawl's residue, the Bridge's new infrastructure, the post-2001 moment's brand-immunity. Blue Ant has taught me to read patterns that I did not previously know I was reading.

I am still allergic to nothing.

Epilogue

Bigend finds me again, last week, in another noodle shop. He is eating for the first time in my presence. He orders the same ramen I am eating. We eat in silence.

After thirty minutes, he says: "What pattern are you reading now?"

"The pattern of this meal," I say.

"Good," he says. "Continue."
