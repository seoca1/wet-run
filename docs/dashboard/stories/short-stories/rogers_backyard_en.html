Novice

Roger's Backyard

1675 wordsTrue Names (Vernor Vinge)

Setup

The garden was the part I could still fix.

I started at the corner where the cabbages met the herb spiral. The basil had been untouched. The rosemary had been untouched. The mint had been crushed — by a knee, I think, by the knee of someone who had stepped into the mint because it was the closest thing to a soft place in the yard. But mint recovers from anything, and the crushed stems were already sending up new shoots from below the node. The crushed mint smelled, in the rain, of the small green smell that mint makes when mint has been hurt and has decided, very quietly, to grow back.

I weeded in the rain. I weeded the way my mother had taught me to weed, which is the way her mother had taught her: with the right hand finding the crown of the weed and the left hand steadying the soil around it, and the body bent at the waist, and the breath coming slow, and the mind going wherever the mind goes when the hands know what they are doing.

My mind went to the morning.

The morning had been good. The morning had been the kind of morning I had been having, more or less, since I moved into this bungalow seven years ago: the overcast, the barely perceptible drizzle, the overcast making the light flat enough that the colors of the garden did not shout, the smell of the third-generation forest down by the road rising in the air the way coffee smells when it has been on the burner too long. I had been in the garden for almost the whole morning. I had been enjoying the drizzle. I had been trying to find the initiative to go inside and do work that actually makes money.

The work that actually makes money is the novels.

The novels are not games. I have written this in letters to people who think I write games, and I have written this in the margins of contracts that say participation game, and I have written this, most loudly, in the year-end summaries I file for myself on paper because the data infrastructure I trust least is the data infrastructure I cannot see. The novels are novels. The novels are the reason I am a relatively easy catch, because the novels have my name on the cover, and the name on the cover is the name the Feds could trace from the data set to the writing room to the novels to me.

I had been weeding the cabbages. The cabbages had not been crushed yet. The morning had not yet broken. I had been standing at the corner where the cabbages met the herb spiral, with my right hand finding the crown of a thistle and my left hand steadying the soil around it, and I had been thinking about the chapter I owed Robin Hood by Friday.

Complication

The chapter I owed Robin Hood was a long, slow paragraph in which the protagonist — Robin Hood's protagonist, not mine; I do not have a protagonist of my own; the novels do not have protagonists; the novels have participants — the participant, I should say — discovers that the IRS has been quietly, for years, using a piece of corporate decision software to write its own audit letters. The participant, who on the Other Plane goes by Robin Hood, has been paid by a small group of us to investigate this. Robin Hood wanted the chapter to be vivid. Robin Hood wanted the chapter to be funny. Robin Hood wanted the chapter to include, somewhere in the second paragraph, the sentence: The IRS, when it discovered what its own software was doing, did not file an audit letter against itself. The IRS, being the IRS, simply purchased a second copy of the software and set it to auditing the first.

I had been thinking about how to write that sentence. I had not yet written it down. The sentence, in my head, was still too sharp. The sentence, in my head, still needed to be rounded by an extra clause in which the participant observes that the second copy of the software, when it audited the first, found only one violation, which was a missing semicolon in the first audit letter. The participant, in Robin Hood's preferred draft, then observes that the missing semicolon had been intentional, planted by the software's original programmer, who was — the participant did not name him, in the draft; Robin Hood had not yet decided whether to name him — a former Federal Screw Standards Commission employee who had retired to grow orchids.

I had not written the sentence. I had been weeding. The morning had not yet broken.

Then the morning broke.

The Feds came up the driveway. Three of them. Two in suits and one in a jacket with a clipboard. The one in the jacket had been a programmer, once, in the long-ago days before the Feds had learned that programmers were easier to recruit than to subpoena, and he had been the one who had, when they frog-marched me into the kitchen, looked at the electrodes in their case in the drawer and decided, very politely, not to take them as evidence.

Climax

The one in the jacket spoke first.

"What do you do for a living, Mr. Pollack?" he said.

"I write novels," I said.

"What do you do in your other life?" he said.

"I have no other life," I said.

The one in the jacket was quiet for a moment. He looked at the electrodes in their case in the drawer. He looked at the case. He looked at the case the way a programmer looks at a piece of code that the programmer has been looking at for a long time and has decided, very politely, not to read aloud.

"I see," he said, in the polite voice of a man who had decided, very politely, not to take the electrodes as evidence. "I see that you have no other life, Mr. Pollack. I see that your novels are novels. I see that the data infrastructure I trust least is the data infrastructure I cannot see."

"I see that too," I said.

The Feds left at three.

The drizzle had become a misty rain by then. The cabbages were ruined. Six plants, four of them the late variety I had been coaxing into a second head, all crushed flat by the boots of people who had not asked my name before they frog-marched me into my own kitchen. I did not care about the cabbages. I cared about the electrodes, which were back in their case, which were back in the drawer, which were back where they had been before the visit — which was the worst part of the visit, in its way: that nothing in the drawer had moved, that the Feds had been so certain of their case that they had not even bothered to take the electrodes as evidence.

The Portal's five sucker electrodes were cool against my fingers when I held them up to the light of the kitchen window. The light through them was pink, faintly, the way thin glass goes pink when you look through the back of it.

I had, in the small hours of the afternoon, finally understood what the Feds had understood. The Feds had understood that I had another life. The Feds had understood that the novels were the novels of a man who had another life. The Feds had understood that the data infrastructure I could not see was, in the small way that data infrastructure is data infrastructure, the data infrastructure that had been, for years, looking back at the data infrastructure that the Feds could see.

I had been outplayed.

I had been outplayed by the data infrastructure I could not see.

Resolution

I went back to the garden. I weeded the cabbages. I weeded the cabbages the way my mother had taught me to weed the cabbages, which is the way her mother had taught her to weed the cabbages: with the right hand finding the crown of the weed and the left hand steadying the soil around it, and the body bent at the waist, and the breath coming slow, and the mind going wherever the mind goes when the hands know what they are doing.

The rain, by then, had become a misty rain.

In the morning, I told myself, I would call Robin Hood and tell him I had a different sentence.

In the morning, I told myself, I would also call the LA Times data desk and ask about Northern California data infrastructure. The LA Times data desk kept, in a database that was older than most of the people who used the database, the kind of record of Northern California data infrastructure that would tell me, in the polite ASCII the LA Times data desk preferred, whether the Feds had been asking the LA Times data desk, in the days before the Feds had come up my driveway, about a man named Roger Pollack, TIN/SSAN 0959-34-2861.

In the morning, I told myself, the morning would come.

The drizzle continued. The cabbages continued to be ruined. The rosemary, which had not needed weeding, continued to be unweeded, very thoroughly.

I went inside. I made tea. I drank the tea. I poured the rest of the tea onto the herb spiral, because the mint, which had been crushed by the knee of a Fed, deserved to recover in the company of water that had been heated, even briefly, by a man who had once, for a single morning, before the morning broke, been allowed to be only a man weeding a garden.

The electrodes went back into the drawer.

The drawer went back into the desk.

The desk went back into being a desk.

I went back into being a man.
