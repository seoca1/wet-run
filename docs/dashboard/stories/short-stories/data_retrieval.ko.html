Data Retrieval

Introduction

*"The sky above the port was the color of television, tuned to a dead channel."*

— Gibson, Neuromancer, Chapter 1

The Sprawl is never quiet, even when the neon dims. You sit in the back of the Sprawl Hole, half a stub of a Thai cigarette cooling in the ashtray, because The Finn said to wait, and waiting is what runners do when the air smells of stale beer and ozone from a busted CRT. Your deck rests on the formica table, its case scratched from a hundred runs, its cables coiled like sleeping snakes. Outside, a rain that hasn't stopped for three days taps against the plexiglass, then runs down the seam of the door in thin mineral threads. The Finn is on the phone in the corner, his voice a low rumble you don't try to listen to. You drink your coffee black because sugar costs extra and the waitress has stopped asking. The cup is warm against your palm; the coffee tastes faintly of iron and burnt chicory. You check your watch. He said eleven, and it's eleven-fourteen now.

Development

You jack in at the Sprawl Hole, the familiar cold of the trodes spreading across your temples. The matrix unfolds around you, a maze of data streams and corporate architecture. The target is a Hosaka subsidiary—not the big leagues, but big enough to have something worth hiding.

"Packet's ready," The Finn says. "Corporate firewall, mid-level security. You should be in and out before they notice."

"Should be," you say.

"Don't get greedy."

The entry node is a simple router, the kind you see a thousand times in any given run. You move through it quickly, following the data streams toward the target. The server architecture is standard Hosaka—clean lines, efficient security, nothing fancy. The kind of place that thinks it's untouchable. So you slip in like you belong there, because the matrix doesn't care about credentials, only about behavior, and your behavior says employee, not intruder. The data streams around you glow in pale corporate blue, then warm to amber as you descend. Each layer of encryption hums at a slightly different frequency. Your deck translates the hum into something your wetware can read: depth markers, system load, the faint heartbeat of a server farm dreaming in shifts.

Somewhere two layers down, you pass a corp security construct—a flat gray shape that doesn't quite have a face. It turns toward you, then turns away, because your behavior signature says maintenance tech, and maintenance techs are supposed to be here. You don't hold your breath, because holding your breath in the matrix means nothing, but you feel the old nervous flutter in your stomach anyway.

You find the data node in the corporate depths. It's buried under three layers of encryption, but your deck makes quick work of that. You reach out with your probe, feeling the connection form. The packet is small, as advertised—a routine pull, the kind you could do in your sleep, the kind The Finn normally wouldn't even bother calling you for.

The data transfers.

But something's wrong.

Climax

The file is bigger than it should be. Instead of the small data packet you expected, this is a full database—employee records, financial reports, something that looks like payroll information. Corporate secrets, the kind that could get people fired. Or worse.

"Finn," you say. "This isn't the right file."

Silence on the other end. Then: "Check the metadata."

You do. The file is tagged with a corporate code you recognize—Tessier-Ashpool. A subsidiary you've never heard of, but the name is everywhere in the Sprawl. The megacorp behind half the data streams you're surfing through. The name your mother once whispered when you were a kid, half a story about how her brother had disappeared into a job for them. The name you had spent ten years trying not to think about.

"This is T-A," you say. "What's T-A doing with Hosaka's payroll?"

"What do you care?" The Finn's voice is different now. Harder. "You got paid. Take the job and run."

You think about pulling the plug right there, dropping the connection, leaving the file to rot in the buffer. That would be the smart play. But the file is already inside you now, pressed against the wet layers of your memory like a splinter of glass, and you can feel its shape—the names, the amounts, the dates stretching back five years. Someone is paying people who shouldn't be on any payroll. Someone is moving money into a subsidiary that doesn't exist on any public ledger. And Tessier-Ashpool, because they are Tessier-Ashpool, has been doing it through Hosaka so the trace ends in the wrong place.

The data streams around you are starting to shift. You can feel the corporate security waking up, sensing the intrusion. You've been in here too long. The blue has gone amber, then a sullen red, then a red that pulses like a vein. Your deck flickers a warning: ICE on close approach. Not a routine patrol, then. Something has flagged the pull, and now something is coming to ask why.

"Jack out," The Finn says. "Now."

You do.

Denouement

The Sprawl Hole materializes around you. The Finn is watching you with that expression he always has, like he's calculating how much you're worth. The data is already gone, transferred to a location you don't know. The trodes lift off your temples with a faint wet click. Your hands are still shaking.

"What was that?" you ask.

"What do you mean?"

"T-A. You knew what was in that file."

The Finn doesn't answer. He just pours himself another drink and looks away. After a while, you realize he's not going to. The whiskey catches the light from the busted neon sign outside, throwing a thin gold line across the back of his hand. The bar smells of bourbon and cigarettes and the faint citrus bite of the disinfectant the new barman uses on the glasses. Somewhere in the back, a girl laughs, then stops.

"Get some rest," he says finally. "Tomorrow's going to be a long day."

You go.

But the data stays with you, burning in your memory like a brand. Corporate secrets. Payroll information. A thread that connects Hosaka to Tessier-Ashpool in ways you don't understand yet. You tell yourself it's just a job, that you pulled the file and you got paid and that's the end of it. You tell yourself the names on the payroll aren't your problem. You tell yourself a lot of things, standing on the wet stairs outside the Sprawl Hole, the rain hitting the collar of your jacket, the neon painting the puddles pink and blue.

The Sprawl is full of threads like that. Pull on one, and you never know what you'll find. Most runners learn that lesson once and then they stop pulling. You have never been a most-runners kind of runner. You pull, because not pulling is how you end up like the old-timers in the Sprawl Hole, telling the same three stories for ten years, watching their reflexes go soft, waiting for a job that never comes.

You pull on your blanket and try to sleep. The rain hasn't stopped. You can hear it through the single-pane window of your cubby, that thin steady hiss that used to mean safety when you were a kid and now means nothing. You close your eyes. You open them. You are still running the numbers on the payroll in your head, because that's what happens when a job goes sideways: the data keeps running in you, like a song you can't get out of your skull, and the only thing that will stop it is another job, or another drink, or another morning.

The data follows you into your dreams.
