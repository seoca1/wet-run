Rydell: Post-Lovelock Drift

837 wordsBridge Trilogy

Hook

"Lovelock was dead because Rydell had been too slow. That was the math. That was the only math."

Three days after the Lovelock job, Rydell sat in a Denny's on the southern-arc of the 101 and tried to drink a cup of coffee. The cup was ceramic. The coffee was bad. He held the cup with both hands because his hands had started shaking again, and he didn't want anyone to see.

He'd been on the bridge once, before. Just once, a long time ago, on a dare, when he was still a cop in Memphis. He'd climbed to the third-tier of the suspension-cable and looked down at the Bay, and he'd felt something he hadn't been able to name. Not fear. Not exhilaration. Something else. Something the bridge had felt like: a place where everything was in-between.

Rising Action

Now he was going back. Not because he wanted to. Because Lovelock's death had made him unfit for corporate-work — his contract with IntenSecure was voided within twelve hours of the body being found — and the only people who would talk to him were the people who'd been on the bridge all along.

Skinner, who Rydell had been told to find. Chevette, who Skinner was training. The Armenians, who fed people and didn't ask questions. The Black Travelers, who moved things across the bridge's many-borders.

Rydell finished his coffee. He left money on the table. He walked out into the parking-lot and stood by his rental-car for a minute, looking at the sky.

The sky was the color of a dead-screen. The clouds were bruise-purple at the bottom and bone-white at the top, the kind of color-contrast that the corporate-cameras loved, the kind of image that sold cars and soft-drinks and the idea that the future was still a thing that could be sold. Rydell had been part of that industry for ten years. He had stood in parking-lots like this, in cities like this, and looked at skies like this, and told himself that he was doing something that mattered.

Midpoint Reversal

He hadn't been. He knew that now.

Lovelock had known it before him. Lovelock had been the first one to admit, in the back of the IntenSecure car, on the way to the job, that the corporate-world was a kind of organized-lies. Lovelock had been older. Lovelock had been smarter. Lovelock had known that the only honest-move was to take the money and not pretend.

Lovelock was dead.

Rydell got in the rental-car. He drove north, toward the Bay, toward the bridge, toward Skinner and Chevette and the Armenians and the Black Travelers, toward the place where everything was in-between.

Climax

He didn't have a plan. He didn't have a job. He didn't have anything except his shaking hands and his dead-employer and the knowledge that Lovelock had been the best boss he'd ever had, and the worst, and the only one who had ever told him the truth.

The bridge was waiting. The bridge was always waiting. That was what bridges did.

He parked on a side-street near the on-ramp. He walked up to the bridge's first-tier and stood there, breathing the salt-and-rust air, feeling the fog on his face, listening to the bridge-creak.

Skinner was somewhere up there. Chevette was somewhere up there. The Armenians were somewhere up there. They would feed him and they would listen to him and they would not ask him to be anything except what he was, which was: a man whose boss had been killed because of him, looking for a place where the in-between felt like home.

Resolution

Rydell climbed.

"The bridge had a way of making everyone an in-between. Rydell had been an in-between all his life. He'd just never had a place that admitted it."

The first person he met was Chevette, who gave him coffee and didn't ask his name. The second was Skinner, who did ask his name, and then told him to sleep, and then sat with him while he slept, and didn't leave.

That was how Rydell came to the bridge. Not because of any plan. Because of Lovelock, and because of the bridge's strange-magnetism, and because some places are made for the people who have nowhere else to go.

The bridge was one of those places. Rydell was one of those people.

They fit.
