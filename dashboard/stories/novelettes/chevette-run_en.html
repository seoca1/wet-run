Chevette Run

1590 wordsSprawl Trilogy

"She picked the lock in fifteen seconds. Three seconds if it was one of those cheap Chinese ones."

— Virtual Light, Chevette Washington

Introduction

Rain was fated to become fog. Fog was fated to become rain. The San Francisco sky was tuned, like some giant 1993 television, to a dead channel — no color, only static. That static was sound — the sound of nothing, the echo of emptiness. Static on the bridge is different. Static on the bridge was resonance. The sound of cables swinging in the wind, chains grinding, someone's bicycle pedal humming. Consequently, the bridge did not stay silent. The bridge sang. Its lyric was refusal. The rhythm of refusal had no end.

I am Chevette Washington. Bicycle messenger. Twenty-four. Black fixie, red hook, sleeping in a tent tied off hanger eleven, born on the bridge. The bridge was my school. The bridge was my church. The bridge was everything I knew. Therefore, if the bridge fell, I fell. But the bridge still stood. The bridge always stood.

Last night I rode a man's bicycle. A man named Roosevelt. He was from Brooklyn, mid-forties, made computer chips. We ate Brooklyn pizza. The pizza was warm. The fog was cold. The contrast of pizza and fog was life. Life was the rhythm between warmth and cold. The bridge was resonance. Life was rhythm. Rhythm was living.

This morning I woke on the bridge. Hanger eleven, five meters up, inside the tent. The canvas was damp. The fog passed through the canvas. Someone's bicycle pedal hummed. Someone's rope creaked. The bridge sang. The bridge's cry was song — our song. Its lyric was refusal. The rhythm of refusal had no end.

Rising Action

Roosevelt's wife went to an SF convention for the first time in her life. Moscow, 1993. There was a man named Matisse. Matisse told a man named Dave — I didn't know, but Matisse told Dave about me. There was a company named Svar. Svar was LOVETIDE's competitor. Svar made "single-determinacy microfilament." Microfilament. I didn't know that word. Therefore, that word frightened me. Fear was ignorance. Ignorance was refusal. Refusal was freedom.

Roosevelt said: "Chevette, someone scanned your bicycle pedal. Data key. Very small. Very expensive. People are going to kill for it."

I looked at the pedal. Red pedal. Black fixie. The pedal was part of the bicycle. A scan — what could it mean? Consequently, the scan was culture. The history of the bicycle etched into my pedal. The pedal stitches the bridge. The pedal reads the bridge. The pedal records it.

"The data key is LOVETIDE's new building blueprint," Roosevelt said. "Svar stole it. It's inside your pedal. Worth half a million dollars."

I laughed. Half a million. On my pedal. I make eight hundred a month. The pedal is a red pedal. A pedal is a pedal.

Roosevelt didn't laugh. "Chevette, I'm not joking. You have to keep the pedal. Buy a new bike. The pedal —"

"No." I said. "The pedal is my pedal. The bike is my bike."

I rode the fixie across the bridge. The Bay Bridge's eastern span had collapsed after the 1989 Loma Prieta quake. The western span was occupied by the squatters. The western span was my home. The eastern span was empty. The eastern span was the sea. In the end, the bridge belonged to the squatters. The bridge was mine. The bridge was everyone's. The bridge was nobody's.

The data wasn't on the bicycle pedal. The data was in me. In my stubbornness. In my refusal. In my fixie. What Matisse had tried to sell Svar wasn't technology — it was culture. What the bridge meant to the squatters. The bridge's refusal of what an SF convention meant. The upgrade cycle trying to dismantle the bridge. Therefore, the bridge was refusal of the upgrade. Refusal was culture. Culture was the bridge.

The cry of chains, the creak of rope, an old song bleeding out of someone's radio. All of it the rhythm of the bridge. Rhythm was civilization. Civilization was refusal. Refusal was culture. Culture was me. I was culture.

But the fog thickened. The fog swallowed the bridge. Swallowing was refusal. Therefore, the fog was refusal. The fog was culture. Culture was me. I was fog. The fog was me.

Climax

Matisse came looking for me. The fog thickened. The fog passed through the canvas. The bridge hummed. The pedal hummed. Fog, bridge, and pedal cried out together. The three cries became one. The cry was song.

"Chevette Washington." Matisse said. "Your pedal. We made it for Svar. The pedal holds LOVETIDE's future."

I said: "It's my pedal."

Matisse smiled. The smile didn't look like a smile. "Chevette, the pedal is Svar's asset. You stole it."

I dismounted the fixie. The pedal was a red pedal. A half-million-dollar pedal. I looked at the pedal. I felt the pedal. The pedal was my pedal. The bridge was my bridge. The pedal and the bridge were me.

"I didn't steal it," I said. "It's part of my bike. The bike is mine."

Matisse laughed. The laugh didn't sound like a laugh. "Chevette, you're a country bumpkin. A bridge bumpkin. Your bicycle pedal holds half a million dollars of technology. You don't even know it."

I did know. I knew. What my bicycle pedal held wasn't technology — it was culture. What the bridge meant to the squatters. The bridge's refusal of the city. The bridge's refusal of the upgrade cycle. The pedal was the symbol of that refusal.

"Matisse," I said. "Your company wants to smash the bridge. Your company wants to move the people off the bridge. Your company wants to erase the squatters. My pedal is the bridge's pedal. You don't deserve my pedal."

Matisse reached out an arm. I flipped the pedal around. The pedal struck Matisse's hand. Matisse staggered. I jumped on the fixie. But Matisse got back up. Matisse's hand held a pistol.

The pistol had no bullets — in the end, a pistol. A pistol was intimidation. Intimidation was the opposite of refusal. Intimidation was acceptance. Acceptance was death.

But I threw the pedal. The pedal cut through the fog and caught on a bridge cable. The cable groaned. The pedal became part of the cable. The pedal became part of the bridge. The bridge was part of me. I was the bridge.

"Chevette," Matisse said. "Give me the pedal."

I said: "The pedal is already part of the bridge. The pedal is the bridge's part. You can't take the bridge. You can't take me."

The fog was thick. The fog swallowed us. In the fog, Matisse and I vanished. What remained was only the bridge. The bridge cried. The bridge sang. The lyric was: refusal. Refusal. Refusal. The lyric of refusal had no end. The rhythm of refusal had no end. Consequently, refusal was eternal.

On the bridge, in the fog, I touched the pedal. The pedal was warm. Warmth was refusal. Warmth was me. The pedal was mine. I was mine. Refusal was mine. I was refusal.

Resolution

The fog still swallows the Bay Area. Same fog. Same bridge.

I drank coffee on the bridge. A chain-smoking old man sat beside me. Menthol. Menthol. Menthol. The first thing Chiba ever taught me. Chiba — wherever was he now. Chiba was gone. But menthol remained. What remained was culture. Culture was on the bridge. The bridge was culture.

"Chevette," the old man said. "The pedal?"

"I gave it to the bridge," I said.

The old man smiled. The smile looked like a smile. "The pedal belongs to the bridge. The bridge's pedal. The bridge belongs to nobody. The bridge is everyone's. The bridge is ours."

I looked at the old man. He was in his seventies. Voice roughened by chain-smoking. He'd lived on the bridge for thirty years. I knew the old man was right. The pedal wasn't mine. The pedal was the bridge's. The pedal was everyone's. The pedal was refusal. Refusal of the upgrade. Refusal of technology. Refusal of the corporation. A pedal of refusal.

"Old man," I said. "You're right. But the pedal is mine. I am the bridge's. The bridge is mine. My pedal is part of my bridge. My bridge is mine."

The old man smiled. We smiled together. The fog wrapped us. The fog was cold. Our laughter was warm. Warmth and cold coexisted. That's how the bridge worked. The bridge was refusal. The bridge was coexistence. Coexistence was culture. Culture was warmth. Warmth was me.

Epilogue

"Tomorrow the bridge will still be here. Tomorrow I will be too."

I looked at the pedal on the bridge. The pedal was a red pedal. The pedal was mine. The pedal was the bridge's. The pedal was everyone's. The pedal was half a million dollars of technology. But I don't know that. I only know the pedal is my pedal. The pedal proved me. I proved myself.

Somewhere the fog still swallowed the Bay Area. Somewhere I was not. But here, on the bridge, I was me. The bridge was me. The pedal was me. Refusal was me. I was me.
