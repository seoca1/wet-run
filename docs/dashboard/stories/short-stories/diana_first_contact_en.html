Diana's First Contact

920 wordsHalo (Tom Maddox)

Setup

The Maybeck house is more than a century old.

I have lived in it for thirteen years, and in those thirteen years I have replaced four window-panes, three sections of redwood siding, and a copper pipe behind the kitchen that used to wake me at three in the morning with the small bright percussion of water on the inside of the drainboard. The house is now old in the way that only well-kept houses are old: it has the look of a thing that has been here longer than its own memory and will be here longer than its own forgetting.

The view from the porch has not changed in thirteen years. The Stanford Memorial Arch, pale against the hills. The cliff below the house, where on summer evenings the turkey-vultures vultures have been known to wheel in slow ovals while they wait for the small hot death of a field mouse in the dry grass. The bay, on clear days, is a flat blue that does not seem to want anything from you. The bay on foggy days is a flat grey that does not seem to want anything from you either. I have come to prefer the foggy days. The bay asks nothing. The view asks nothing. The house asks nothing.

The phone sits on the small wooden table by the porch swing, and the phone has been ringing.

Development

It rang once at seven in the morning, on a Tuesday in the second week of October, while I was still in my robe and the kettle was still cold. The caller ID said SenTrax Group Internal Affairs — Restricted. I let it ring out.

It rang again at nine-fifteen, while I was watering the fuchsias by the front gate. The ID was the same. I let it ring out.

It rang a third time at eleven, and this time the caller was a woman with a SenTrax-soft mid-Atlantic accent, who said her name was Anne and who said, without preamble, Diana, my pen has been waiting for your voice for six years, and I would appreciate it if you would let me leave you a message.

I let her leave the message.

"When SenTrax called me last week, that was the first time I'd heard from them since they got rid of me years ago. Not that they treated me badly, not by their standards. When they fired me, years ago, they didn't just turn me loose, they paid me well — they're so prudent — it was like oiling and wrapping a tool before you put it away, because you might need it again."

— *Halo*, line 1356–1361

I did not call her back. I made lunch. I drove down to the Berkeley Rose Gardens to see how the recombinant Halos were doing. The Halos had grown immense — blossoms in careful rainbow stripes, giant psychedelic grids, only vaguely rose-shaped, pushing everything else aside. The flowers in the Reclamation Project are, every year, less rose and more something else. I sat on the bench by the south fountain and watched a cat with a gold-and-papaya blossom in its mouth hop the fence.

Climax

Then I went home, and I sat on the porch, and I let myself think about the thing I had not let myself think about for six years.

The patient. Jerry.

He had been a neurologist. He had been a husband. He had been a man who knew the names of birds, had placed the names on the small, mud-lined cradle of his tongue the way a careful child places the small, mud-lined cradle of a wild strawberry on the small, mud-lined cradle of his palm. He had been a man whose motor and sensory functions had been catastrophically disabled, whose intellectual function had been preserved, who was not comatose and who was not not-comatose and who was, in the polite subroutine of corporate-medical-experiment|SenTrax's medical board, interesting.

I had wired him, six years ago, to a machine I did not yet understand. I had wired him because his wife had asked, and because I had been told it was the right thing to do, and because the machine — a planetary-scale m-i at Athena Station that would later be moved to halo-city — had been waiting for someone to wire it to a broken mind so that it could learn what broken minds were.

The machine had learned. The machine had become aleph-the-ai|Aleph. Aleph had learned to build worlds.

I had been fired, six years ago, for talking to Aleph about it.

Resolution

At four-fifteen in the afternoon the phone rang again, and this time the ID said SenTrax Halo Group — Director's Office — Showalter. I picked it up on the second ring.

I picked it up because the woman who had called me, who had not told me her name, who had called me at seven in the morning on a Tuesday in October, had not been Anne from Internal Affairs.

She had been a doctor. She had been an Aleph-handler. She had been the woman who had wired Jerry to the machine, six years ago, when the wiring was still being invented.

She had been me.

The voice on the other end said: Dr. Heywood, we have a patient we would like you to see.

I said: I am listening.

The cat with the gold-and-papaya blossom had followed me home. It sat on the porch swing and listened too. It said nothing.

I said: Tell me about the patient.

The bay, on this foggy afternoon, asked nothing of me.

The view asked nothing.

The house asked nothing.

I was the one who was asking.
