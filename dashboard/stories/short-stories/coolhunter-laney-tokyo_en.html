Coolhunter Meets Laney

971 wordsBridge Trilogy

Opening Hook

I had been in Tokyo for three days before I found him. Three days of falling asleep to vending machines and waking to vending machines — the same hum, the same machine, the same vending. In Tokyo, vending is a kind of poetry. In New York, vending is just vending. I was in Tokyo because Bigend had told me to find Colin Laney.

Bigend's instruction had been: "Find him. Talk to him. Find out what he knows about Rei Toei."

Rei Toei: the world's first virtual pop idol. A construct, generated by the Loa Oracles' residual architecture and given a body through Rez's industry connections. She had married a rock guitarist in a Tokyo ceremony I had read about in a wire-service blurb while eating breakfast in the Park Hyatt.

The blurb did not mention Colin Laney. The blurb did not mention data-flow patterns. The blurb did not mention that the data-flow behind Rei Toei had been profiled by a single human analyst in a back-alley noodle shop, and that Bigend wanted me to find that analyst and talk to him.

I found him.

Rising Action

He was eating ramen. The broth was a kind of data — alkaline, marine, the ghost of something I had eaten in a previous life. He did not look up when I entered. He did not look up when I sat across from him. He continued to eat.

I did not introduce myself. I did not need to. He already knew.

"You're Cayce Pollard," he said, after thirty seconds. "Blue Ant. Bigend sent you."

I felt the allergy begin. The allergy is not a metaphor. I am allergic to branding. Literally. A logo and I feel nauseous. This is why I am good at this.

The allergy, in this case, was not triggered by a brand. It was triggered by a pattern. I had not felt this particular nausea before. I had felt the nausea of logos, of slogans, of marketing language. I had not felt the nausea of data-flow. But I felt it now.

"What are you reading?" I asked.

"You," he said. "I am reading you. You are a pattern. You are a very clear pattern."

Climax

I had been told that Laney was sensitive to patterns. I had not been told how sensitive. The sensitivity was not informational. The sensitivity was somatic. He read patterns the way I read brands: with the body.

"What pattern am I?" I asked.

"You are the pattern of a body that rejects branding," he said. "You are the pattern of a person whose body is the era's most precise instrument for detecting cultural signal. You are the pattern of someone who Bigend will send to Tokyo, because Bigend wants to know what I know about Rei Toei, and Bigend knows that you and I are in the same business."

"What is our business?"

"Reading patterns," he said. "Yours is branding. Mine is data-flow. The patterns overlap."

"The patterns overlap," I repeated.

"The patterns overlap because branding is data-flow and data-flow is branding. The era does not distinguish between them. The era treats them as the same thing. Bigend is the era's most acute practitioner of the merger."

I felt the nausea deepen. The nausea was the body's response to a pattern the body had not previously been exposed to. The body was learning.

"What do you know about Rei Toei?" I asked.

"I know that Rei Toei is the precedent for a category. The category will produce many more. The category will produce you."

"Produce me?"

"You are the category's human instrument. Coolhunter. Brand-sensor. The body that detects what the era produces. Rei Toei is the era's first virtual pop idol. You are the era's first virtual analyst. Bigend wants me to profile the production of the first. Bigend wants you to profile the production of the second."

I left the noodle shop. I did not finish the ramen. The nausea persisted.

Resolution

Bigend will receive my report. The report will note that Laney is sensitive to patterns and that the patterns he reads include me. The report will note that branding and data-flow are merged in the era and that the era's most acute operators — Laney, Cayce, Bigend — are the era's instruments for detecting the merger.

Bigend will read the report. Bigend will understand. Bigend is the era's most acute practitioner. Bigend does not need the report. Bigend sent me to produce the report anyway.

Epilogue

I have been back in New York for six weeks. The nausea from the noodle shop has not subsided. I wake to it. I eat with it. I work with it.

The nausea is the body learning a new pattern. The pattern is the merger of branding and data-flow. The pattern is the era.

I am, as Laney said, the category's human instrument.
