The Footage Remained

1774 wordsBlue Ant Trilogy

"The footage was the only thing that was real. Everything else was interpretation. But interpretation was what the world ran on."

— Spook Country, Tito's internal monologue

I. Havana Rooftop

The iPhone vibrated against Tito's palm. Hot metal, the way a stolen phone always runs hot. He stood on the concrete rooftop of a Centro Habana tenement, his back to the building's water-tank, the Plaza de la Catedral spread below him in the late-morning haze of exhaust fumes and frying food.

The locative art piece hovered above the cathedral's bell tower — a ghostly halo of light, the Virgin Mary's missing face, visible only through the phone's camera. The art was his installation. The art was the groundwork — the Brown operation's term for what he did for them. The groundwork was: ensure the locative art pieces were deployed across the geopolitical fault lines Bigend's clients wanted to understand. Havana. Busan. Rotterdam. Vancouver. The art was the evidence the trucks moved.

The phone vibrated again. He answered. Cayce Pollard's voice was a small cold sound in his ear.

"The container in Busan. The Ono-Sendai. The marks."

"I know the marks." Tito spoke low. The sun was white on the white buildings. The buildings made the sun hotter. "The marks are coordinates for the next installation. The installation after the next. The installation after that."

"The marks are what Bigend wants to find. Bigend doesn't know the marks are coordinates. Bigend thinks the marks are artifacts."

"The marks are both," Tito said. He crouched by the water-tank. The tank was rusted. The rust left a film of iron on his fingers. The iron tasted of pennies. "The marks are coordinates for the art. The marks are artifacts for the operation. Both are true. Neither is the answer."

"What is the answer?"

"The answer is the gap. The answer is what the camera didn't film. The answer is what happened between the airports."

The phone went silent. The silence was the smell of fried plantains from a window across the courtyard. The plantains were someone's lunch. The lunch was a stranger's life.

II. Soho Apartment

The drive was warm against Cayce's thigh. Three terabytes. The warmth bled through the fabric of her jeans. The warmth was Tito's heat from when he'd handed her the drive in a hotel lobby in The Hague. Tito's hands had been cold. The drive's warmth was the only warm thing she'd touched in weeks.

The apartment in Soho smelled of dust and old paper and the cold leather of her Filson bag. Cayce set the drive beside her laptop. The laptop was open. The laptop's screen was empty. The laptop had been empty for three hours.

Milgrim stood in the doorway. He hadn't trembled in six months. His face was the color of bruised paper. His voice was the color of nothing.

"Bigend wants the audit by noon."

"The audit will say what the audit will say." Cayce didn't look up. The drive's warmth was the only warm thing in the room. The room smelled of cold.

"What does the audit say?"

"The audit says the Brown operation moved Ono-Sendai units marked with locative art coordinates." Cayce reached for the drive. Her hand was cold against the warmth. "The audit says Bigend has been funding both ends of the network without knowing it."

Milgrim walked into the room. His shoes squeaked on the wood floor. The wood floor was a wood floor that had never been polished. The polish was for apartments that smelled of polish. The Milgrim apartment smelled of Milgrim.

"What does Bigend pay for?"

"Bigend pays for the audit." Cayce opened the first file. Havana. The locative art piece — Tito's halo, his missing face, the ghost of a ghost. "Bigend pays for the answer. Bigend doesn't get the answer. The answer is the gap."

"The gap is what?"

"The gap is the data the cameras didn't capture. The gap is what Don Alejandro filmed before he died. The gap is what Tito filmed after he died. The gap is what Bigend pays fifty thousand American for and never receives."

III. The Pattern

The third file was Tito's face. The face filled the laptop. The face was older than the Havana rooftop. The face was the brown operation's last survivor, breathing into the camera in a container in Busan.

Tito was saying something. The audio was stripped — Brown operation protocol. But Cayce could read his lips. She had spent three years reading Tito's lips through footage that was missing audio. Three years. 412 hours. The reading was a smell she couldn't name — the smell of absence.

The footage showed: not weapons. Not drugs. Not currency. Servers. Racks of them. Blade servers. Custom cooling that hummed like a refrigerator in a morgue. The cooling hummed in the footage. The cooling hummed in Cayce's apartment. The cooling hum was the same hum.

Labels in Korean and English: Hosaka. Maas Biolabs. Kombinat. The labels were white. The labels were the absence of color.

Beneath the servers: a single Ono-Sendai unit. Modified. The casing was scored with marks. Cayce leaned closer. The laptop screen pushed warmth onto her face. The warmth was the Ono-Sendai's warmth. The Ono-Sendai had been warm since Tito handed it to her in The Hague.

"Coordinates," she said out loud. Milgrim was standing three feet behind her. She could smell his breath — coffee, weak. "Forty-seven locations. The same coordinates as the locative art. The same coordinates as Don Alejandro's 412 hours."

"The same coordinates," Milgrim said, "as the Brown operation's debriefs."

"The Brown operation doesn't have coordinates." Cayce turned around. Milgrim's face was the face of someone who had once been afraid of everything and now had nothing left to fear. "The Brown operation has clients with coordinates. The coordinates are Bigend's coordinates. The art is Tito's art. The operation is the gap between them."

IV. The Cue

Cayce called Tito. The call was at 14:00. Cayce's apartment smelled of itself.

"The coordinates are Bigend's." Cayce's voice was the voice that Brown operation clients never heard. "The coordinates are Bigend's Blue Ant coordinates. Bigend's coordinates are the same coordinates as the Brown operation's coordinates. Which means Bigend has been funding the Brown operation's own gap. Without knowing it. The gap has been funded by the gap. The absence has been paid for by the absence."

Tito stood on a different rooftop. The rooftop was in the morning. The morning was in Havana. Havana was in the gap.

"The cameras filmed the gap," Tito said. "The cameras filmed the gap because the cameras were inside the gap. The cameras were inside the gap because Bigend was outside the gap. Bigend was outside the gap because Bigend pays the gap to be outside the gap."

"The gap is the answer."

"The gap is the product." Tito's voice trembled. The trembling was the gap. "The gap is what Bigend buys when Bigend buys the archive. The gap is what the cameras film when the cameras film the cameras. The gap is what the Brown operation has been moving — the Brown operation has been moving the gap. The gap is the answer. The gap is forever."

V. The Footage

The drive went back into the shielded case. The case went into the bag. The bag went onto Cayce's shoulder. Cayce's shoulder smelled of itself. Cayce's shoulder was tired.

At 15:30, Cayce walked to the Tanaka tower. The elevator smelled of burned coffee and someone else's perfume. The elevators in Tanaka tower always smelled of someone else's perfume. The perfume was the gap.

Bigend's office was on the forty-seventh floor. The office smelled of brushed steel, pale wood, soft lighting, no smell at all. Bigend sat behind his desk. The desk was a desk. The desk was the absence of a desk.

"What did you find, Ms. Pollard?"

"I found the gap." Cayce set the shielded case on the desk. The desk was cold. The case was warm. The warmth bled into the desk. "The Brown operation has been moving the gap. The footage is the gap. The locative art is the gap. The Ono-Sendai is the gap. The gap is what you wanted, Hubertus. The gap is what Blue Ant bought."

"The gap is what I'm selling."

"The gap is what you can't sell." Cayce's voice was the voice of someone who had spent three years reading footage that was missing audio. "The gap has been sold already. The gap has been sold to the cameras. The gap has been sold to Tito. The gap has been sold to me. The gap has been sold to the Brown operation. Bigend, the gap is the only thing in the archive that doesn't have a price."

Bigend's smile was a thin smile. The smile didn't reach his eyes. The eyes smelled of nothing.

"The gap," Bigend said, "is what Bigend buys when Bigend buys nothing."

"The gap," Cayce said, "is what remains when Bigend has bought everything else."

VI. After

Cayce left the office. The elevator smelled of burned coffee. The lobby smelled of marble cleaner. The street smelled of diesel and rain. Cayce walked to the corner. The corner smelled of Cayce.

The footage remained. The footage was the only thing that was real. Everything else was interpretation. But interpretation was what the world ran on. And the world ran on the footage. And the footage was what Tito had filmed and what the cameras had filmed and what the Brown operation had moved and what Bigend had paid for and what never arrived.

The gap remained.

Somewhere Cayce was not. Somewhere Tito was not. Somewhere the footage was —

The footage was. The footage was. The footage was.
