Black Travelers: Saltline Run

1027 wordsBridge Trilogy

"The Black Travelers had no fixed-bridge-position. The Black Travelers moved along the bridge the way salt moves along a coastline: with the tide."

Hook

The run started at midnight, when the bridge's lower-tiers had emptied of the Lost-Norters and the tourists had gone back to the city below. The saltline was the invisible-border between the Travelers' territory and the Armenians' territory, a border that ran along the bridge's second-tier at the third-bay west, and the saltline was a thing that had to be crossed carefully.

The Black Travelers crossed the saltline carefully. The saltline was not a physical-barrier. The saltline was a kind of social-membrane, a kind of cultural-skin, a kind of unspoken-rule that said: this is where we end and where they begin.

Rising Action

The Travelers were moving cargo tonight. The cargo was food, mostly, but also some medicine, some clothing, some tools. The cargo was going from the Armenians to a community-of-Lost-Norters who had set up a temporary-camp at the bridge's fourth-tier east. The cargo was not heavy. The cargo was not dangerous. The cargo was just the bridge's basic-economy, the bridge's life-blood, the bridge's slow-trade.

The Travelers crossed the saltline in pairs. The first-pair went ahead. The first-pair checked for trouble. The first-pair signaled back when the coast was clear. The second-pair followed, carrying the cargo.

I was in the second-pair tonight. My partner was a woman named Squib, who had been a Traveler for twelve years. Squib was thin and dark and fast, the kind of person who moved through the bridge the way the wind moved through the cabling. Squib did not talk much. Squib did not need to talk much.

We moved through the dark. The bridge's second-tier was quiet. The fog was thin tonight. The Bay was visible below us, the orange-and-red lights of the city reflecting off the water in long-streaks that moved when we moved.

Midpoint Reversal

But the cargo was not just food. The cargo was also something else. Squib had told me about it before the run. Squib had told me that the Armenians had found something in the bridge's structural-crevices, something that the Lost-Norters had been using as a kind of currency, something that was worth more than food.

The cargo was a piece of the bridge itself. The cargo was a piece of the original-bay-bridge that had fallen into the Bay during the earthquake and had been recovered by the Lost-Norters and had been polished and shaped and had been kept as a kind of relic. The cargo was a piece of the bridge's history.

But the saltline was not the only border. The saltline was just one of the bridge's many-borders. The bridge had other-borders, between the bridge's many-substrate-economies, between the Armenians and the Lost-Norters, between the tourists and the corporate-extraction-agents. The bridge had borders that crossed and re-crossed each other. The bridge had borders that no single-person could see all of. But the Black Travelers could see some of them. The Black Travelers could see the borders that mattered. The Black Travelers could see the borders that carried the bridge's slow-trade.

The cargo was heavy. The cargo was heavier than the food-and-medicine combined. Squib and I carried it between us, and we carried it carefully, and we carried it as if we were carrying a piece of the bridge's memory.

Climax

We reached the Lost-Norters' camp at four AM. The Lost-Norters were asleep in their temporary-shelters, the kind of shelters that were made of corrugated-plastic and salvaged-tarps and whatever else the bridge had given them. We set the cargo down at the camp's edge, where the Armenians had told us to set it down.

A woman came out of one of the shelters. The woman was old, in bridge-years, which meant she was maybe-thirty. The woman looked at the cargo. The woman looked at us.

"Thank you," the woman said.

"It's the bridge's," Squib said.

The woman nodded. The woman touched the cargo with both-hands, the way a person touches a relic. The woman carried the cargo into the shelter.

Squib and I stood at the camp's edge for a minute, listening to the bridge-creak, looking at the city-lights beyond the fog. Then we started back toward the Armenians' territory.

Resolution

We crossed the saltline again on the way back. The saltline was the same. The saltline was always the same. The saltline was the invisible-border between the Travelers' territory and the Armenians' territory, the same border that we had crossed an hour ago, the same border that we would cross again tomorrow-night.

But we were not the same. We had carried a piece of the bridge's memory. We had carried a piece of the bridge's history. We had carried a piece of the bridge to the people who were keeping the bridge's history.

That was what the Black Travelers did. That was what we had been doing for years. We moved things across the bridge. We moved food and medicine and clothing and tools. We moved the bridge's basic-economy. And sometimes, on special-nights, we moved the bridge's history.

"The Black Travelers were the bridge's immune-system. We moved the things that needed to be moved. We moved the things that could not be moved by anyone else. We were the bridge's hands."

The fog thickened. The bridge-creak sounded. The city-lights flickered. We walked back toward our territory, two Travelers crossing a bridge in the dark, carrying nothing but the memory of having carried something.
