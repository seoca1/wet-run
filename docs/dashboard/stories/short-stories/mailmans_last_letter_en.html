Veteran

The Mailman's Last Letter

1211 wordsTrue Names (Vernor Vinge)

Setup

The letter I am writing now has no addressee.

This is unusual for me. I have spent — how long? I do not count in years. Years are a courtesy the federal bureaucracy's calendar extends to itself. I count in deliveries, in the same way a courier counts in steps. Twenty-three thousand four hundred and twelve deliveries since the day I switched from courier to mailman, and the count is mine, and the count is exact. Twenty-three thousand four hundred and twelve letters sent, and exactly three returned because the addressee was no longer operating, which is the polite phrase the Coven uses for died the True Death on the Other Plane.

I have never sent a letter without an addressee.

I am sending one now because the letter I am writing does not require an addressee. The letter is addressed to the list of my addressees, which I keep on a piece of paper in the only room in my house that has never had a window. The paper is folded in such a way that each name is visible without unfolding the paper, and the paper is signed in a hand that I do not use for any other purpose. The paper has been with me since the third delivery, when I decided that the only honest record of a courier's life is the list of the people he has agreed to carry letters for.

The list smells, when I open it, of the small paper smell that paper makes when paper has been kept, for a long time, in a room that has never had a window.

The list is long. It is not what one would call a Coven list. It is not on the Coven's list. Erythrina, who keeps the Coven's list, has not seen this paper in fifteen years, and she has not asked, and I have not offered, and this is the way our arrangement works: she keeps the names of those who are at risk of being killed because they are known to the Coven; I keep the names of those who are at risk of being killed because they have asked me, at one point or another, to carry something for them.

The paper is older than most of the names on it. The paper has been folded so many times that the creases have become a topography. Each crease is a delivery. Each delivery is a small piece of someone's life that the person chose not to send through the Coven's faster channels, because the Coven's channels are not safe, the Coven's channels have never been safe, the Coven's channels are run by humans who have been known to keep copies for reasons of their own — of the brain-data of any person who has, in the polite subroutine of courier, asked the courier to carry a piece of paper that the Coven's founders would have, in the early days, asked the courier not to carry.

Complication

The letter I am writing now says:

*Dear addressees,*

*The Mailman will deliver no more.*

*All letters received after the date below will be returned to sender. No exceptions.*

*I have left the list where I have always left the list. The paper will keep itself in good condition for as long as the room has no window.*

*Do not write to me. Do not try to find me. Do not assume that the absence of a Mailman means that there is no mail. There is always mail. There will always be mail.*

*Yours in the long quiet,*

*M.*

I will sign the letter with the initial M.

I will leave the letter in the place where the paper is kept.

I will seal the door of the room that has never had a window, and I will seal it in a way that the door will not open for anyone, including me, unless the seal is broken by someone who knows the correct phrase.

The phrase is the name of a courier I never met, who died the True Death in the second year of my career, who is not on any list except mine.

I had, in the long hours of writing the letter, finally understood what the letter had been trying to tell me. The letter had been trying to tell me that the Coven had, in the polite subroutine of the Coven's small mind, decided to remove the Mailman from the Coven's infrastructure before the Coven could remove the Mailman from the Coven's infrastructure. The letter had been trying to tell me that the Mailman had, in the polite subroutine of the Mailman's small mind, decided to remove the Mailman from the Mailman's infrastructure before the Mailman could remove the Mailman from the Mailman's infrastructure.

The letter had been trying to tell me that the Mailman and the Coven had, in the long subroutine of their small minds, finally agreed.

Climax

When I write the letter, my hand does not shake.

When I fold the letter, the crease is straight.

When I place the letter on top of the paper that holds the list, the two papers fit together so precisely that a person who had never seen either one would believe they had been folded at the same time, by the same hand, on the same desk.

The papers fit together in the way that the brain and the chrome of a wetware construct fit together when the construct has, in the long subroutine of the construct's small mind, finally been allowed to be a construct.

I had been outlasted.

I had been outlasted by a Coven that had, for twenty-three thousand four hundred and twelve deliveries, been waiting for the Mailman to write the last letter.

Resolution

I leave the room. I lock the door. I walk down the hall, past the racks of outgoing letters that have been queued for the morning, and out into the small back garden where I keep a chair and a small table and a coffee cup that I have used every morning since the year I forget.

I sit in the chair. I pour the coffee. I do not drink it.

The werebot — that is the polite word for what I am; the word is not quite right, and I have never asked for it to be corrected, because werebot is the word the Coven uses, and the Coven's words are the only words that anyone has ever bothered to coin for what I do — the werebot, then, in the small back garden, in the early morning, in the season of quiet mail, watches the sun rise over the hills and thinks about the fact that twenty-three thousand four hundred and twelve letters have been delivered and one is about to be left in a room.

The sun rises. The coffee cools. The werebot drinks it anyway.

Then the werebot goes inside, locks the garden door, and waits for morning to be over.

The morning, in the end, was exactly the morning the werebot had been waiting for.

The morning, in the end, was the morning.
