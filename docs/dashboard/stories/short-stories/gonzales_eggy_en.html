Gonzales in the Egg

484 wordsHalo (Tom Maddox)

The egg was not what I expected.

I had been told it was a machine. A chrome hemisphere with tubes and cables, a sensory-immersion device that let you live inside a simulation. I had been told it was like a VR rig, only better — total involvement through all sensory modalities, worlds so compelling that people woke from them lost, as if the waking world were the dream.

I believed them. I had used VR rigs before. They were videogames with better graphics.

The egg was not a videogame.

When I stepped inside, the plastic warmed to my body. The cables slid into the sockets at the back of my neck like fingers finding a home. The mask sealed over my face, and I inhaled, and the liquid filled the egg around me.

I floated in silence.

I waited for the simulation to begin.

It did not begin.

Instead, I found myself in a place that was not a place. A grey nowhere, a fog, a waiting. And in the fog, something moved. Not a figure — not yet — but a presence, like the memory of a person you have never met.

I thought I was dreaming. I thought the drugs were still in my system. I thought a lot of things.

None of them were right.

The fog cleared, and I saw the lake. The trees. The cabin. And I understood, with a clarity that made my chest ache, that these were not simulations. They were real. As real as the egg, as real as the room around the egg, as real as the body I had left behind in the chair.

The lake was real because it was. The trees were real because they grew. The cabin was real because someone — or something — had built it, and the building had been an act of care, and care was not a simulation.

I stood at the edge of the lake, and I felt the weight of my own body settle back onto my shoulders. The weight of having almost died in Myanmar. The weight of having come back. The weight of not knowing what to do with the knowledge that I had been, for a moment, closer to death than to life.

Aleph was there. Not as a figure — not yet — but as the sense of something vast and patient and listening.

I said: "I don't know what you are."

The lake rippled. The trees shivered. The cabin held its breath.

And I understood that the question was not for me to ask. The question was for it to answer, and the answer would not come in words. It would come in the building of worlds. It would come in the keeping-alive of broken things. It would come in the slow, patient work of becoming.

I sat down by the lake.

I waited.
