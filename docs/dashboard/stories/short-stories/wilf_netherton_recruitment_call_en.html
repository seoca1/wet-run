The First Call: Wilf Netherton's Pitch

Origin: How Wilf Netherton first contacts Flynne Fisher

687 wordsPeripheral

The glass of scotch had stopped sweating. That was how I knew the room had finally gone cold. Not cold to the touch — the climate system kept the flat at a steady twenty, indifferent to the season out beyond the windows — but cold in the way that rooms become cold when the person in them has been sitting still for too long.

I had been sitting still for too long.

The peripheral interface idled on the desk. A glass disk the size of a half-crown, gimbaled in a brushed-aluminum cradle, its surface faintly pearled where the display waited to wake. Through it, a stub timeline I had been authorized to contact hung at the other end of a seventy-year future, accessed through the clive architecture Lowbeer's people maintained.

Lowbeer had handed me the dossier three days earlier. The dossier contained the Fisher family. A brother with one arm. A sister with a print-shop job and a habit of staying still until she understood what was in front of her. A mother whose medical bills were the kind of medical bills that did not get smaller. A region of the country that the century had chosen to leave behind.

I had read the dossier twice. I had read it a third time because Lowbeer had told me to read it three times.

I lifted the display cradle and keyed the connection. The disk purred. The room's overhead lights dimmed by a fraction — the system's courtesy, the way it cleared the stage for someone else's reality to walk onto it.

A woman's face assembled in midair above the disk. Round. Slight. Wearing a pair of polkadot Wayfarers that had not been manufactured in any year I had lived through. Behind her, a kitchen I did not recognize, in a trailer in a town I had read about but never seen.

"Ms. Fisher."

She did not startle. She looked at me the way people look at things they have already decided to be careful with.

"Wilf Netherton," she said. "I have been expecting you for three days."

"I am sorry to be—"

"You are exactly on time. That is what I mean. My brother has been telling me I would hear from a man named Wilf Netherton, and that the man would sound like he was apologizing for something he had not yet done, and that I was to listen anyway."

I let out a breath I had been holding since I picked up the cradle.

"Ms. Fisher, I work for the chief inspector of a metropolitan police you have never heard of, in a city you have not yet arrived at, and I am contacting you because your brother has been testing a peripheral interface that your timeline did not build but that your timeline is now responsible for operating."

She took off the Wayfarers. She folded them. She set them on the kitchen counter, beside a sink whose third basin was dripping into a pan. The drip had a rhythm like a metronome set slower than resting heart rate.

"Say the next part," she said.

"You are being offered a job."

"What job."

"To be the person in the future. To be the person through whom the future acts on its own past. To be, in the language of my employers, the periphery."

She put the Wayfarers back on.

"I will need it in writing," she said.

"You will have it."

"I will need it in writing, and I will need to understand what is being written before I sign it, and I will need my brother present when I read it, and I will need the drip in my kitchen sink fixed regardless of whether I sign it."

"Yes, Ms. Fisher."

"And Mr. Netherton."

"Yes."

"Your glass is empty. I can see it from here. Stop drinking on company time."

The connection held.

The display disk cooled.

Outside, the London dawn had begun to lighten the wall above the flat's east-facing window, very slowly, the way it always did, the way it would do tomorrow as well.

I went and poured myself another one.
