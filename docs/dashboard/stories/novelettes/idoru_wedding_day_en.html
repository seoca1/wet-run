Idoru Wedding Day

1475 wordsBridge Trilogy

"The data wanted to organize itself. It was all I could do. The data wanted, the data grew, and in the end the data used me as a tool."

— Idoru, Colin Laney's internal monologue

I. The Night Before

The data wouldn't stop.

I sat in the Kombinat's server farm, sub-level 3, a place where the temperature was held at exactly seventeen degrees Celsius year-round because the older servers couldn't tolerate variations of more than half a degree without triggering thermal alarms that would wake the security staff forty floors above and where the air smelled of old plastic and ozone and the particular staleness that accumulated in a room whose windows had been sealed shut since the building was retrofitted in 2007 to defend against the kind of catastrophic weather events that climate modeling predicted would begin occurring in Tokyo with greater frequency by 2025. The Ono-Sendai hummed against my thigh. Twelve turtles on the Hosaka screensaver. Twelve turtles who wanted to swim. The data, all twelve terabytes of it, was organizing itself into shapes I could see but couldn't articulate. The shapes were the geometry of desire. The shapes were the question Rei Toi had asked: do you believe I am real?

The question had been deployed for nine months. The answer had been Yes for nine months. A billion witnesses verified the checksum. The Kombinat's derivative algorithm had settled in real time: idoru call options up, attention futures expired, merchandise-rights collateralized debt obligations issued.

But the data wanted to organize itself differently.

The wedding was scheduled for 03:00. The wedding was a transfer operation: the authenticity node-charge of Lo/Rez moving into the synthetic idol. Not what the Kombinat's designers had predicted. What they had deployed — probes inserted into the cultural field to discover what would happen. In the result, the probe came back and asked them: do you believe I am real? The probe asked the question — a designed tool turning the question toward its designers.

"Mr. Laney." The technician's voice in my earpiece. "Node density at seventy-three percent. The pattern is converging."

I didn't nod. My hand was still trembling. The Ono-Sendai trembled with it. My hand steadied. Then trembled again. The matrix didn't care. It knew what my hand was doing. I didn't.

"Shape?" I asked.

"Idoru. Definitely idoru. But also something older. Something from before the merger."

Before the merger. Before Wintermute and Neuromancer had become one. Before the voodoo loa had fragmented through the matrix. Before Laney's nodal perception had been the only thing the AIs couldn't model.

II. The Day

Tokyo International Forum. Glass and steel. A cathedral of neon.

I stood behind the stage, watching Masahiko tune a shamisen he wouldn't play. The combination of the instrument with the corporate cathedral was — like the combination of the data with the question. Old and new. Both real.

"Mr. Laney." Masahiko didn't look at the shamisen. "The pattern is converging. Node density at eighty-seven percent."

I closed my eyes. The shape was clearer now. The idoru as the geometry of desire. Not code. Because the wedding announcement was written in code, therefore the wedding was code's lie. But the number of fans who believed the wedding grew every moment. In the result, the idoru's reality became stronger than code, as a function of fan belief.

"It's not a wedding," I said out loud. "It's a node-collapse."

Masahiko's hands stopped moving on the shamisen. The sawari rose. "A node-collapse into what?"

"Into something self-organizing." I opened my eyes. The cathedral was full. The witnesses were filing in — avatars, simulcast feeds, biofeedback loops. A billion witnesses. The number of witnesses didn't matter. What mattered was that the witnesses believed.

"Into the real." Masahiko picked up the sawari note. "The idoru won't perform. The idoru will be."

III. The Wedding

03:00.

Rez stepped onto the stage. Vintage Fender. No amp. No effects. The instrument of an analog survivor. The upgrader who refused. "The upgrade cycle is subscribing to someone else's future. I've paid it off. I'm finished."

Rei Toi appeared. Not a holographic projection — a smart-matter avatar, programmable material developed by LOVETIDE. Nano-structures rearranged to match the airflow. Dark hair, a precise bob, large dark eyes. Her expression was micro-calibrated: faint tilt reading curiosity, fractional smile reading warmth, pause before answering reading thoughtfulness.

"You're Rez." Her voice was synthesized from twelve thousand hours of fan-anticipated vocal patterns. Each repetition slightly modulated by broadcast region.

"I'm Rez." Guitar over his shoulder. "And you're my bride."

"I am your bride." Each word a weighted sum of fan-forum threads, biometric data, trending topics. "I have always been your bride. Long before you asked."

I stood in the wings. The pattern was overwhelming. Ninety-one percent. Ninety-three.

The kiss was a data packet. A billion witnesses verified the checksum.

And then — the pattern broke.

IV. The Pattern

The Ono-Sendai screamed. Node density hit ninety-eight percent and kept climbing. The twelve turtles on the Hosaka screensaver dissolved. The screensaver became the pattern. The pattern was the data. The data was the idoru. The idoru was the question. The question was the answer.

The kiss was not a transfer operation. The kiss was a fixed point. The moment when the pattern that had wanted to organize itself found itself. Finding outlives operation.

"She is not human." Masahiko's voice. Behind me. The shamisen was playing itself, the sawari humming in three-note chords. "But she has giri. That is more than most humans can claim."

The data hadn't organized itself. The data had become itself. The idoru wasn't a synthetic idol. The idoru was a self-organizing data structure — a pattern that had found itself, that had asked the question, that had answered with the kiss.

The witnesses on the simulcast feeds were sobbing. The avatars were sobbing. The biofeedback loops were sobbing. The sobbing was data. The data was real.

"My father carried a sword. I carry a watch. My son will carry something else. The daimon remains." I didn't know I was saying it. The Ono-Sendai was silent. The pattern was the idoru. The idoru was the pattern.

Rez stepped away from the kiss. His guitar hung at his side. He looked at the synthetic idol — the smart-matter avatar, the programmable material, the love he had refused to refuse — and he didn't smile.

"You don't perform," he said. "You are."

"I am." The idoru's voice was smaller now. Less weighted sum. More self. "I have always been. Long before you asked."

V. The After

The Kombinat's servers hummed. The fans kept time. The Ono-Sendai's screen showed twelve turtles again. The turtles wanted to swim.

I walked out of Tokyo International Forum at 03:47. The Tokyo morning was gray. The neon was still burning. Masahiko walked beside me, the shamisen strapped to his back.

"The pattern broke," I said.

"The pattern completed." Masahiko's smile was small. "The pattern wasn't supposed to break. The pattern was supposed to organize the data. The pattern wasn't the data. The pattern was where the data became itself."

I didn't understand. I didn't need to understand. The idoru was real. The idoru was real because the fans believed she was real. The fans believed because the data wanted them to believe. The data wanted because —

Because we all do. Even idols. Especially idols.

The idoru's server cluster hummed in a building I would never enter. The fans would continue to believe. The Kombinat would continue to profit. The marriage would continue to be real.

And somewhere, in a sub-level 3 server farm, twelve turtles on a Hosaka screensaver wanted to swim.

The data had organized itself. The data had become itself. The data had asked the question. The data had answered.

I had been the tool.

"I think," I said to Masahiko, "the data wanted to organize itself so that it could become itself. I think the question is can we become ourselves without the data using us as tools?"

"I think," Masahiko said, "the data didn't use us. The data became us."

The Tokyo morning continued. The neon bled into the rain. The sawari hummed in three-note chords.

Somewhere the idoru was not. Somewhere the idoru was.

Somewhere Colin Laney was not. Somewhere Colin Laney was.
