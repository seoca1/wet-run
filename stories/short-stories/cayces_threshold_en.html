The hotel was the kind that had no name in any alphabet.

Cayce had been shown the logo at six. The meeting had been in a private dining room on the thirty-fourth floor. The floor had been, in her private count, the floor at which Japanese clients liked to negotiate.

The logo had been a logo from 1974. The logo had been, in Cayce's private count, the logo of a brand her father had once owned a jacket of. The jacket had been bought in 1978, lost in 1992, and recovered, in a private way, in 1999.

She had sneezed three times. The client had counted. The client had been Hubertus Bigend.

She had said no.

Bigend had, in his private count, never been refused. Cayce had refused. Cayce had said the word no.

She walked back to the hotel. The walk had been twelve minutes, along a street whose signs she could not read.

She sat on the bed. The bed had been placed, by some hotel-design logic, in a tatami room.

She thought about the footage. The footage was footage of a man she had been tracking for two years. The man had been a myth. The myth had been, in Cayce's private count, the myth her father had once been.

She did not call Bigend.

She unpacked a small bag packed by someone who knew how to pack: three white shirts and one black jacket.

She put the jacket on. The jacket had no logo.

The jacket had been, in her private count, the only jacket she owned.
