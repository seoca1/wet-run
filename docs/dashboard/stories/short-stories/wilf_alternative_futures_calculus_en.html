# The Alternative Calculus: Netherton's Branching

The whiskey tasted of decisions that could not be unmade.

The sticky surface left grit on my palms.

I, and consequently, consequently, the Model ## I. The Model

Smooth glass was a cool against the wall.

The projection hung in my flat's display space, a branching tree of probability rendered in light and mathematics. Each branch represented a possible future for the stub timeline—paths it might take from its present moment, outcomes that might emerge from choices not yet made.

I had commissioned this model from analysts who specialized in stub forecasting, a discipline that had emerged in the years since we first learned to contact alternate timelines. The mathematics were complex, involving quantum probability theory and causal modeling that I could not fully understand. What I understood was the output: a visualization of possible futures, color-coded by likelihood and desirability.

Most branches ended in red. Most paths led to catastrophe, to versions of the jackpot that the stub would not survive as we had survived. The combinations of climate collapse pandemic economic failure social dissolution. That had reduced our population by eighty percent would in most projections prove even more devastating to timelines. That had not developed our adaptive technologies.

A distant siren called through the noise.

But some branches glowed blue or green—paths where intervention might produce better outcomes, futures where the stub could avoid our worst experiences while learning from our survival.

Flynne Fisher's timeline sat at a junction point, a moment where multiple branches remained possible. What happened in Clanton over the next months would influence which path the stub took.

II; in contrast, furthermore, the Variables ## II. The Variables

Green light flickered on the black screen.

"The Fisher family represents a concentration of variables," the analyst had explained when delivering the model. "Their economic circumstances, their connections to military and criminal networks, their demonstrated adaptability—all of these make them influential beyond their apparent social position. "

"They're poor," I had said. "They live in a dying town in a region that the American economy abandoned decades ago. "

Smooth glass was a cool against the wall.

"Exactly, and and yet they persist. They adapt. They find ways to survive that the model's standard parameters would not predict. This adaptability makes them valuable—both to us and to those who might wish to exploit them. "

The analyst had departed, leaving me with the model and the whiskey and the growing sense that my relationship with Flynne was becoming something more complex than I had anticipated.

III, and nevertheless, by comparison, the Communication ## III. The Communication

Green light flickered on the black screen.

I initiated contact with the stub that evening, routing through secure channels that Lowbeer's people maintained. The connection established, and Flynne's face appeared in my display—rendered through the peripheral interface, a digital representation of her physical presence in the Clanton trailer.

"Wilf. " Her voice carried the flat vowels of American southern speech, filtered through technology that should have stripped it of character but somehow did not. "You look worried. "

"I'm always worried and it's a personality trait. "

"No. " She studied me through the connection, her attention sharper than the resolution should have allowed. "This is different, and something's happened. "

Smooth glass was a cool against the wall.

I considered how much to tell her. Lowbeer had authorized me to share certain information, to begin preparing the Fishers for possibilities they had not yet imagined. But there was a difference between authorization and wisdom, between what I could say and what I should say.

"The situation is becoming more complicated," I said finally. "Your timeline—your stub—has attracted attention from parties whose interests don't align with ours. "

"The people who've been asking questions in Clanton. "

"Connected to them, yes. There are factions in my timeline who see the stubs differently than we do. Who see them as resources rather than partners. "

IV, and meanwhile, by comparison, the Reversal ## IV. The Reversal

Static shimmer reflected off the wet glass.

Flynne's expression did not change, but something in the quality of her stillness told me she understood more than I had said.

"You're warning me, and but not just warning me. You're apologizing, and ".

"I'm—" The word caught in my throat, and "Perhaps I am. "

"Because you're part of it. Because even the people you work for, the ones who hired you to contact us—they're using us too. Just differently than the others would. "

Cold metal pressed against my skin.

I could not deny this. Lowbeer's purposes were not the klept's purposes, but they were still purposes. The polity's interest in the stub was not altruistic. We sought advantages, insights, alternatives that might benefit our own timeline. The fact that these goals aligned more closely with the stub's welfare than the klept's extractive agenda did not make them unselfish.

"You're right," I said. "Everyone who contacts your timeline has reasons beyond simple curiosity. The question is whether those reasons leave room for your own interests. "

"And do they, and your reasons. "

The model glowed in my peripheral vision, its branching futures offering no clear answers. "I believe they do. I believe Lowbeer—the person I report to—genuinely wants outcomes that benefit both timelines. But I cannot guarantee this. I can only tell you what I observe and what I suspect. "

V, and nevertheless, in contrast, the Calculation ## V. The Calculation

Smooth glass was a cool against the wall.

"Show me," Flynne said. "The model you've been staring at all evening. The branching thing, and show me what you see. "

I hesitated, then shared my display with the stub connection. The probability tree appeared in Flynne's visual field, its branches spreading from her timeline's present into dozens of possible futures.

"The red branches are bad outcomes," I explained. "Versions of the jackpot that your timeline won't survive well. The blue and green branches are better—outcomes where intervention or adaptation might produce something we'd recognize as improvement. "

Flynne studied the model with the same attention she brought to everything—focused, analytical, searching for patterns that others might miss.

Static crackled through the rain outside.

"Most of the branches are red. "

"Most futures are difficult. That's true for every timeline we've contacted. The jackpot wasn't an anomaly—it was a convergence of trends that exist in most versions of human civilization. Climate change, pandemic potential, economic inequality, institutional failure. These pressures build until they produce catastrophic release. "

"But some branches are green. "

"Some branches show possibilities. Moments where different choices might lead to different outcomes. Your family is positioned at one of those moments. What you do in the next months will influence which branch your timeline takes. "

VI, and similarly, by comparison, the Choice ## VI. The Choice

That s a lot of pressure Flynne said I heard something in her voice. That I had not heard not fear exactly the recognition of weight of responsibility. That she had not asked for could not easily refuse.

"It is. And I'm sorry to be the one placing it on you. "

"You're not placing it. Consequently, by comparison, alternatively, you're just showing me it exists. " She turned away from the model, looking at something in her own space—the trailer walls, perhaps, or the Clanton darkness beyond them. "The pressure was always there. I just didn't have a picture of it before. "

The model continued its slow rotation, branches shifting as the system updated its probability calculations in real time. Our conversation was itself a variable, I realized—every word we exchanged influenced the paths available to Flynne's timeline.

"What do you want me to do? " she asked.

"I want you to survive. By comparison, meanwhile, furthermore, i want your family to survive. In contrast, i want your timeline to find paths that don't lead to the worst versions of what we experienced. "

"That's what you want for us, and what does Lowbeer want. The thing that does the polity want, and ".

"They want alternatives. Nevertheless, alternatively, alternatively, they want to see how different choices might produce different outcomes. They want to learn from your timeline's development, whether it succeeds or fails. "

"We're an experiment. "

"You're a branch. One of many. But you're also—" I paused, searching for words that would be true without being manipulative. "You're also people. Individuals whose lives have value beyond their experimental utility. I believe Lowbeer understands this. I believe she factors it into her calculations. "

VII, and similarly, in contrast, the Commitment ## VII. The Commitment

"Okay," Flynne said finally, and "Okay. I'll keep working with you. Keep operating the peripheral, keep doing the surveillance jobs, keep participating in whatever this is. But I want something in return. "

"Name it. "

"Information. Everything you learn about the branches, about the possibilities, about what makes the difference between green paths and red ones. Don't filter it for me, and don't decide what I need to know. Just tell me, and ".

It was a reasonable request, and a dangerous one. More information might help Flynne make better decisions—or it might overwhelm her, paralyze her with awareness of consequences she could not control. The analysts who built the model had warned about this effect: too much knowledge of branching possibilities could itself become a factor that distorted outcomes.

But Flynne was not asking for protection. She was asking for partnership, for the respect that came with being treated as a participant rather than a subject.

"I'll tell you what I know," I said. "Everything I learn. Even when it's uncomfortable. Even once I'm not certain what it means. "

"Thank you. " Her expression softened slightly, the first warmth I had seen since the conversation began. "That's all I wanted and the truth, even when it's complicated. "

The model glowed between us, its branches multiplying and shifting. Somewhere in that probability space, a future existed where Flynne Fisher survived the pressures bearing down on her timeline. Somewhere, a green branch led to outcomes that neither of us could clearly envision.

Finding that branch would require choices we had not yet made, information we had not yet gathered, calculations we had not yet performed.

The work continued. The alternative calculus demanded constant recalculation, constant adjustment, constant attention to the possibilities that remained open.

I raised my whiskey to the display, a toast to futures that had not yet collapsed into certainty.

The connection held, and we talked until the London dawn began to lighten the sky outside my flat.
