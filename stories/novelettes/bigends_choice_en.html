Bigend's Choice

1481 wordsBlue Ant Trilogy

"The refusal was the loudest brand of all. Bigend understood this. Bigend had been buying the refusal for twenty years."

— Zero History, the courier's internal monologue

I. The Box

The courier's name wasn't important. Couriers don't have important names. Couriers have what they carry. What I carried was a single Gabriel Hounds box. Black. Latched. The latch was unbranded. The box was unbranded. The courier was unbranded — Muji jacket, Muji cap, no labels anywhere, nothing that would make anyone look twice. Nothing that would make anyone follow.

The box smelled of new leather. Not fresh leather — that would be chrome-tanned, chemical, the smell of a mall. New leather. The smell of leather that had been cured by someone who had cured leather for forty years. The smell was the absence of the smell of a shipping container. The smell was a small cathedral.

I delivered the box to Mori tower. The lobby smelled of marble cleaner and someone else's perfume. The elevators smelled of burned coffee. The forty-seventh floor smelled of nothing. The forty-seventh floor's nothing-smell cost more than my apartment.

Bigend opened the door. Bigend was small. Smaller than the photographs. Bigend's shirt was unbranded — yardage of white cotton that hadn't been bleached. Bigend's watch was unbranded — brushed steel band, no face. Bigend's eyes were unbranded — they looked at me and didn't see a courier. Bigend's eyes looked at the box.

"The Tokyo pair?" Bigend's voice was small. The voice fit the eyes.

"The Tokyo pair." I handed him the box. The box was warm from my hands. The hands had been holding the box for forty-seven minutes on the subway. The hands smelled of the box. "The original buyer gave you first refusal."

Bigend took the box. Bigend's hands were unbranded. The hands and the box met in the doorway. The doorway smelled of Bigend's cologne, which was the absence of cologne.

"Thank you." Bigend didn't give a tip. Couriers don't expect tips from Bigend. Bigend expected the courier to be the courier.

I rode the elevator down. The elevator smelled of burned coffee. The lobby smelled of marble cleaner. The street smelled of diesel. My apartment, when I reached it two hours later, smelled of my apartment. The box-smell had left me somewhere around Shibuya station. The box-smell was gone the moment I'd handed the box to Bigend. The handing was the absence of the box-smell. The absence was how couriers know the job is done.

II. The Forge

I didn't see Bigend open the box. Couriers don't see the box opened. Couriers see the box delivered. The box is delivered. The box is no longer ours.

But I heard — three days later, through a friend who worked at the Church's outlet — that Bigend had held the left boot for an hour. The boot was warm. The boot was warm from Bigend's hands or from the box or from the leather's memory of someone who had worn it. The warmth was the warmth of an object that had been worn.

The Tokyo pair — the woman in the Yohji Yamamoto coat — had worn the boot for three years. Three years of Japanese rain. Three years of leather oil. Three years of footsteps on Yamanote line platforms. The boot remembered. The boot remembered the feet. The boot's memory was the warmth Bigend felt when he held it.

The Church's outlet friend told me this in a café in Kichijoji. The café smelled of espresso and old bread. The bread was the bakery two doors down. The espresso was the café's espresso. The espresso was a small Italian object in a Japanese café.

"So Bigend buys the refusal," I said.

"Bigend's bought the refusal for twenty years," the friend said. "The Hounds are the refusal in shoe form. The shoes are Bigend's twenty-year project as a wearable."

"He's been waiting."

"He's been waiting."

III. The Call

The Kombinat's voice came through Bigend's secure line at 03:00. I wasn't there. But the courier network knows what the courier network knows. The Kombinat offered sixty million American. The Kombinat offered Cayce Pollard as a senior consultant. The Kombinat offered unlimited travel. The Kombinat offered everything Bigend had spent twenty years not buying.

"The Kombinat wants the refusal," Bigend said.

"The Kombinat wants Cayce," the Kombinat's voice said.

"The Kombinat wants both. The refusal is Cayce now. Cayce is the refusal. Cayce has been the refusal since 1989. Bigend has been buying the refusal. The refusal is what Bigend has been buying."

"The refusal," the Kombinat said, "is the loudest brand of all. We're offering the brand."

The call ended at 04:30. The call was an hour and a half. Bigend didn't move. Bigend held the left boot for another hour. The leather didn't cool.

IV. The Witness

Bigend called Cayce. Cayce was in Soho. Cayce's apartment smelled of dust and old paper and cold leather. Cayce's phone sounded like distance.

"I bought the Hounds' Tokyo pair." Bigend's voice was the voice of a man holding a boot. "I bought the refusal."

"And?" Cayce's voice was small. Cayce's voice was always small.

"I'm giving the boots to you."

The line went silent. The silence was the silence of someone trying to absorb a small explosive. The silence lasted forty seconds. Then:

"The boots?"

"The boots." Bigend set the boot on his desk. The desk was unbranded. The boot was warm. "From one refusal to another. From the buyer of refusal to the wearer of refusal. You can't wear them. Your skin wouldn't tolerate the material. But you can hold them. You can know they're yours."

"The boots are yours," Cayce said. "Bigend bought them. The boots are Bigend's."

"The boots are refusal's." Bigend's voice was the voice of a man who had spent twenty years buying something he didn't know how to wear. "The refusal has been looking for someone to wear it. The wearer has been you. The wearer has been Cayce Pollard. The wearer has been Cayce Pollard since 1989."

"I'm the wearer."

"You're the witness. Witnesses don't wear the refusal. Witnesses carry the refusal. Carrying is different from wearing. Carrying is what you do when you've been hired to deliver something and you've decided not to deliver it."

"You're the courier of refusal."

"I'm the courier of everything." Bigend smiled. The smile was the refusal. The smile was the loudest brand of all. "I'm the courier who has been delivering refusal for twenty years. I'm the courier who is delivering refusal to Cayce. The refusal is yours now. Yours to refuse. Yours to carry."

V. After

Cayce came to the Mori tower at 10:00. The lobby smelled of marble cleaner. The elevators smelled of burned coffee. The forty-seventh floor smelled of nothing.

Bigend had put the boots back in the box. The box was open. The boots were on black velvet. Raw leather. Hand-stitched.

Cayce held the left boot. The leather was warm. The leather was alive. The stitching was human-perfect — the kind of perfect that carried the ghost of the maker's breath. The breath was somewhere in Hokkaido. The breath had been breathing for forty years.

"No logo," Cayce said.

"No logo." Bigend set the right boot on the velvet. "The Hounds are the logo. The wearer is the brand. The refusal is the product."

"The wearer is a woman in a Yohji Yamamoto coat who took the train every morning in Tokyo."

"The wearer is now. The wearer is whoever chooses the boots. The boots have chosen you. The boots have chosen you by surviving the courier system, by surviving Bigend, by surviving the trip from Tokyo to Soho to Mori tower."

Cayce set the boot down. The leather cooled. The stitching held. The ghost of the maker's breath was somewhere in Hokkaido. The breath was cold now. The breath was always cold. The breath was what Bigend had bought for sixty million American. The breath was the refusal.

"Bigend."

"Yes, Cayce."

"I'm keeping the refusal."

"I know you are."
