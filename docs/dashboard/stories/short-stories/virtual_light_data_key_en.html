Virtual Light Data Key

1134 wordsBridge Trilogy

"The glasses showed you what was there. The data key showed you what wasn't."

— Virtual Light, Berry Rydell's internal monologue

Introduction

The sunglasses sat on the dashboard of the patrol car. Cheap frames. Dark lenses. The kind you bought at a gas station for twelve dollars and threw away when they scratched.

Berry Rydell knew better. He'd seen what the glasses did. He'd seen the data key slide into the slot at the temple, the way the lenses went opaque, the way the world rewrote itself.

Virtual light. The city that wasn't there. The buildings that had never been built. The streets that existed only in the geometry of a corporate server farm in Oregon.

The patrol car's engine idled. The San Francisco night pressed against the windshield — fog, sodium vapor, the smell of salt and exhaust. Rydell's partner, a woman named Guerra who'd been on the force twelve years and hated every minute of it, slept in the passenger seat. Her mouth open. A strand of dark hair across her cheek.

Rydell didn't sleep much. Not since the academy. Not since the thing in the evidence room — the sunglasses, the data key, the way the virtual city had bloomed around him like a flower made of light.

He picked up the glasses. The frames were warm from the dashboard. The data key was in his pocket, a slim black rectangle the size of a stick of gum. Ono-Sendai. Prototype. The kind of thing that didn't exist in any catalog.

Rising Action

The call came at 02:47. Domestic disturbance. A building on the Embarcadero — one of the new condo towers, the kind where the doorman knew your net worth before you stepped out of the elevator.

Rydell woke Guerra. She swore, wiped her mouth, checked her sidearm. Professional. Tired. Human.

The condo tower's lobby was all glass and polished stone. The doorman — a man in a uniform that cost more than Rydell's monthly salary — looked at them with the particular contempt of someone who knew the police were a service industry.

"Unit 47C." The doorman's voice was smooth. "The Washington woman. Again."

Rydell's fingers tightened on the data key in his pocket. Washington. Chevette Washington.

He'd read the file. Messenger. Bike courier. The kind of job that didn't exist in the virtual city — the virtual city didn't need messengers, it was the message. But the real city still needed people to carry things from here to there. Physical things. Things that couldn't be transmitted.

The elevator ride was silent. Guerra checked her reflection in the mirrored walls. Rydell watched the floor numbers climb. 12. 18. 24. 31. 47.

The door opened on a hallway that smelled of expensive cleaning products and something underneath — something old. Dust. Time. The things money couldn't clean.

Unit 47C. The door was ajar.

Climax

Inside: chaos. Furniture overturned. Glass shattered. A lamp smashed against the wall, its shade a torn drum of light.

And in the center of it, Chevette Washington. Small. Compact. Her messenger bag on the floor, contents spilled — a vintage Nokia, a folded map, a single .38 round.

She wasn't crying. She was looking. Her eyes moved across the wreckage like she was reading code.

"They came for the key." Her voice was flat. "They didn't find it. They won't."

Rydell's hand closed around the data key in his pocket. The Ono-Sendai prototype. The thing that didn't exist.

"What key?" Guerra asked. Professional. Tired. Human. But her hand was on her sidearm.

Chevette's eyes found Rydell. She knew. She knew.

"The Virtual Light data key." She said it like she was reading a menu. "The one Yamazaki's sociological study tracked. The one the Cops in the evidence room lost. The one that shows you the city that isn't there — the city they're building while we sleep."

Rydell pulled the key from his pocket. The black rectangle caught the hallway's soft light.

"They're building a city on top of this one." Chevette stepped closer. Her messenger bag's strap had caught on a shard of glass. "A virtual city. For the people who can afford to live in it. The rest of us — we get the scaffolding."

"Chevette." Rydell's voice was quiet. "You stole evidence."

"I borrowed the future." Her smile was sharp. "The data key doesn't just show you the virtual city. It shows you the difference. The delta between what is and what they're planning. Every condemned building. Every displaced neighborhood. Every bridge they'll sell to a corporation while the squatters are still sleeping on it."

She held out her hand. "Give it to me. I know where it goes. I know who needs to see it."

Guerra's hand tightened on her sidearm. "Ma'am, you're under arrest for theft of police evidence, breaking and entering —"

"Guerra." Rydell didn't look at his partner. "The data key shows the virtual city. The real city is what happens when people decide the virtual one isn't enough."

He placed the key in Chevette's palm.

Resolution

The patrol car drove away from the Embarcadero. Guerra drove. Rydell sat in the passenger seat, the sunglasses back on the dashboard.

"You just gave her stolen evidence." Guerra's voice was flat. "Rydell, that's your career. That's your pension. That's —"

"Guerra." Rydell watched the Bay Bridge appear through the fog. The real bridge. The one with the squatters and the improvised wiring and the daimon. "The virtual city is what they sell. The real city is what we protect."

"Since when do you talk like a philosopher?"

"Since I saw what the data key shows." Rydell's fingers found the .38 round in his messenger bag. The one he'd carried since the night he met Chevette. "The virtual city has no bridges. Only connections. The real city — the real city is the bridges."

The fog burned off. The morning light caught the cables. The bridge sang.

Rydell closed his eyes. Behind his eyelids, the virtual city bloomed — and then the real city bloomed through it, messy and improvised and alive.

The data key was gone. The future was borrowed.

And somewhere, Chevette Washington was riding her bike through the delta, carrying the difference.
