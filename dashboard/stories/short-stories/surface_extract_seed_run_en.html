The Seed Had Been a Run

The seed run was in a vault under what had been a tobacco barn in what had been North Carolina. The vault's door was the kind that the Finn had not seen since before the Sprawl — a real door, with a real key, and behind the door a real cold that the matrix did not provide.

The Finn had been sent by the Fukuoka mediator. The mediator had said: extract the seed run. The lineage is older than the Sprawl. The lineage is the only one of its kind. The mediator had not said what the lineage was for. The mediator had not needed to.

The seed had been a run for forty years. The run had been a seed. The woman who opened the vault was named, the Finn thought, something his grandmother would have known — a name from the pre-Sprawl south, when names meant the family you came from and the land you worked.

She was not a construct. The Finn checked, the way he always checked, with the coat-pocket scanner. She was not a construct. She was a person, of the kind the surface still made.

"You came for the seeds," she said.

"The mediator sent me."

"The mediator," she said, "is a construct." She said it the way the surface says things, which is to say without malice. "I am not a construct. I am the seeds' keeper. They have been in my family for nine generations."

The Finn looked at the vault. The cold was coming from a refrigeration unit that was older than the Finn, that had been running since before the Fukuoka, that would run, the woman said, until the seeds no longer needed to be cold.

"The seeds," the Finn said. "What are they?"

"Maize," she said. "Bean. Squash. The three sisters. The seeds that the surface remembers."

The Finn did not know what the three sisters were. He knew that the matrix had a file format called `three-sisters.maize` which the corporate agronomists used to bulk-engineer seed-stock for the orbital colonies. He had not known the format had a name from the surface.

"I am supposed," the Finn said carefully, "to extract the seed run."

"To where?"

"To the Fukuoka archive. Where the seeds will be safe."

The woman looked at him for a long time. The vault hummed.

"Safe," she said. "The seeds are safe here."

"The matrix—"

"The matrix," she said, "is what ate the seeds the first time."

The Finn sat down. He had been on the surface for two days. He had not eaten. He had not slept. The cold was, he understood now, the kind of cold that the surface used to keep things — not just seeds, but lineages, but memories, but the things that the matrix could not yet liquidate.

He thought about the mediator. He thought about the Fukuoka archive. He thought about the cold-storage nodes that the zaibatsu had been building all across the surface, the cold-storage nodes that were not, he understood now, about data.

"Maize," he said. "Bean. Squash."

"And the memory of how to plant them," she said.

He did not file a report. He drove back to Fukuoka with the seeds in the trunk of the surface-mail truck. He did not take them to the archive. He took them to the recycling baseline — the baseline the foreman had tried to reclaim, the baseline the workers had defended. The workers had a garden. They had, the Finn understood, been waiting for the seeds.

The seed had been a run. The run had been a seed. The Finn thought, as the workers planted the first row in soil that had not been planted in for forty years, that the substrate was the only thing the matrix had not yet learned to liquidate, and that the substrate had just learned to plant itself.

He drove back to Fukuoka at dusk. The mediator would not be pleased. The mediator was a construct. The Finn did not care.

The seed had been a run. The run was now a seed.
