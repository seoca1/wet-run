The Baseline Had Been a Recycling

The Finn was not a soldier. He had been, briefly, in a war that was now classified under three different names depending on who was buying the records. But defending a recycling baseline was not soldiering. It was, in the Finn's own private vocabulary, substrate.

He arrived at the baseline at the same time the zaibatsu reclamation crew did: dawn, off the same spur, in a convoy of seven flatbeds. The crew was Fukuoka-Mediabank. The Finn knew this from the plate numbers, which were the same plate numbers that had run the Memphis-Shelby ice-line in the early nineties, before the Sprawl had eaten Memphis.

The baseline had been a recycling for forty years. The recycling had been a baseline. The crew's foreman — a thin man in a Mediabank coverall with the zaibatsu lightning-bolt stitched over the breast — climbed down from the lead flatbed and looked at the Finn.

"Finn," the foreman said.

"Finn," the Finn said.

"We have the reclamation order." The foreman held up a tablet. The screen glowed in the dawn light. "Cold-storage node. Twelve thousand units. The substrate is adequate."

The Finn looked at the baseline. It was, he had to admit, adequate. Pre-Sprawl construction. Cinder-block. A loading dock that had once handled — what? He didn't remember. Memory on the surface was something that the surface itself did, not something that the people on the surface did.

"The workers," the Finn said.

"Reassignable."

"To where?"

"To Fukuoka." The foreman said it as if Fukuoka were a destination, not a deportation. "The work there is cleaner."

The Finn sat down on the loading dock. He had his coat-pocket scanner running but he didn't need it. He knew what the baseline was. He knew what the workers on it were.

"No," he said.

"Finn—"

"No."

The foreman made a small sound, the sound of a man whose zaibatsu had given him an order and whose zaibatsu did not understand the word no.

Then the doors of the baseline opened. The workers came out. They were not many. They were not young. They were the kind of workers the Sprawl had not yet fully replaced: women and men who could still tell the difference between a recycler and a refrigerator, who could still fix the kind of hardware that ran on the kind of electricity that the surface still had.

They stood behind the Finn. They did not say anything.

The baseline had been a recycling. The recycling had been a baseline. The recycling was, the Finn thought, what the matrix had forgotten. The matrix had not learned to remember the recycling, because the recycling was not a thing the matrix could profit from. The recycling was, instead, the substrate of the matrix — the place the matrix's servers got their cases from, the place the matrix's operators ate their lunches in, the place the matrix's viruses wrote themselves on.

The Finn stood up.

"Twelve thousand units," he said. "For what?"

"For cold storage," the foreman said.

"Cold storage," the Finn said. "The substrate is already cold."

The foreman did not understand. The workers did. They had been cold storage for forty years, and they had made themselves into something that did not freeze.

The zaibatsu left. The baseline remained. The recycling continued.

The Finn drove back to Fukuoka at noon. He did not file a report. He had not been paid, and the baseline was not his business. But he had been hungry, and the workers had given him a sandwich, and the sandwich had been the best sandwich he had eaten in years, because it had been made by people who knew that sandwiches are made.

The baseline had been a recycling. The recycling would remain a baseline. The Finn thought, as he drove, that the substrate was the only thing the matrix had not yet learned to liquidate.
