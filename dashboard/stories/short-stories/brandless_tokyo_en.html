Brandless in Tokyo

1923 wordsBlue Ant Trilogy

"The objects that tell the truth are the ones without brand. Everything else is screaming to be sold."

— Zero History, Cayce Pollard's internal monologue

Introduction

The Muji store in Yurakucho smelled of plain wood and unscented soap. The wood smelled like the lumber section of a hardware store — clean pine, fresh-cut, the smell of the tree before it knew it would be a shelf. The soap smelled of nothing. The soap's nothing-smell was its product.

Cayce Pollard stood in the apparel section. Her fingers hovered over a rack of cotton shirts. No labels. No logos. No tags to make her skin crawl. The shirts were cotton. The cotton was warm. The warmth of the cotton was a warmth her fingers could feel — the warmth of a thing that had been made by hands that didn't know what branding was for.

The store's wooden shelves held 4,741 items. She'd counted once. The number came back to her every time she needed a clean surface. Muji was clean. Muji was brandless. Muji was the only place in Tokyo where Cayce could touch things without nausea.

Muji was originally a Seiyu brand. Seiyu was a Japanese supermarket chain. Muji was launched in 1980 as a joint venture with Sogo. Muji was designed without brand to sell to people who wanted less. Muji sold 4,741 items without packaging. The items were the packaging. The packaging was the items. The knowing of the items was the cost of the items.

Cayce's phone vibrated. The vibration was a small metal pulse against her palm. The metal was cold. The cold was the cold of the subway platform she'd been standing on.

The text read:

Bigend's office. Now. Tokyo.

She didn't reply. She bought three shirts. No bag. She folded them into her messenger bag — a vintage Filson, waxed canvas, no synthetic fibers, no obvious logo. Inside, alongside her Rick Owens jacket and her Gabriel Hounds tag, the shirts lived without labels.

Rising Action

The Mori tower elevator smelled of burned coffee and someone else's perfume. The coffee had burned three hours ago. The perfume was from a stranger who had ridden up at the thirty-eighth floor. The stranger had ridden down at the thirty-eighth floor. The perfume remained.

Bigend's office was on the forty-seventh floor. The interior was all brushed steel, pale wood, and the kind of soft lighting that made everyone look like they'd been filtered. Cayce's sunglasses stayed on. The sunglasses were unbranded. The lenses were dark. The dark was the dark Cayce needed to see Bigend clearly.

"Ms. Pollard." Bigend didn't rise. He never rose for anyone. The chair behind him was empty. The chair in front of him was for her. The chair was leather. The leather was unbranded. "You're looking well. The Tokyo does suit you."

"Tokyo suits no one." Cayce sat. The chair was warm. The chair had been warm for three hours. The chair had been waiting. "What do you want, Hubertus?"

"The brandless thing." His smile was thin. The thinness of the smile was the thinness of the brushed-steel desk. "You've been studying it. Three weeks. Muji. Comme des Garçons. The Hounds. The whole no-logo movement in Japanese fashion."

"I've been buying shirts."

"You've been studying the shift in market share." Bigend opened a folder. The folder was unbranded. Of course it was. "Brandless apparel grew four hundred percent in the last fiscal year. The buyers are the same buyers who used to buy logos. The shift isn't about aesthetics. It's about anti-aesthetics. The brands are dying because consumers want what doesn't shout."

Cayce's sunglasses stayed on. Her skin wasn't crawling. The skin-absence was the absence of a thread on a tag. Bigend was the only person in the world she could sit across from without her hand going to her neck.

"The buyers want what doesn't shout."

"The buyers want what doesn't lie." Bigend closed the folder. The folder's closing was the closing of a small steel drawer. "The big brands are panicking. They're paying me to tell them what comes next. I need a forecast by Friday. You have ten thousand hours of footage of Cayce Pollard trying on unbranded clothes. You are the forecast."

Cayce stood. The chair groaned — soft, expensive wood. The wood groaned the way wood groans when it has been told to be silent for three hours.

"You don't need a forecast. You need a witness." She walked to the window. The window was unbranded glass. The glass smelled of the cleaner the Mori tower cleaners used once a week. "The Hounds. Muji. Comme des Garçons. They're not anti-aesthetic. They're aesthetic of refusal. The brands are dying because consumers want to refuse what they used to choose."

"The refusal is the new brand."

"The refusal is the new refusal." Cayce turned. The turn was the turn of someone who had been studying refusal for three weeks in Tokyo. "You can't sell refusal. You can only be it. The being is the cost."

Climax

Bigend's smile widened. It was the smile of a man who'd just heard what he wanted to hear — and what he didn't want to hear, both at once. The smile widened the way a Tokyo street widens into a courtyard.

"You're telling me the market for refusal is the absence of market. You're telling me my forecast for the next fiscal year is don't forecast." His fingers drummed the desk. The desk was unbranded. The drumming was the small sound of a man testing whether the absence had a sound. "I'm paying you six figures for don't forecast?"

"I'm free." Cayce walked to the door. The door was unbranded. The walking was the walking of someone who had been in Tokyo for three weeks and had stopped counting days. "And I'm leaving."

"Ms. Pollard." Bigend's voice stopped her. The voice was a knife wrapped in a velvet sleeve. "The Hounds' Tokyo pair. We know who she is. We know where she stays. We know where she buys her coffee. The coffee is from a small shop in Daikanyama. The shop has no sign. We know which train line she takes each morning. The train is the Chiyoda line. We know she doesn't belong to a network. She doesn't belong to a brand. She belongs to herself."

Cayce turned. Her sunglasses stayed on. The lenses were dark. The dark was the dark of someone who had heard Bigend threaten a Hounds wearer.

"You're threatening a Hounds wearer."

"I'm reminding her." Bigend's smile was gone. The smile's absence was the absence of a small steel drawer. "The Hounds' Tokyo pair has standards. The standards don't scale. But the standards — the standards can be acquired. The standards can be applied. The standards can be sold to someone who wants to be told they're wearing the right thing."

"The standards aren't for sale."

"Everything is for sale, Cayce." Bigend rose from his chair. The chair didn't groan. The chair was silent. The chair was the kind of chair that knew its silence was expensive. "The Hounds chose her because she refused. You refused my offer. You're still refusing. You're wearing unbranded shirts in an unbranded store. You're refusing to be acquired."

He stepped closer. His shoes were Church's — old shoes, pre-buyout, when Church's still meant something. The leather creaked. The creak was the creak of a man who had spent twenty years buying refusal.

"But the refusal is so loud, Ms. Pollard. The refusal is its own brand. You're wearing not wearing as a uniform. The refusal is loudest when everyone knows you're refusing."

Cayce's skin didn't crawl. Bigend was right. Bigend was always right. The refusal was loud. The loud was the small sound of a courier's jacket on a subway platform.

"Your forecast," she said, "is that the refusal will be sold to consumers who want to refuse. The brands will sell the refusal. The brands will rebrand the refusal. The refusal will become the loudest brand of all."

"And you'll be wearing it."

"I'll be refusing to wear it."

Resolution

Cayce left the Mori tower. The Tokyo morning was gray. The gray was the gray of a city that had been neon the night before. The neon had burned itself out. The shoppers were emerging — the salarymen in suits, the schoolgirls in sailor uniforms, the brands screaming in every direction. The screaming was the small screaming of logos on umbrellas. The umbrellas were open. The umbrellas smelled of wet canvas. The canvas was the canvas of strangers' lives.

Her messenger bag was lighter than when she'd entered. The unbranded shirts sat inside, folded, silent. They weren't branded. They weren't refusing. They were just shirts. The just-shirt was the absence of brand. The absence was the loudest thing Cayce had ever worn.

Bigend was wrong about one thing. The refusal wasn't a brand. The refusal was a posture. A way of holding the body. A way of looking at the world without flinching from the logos that wanted to make her flinch. The flinching was the small smell of someone's perfume in a Mori tower elevator.

The refusal was the absence of brand. The absence of brand wasn't the brand. The absence of brand was the silence. The silence was the loudest thing she could wear. The silence weighed what cotton weighed. The cotton was the cotton of Muji's 4,741 items.

Cayce walked past a Muji store. She didn't go in. The store was inside her. The 4,741 items were inside her. The unbranded shirts, the unwritten labels, the unscented soap, the wooden shelves held every silence she'd ever wanted. The silence was a warmth the size of a Muji store.

Bigend could sell the refusal. The brands could rebrand the refusal. The consumers could buy the refusal. The buying was the absence of refusal. The absence of refusal was refusal. The refusal was a circle. The circle was the absence of a brand.

Cayce would refuse to buy the refusal. Cayce would refuse to refuse to buy the refusal. Cayce would simply exist, brandless, in a brandless world that didn't want her to be.

The subway entrance swallowed her. The Tokyo morning continued. The brands screamed. Cayce walked among them without flinching. The not-flinching was the smell of wet canvas on a stranger's umbrella. The umbrella was unbranded. The umbrella was Cayce's.

Somewhere the Hounds' Tokyo pair was walking too — wearing her scuffed boots, her Yamamoto coat, her unbranded self — and the refusal was loud in both of them. The loud was the absence of the loud.

Somewhere Cayce was not. Somewhere Cayce was.
