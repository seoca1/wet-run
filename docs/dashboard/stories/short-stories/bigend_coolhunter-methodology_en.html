Bigend: Coolhunter Methodology

945 wordsBlue Ant Trilogy

Hook

"Bigend did not invent coolhunting. Bigend perfected it. The distinction mattered."

Hubertus Bigend sat in his office in Notting Hill, looking at the wall that wasn't a wall but was a screen, and waited for his next-coolhunter to bring him the next-piece-of-the-future.

Blue Ant had been a coolhunting-firm for nine years. Before that, it had been a marketing-firm. Before that, Bigend had been a junior-executive at a firm whose name he had long-since stopped saying. The transformation from marketing-firm to coolhunting-firm had not been Bigend's idea originally. The transformation had been the market's idea. The market had decided, sometime around 1995, that it no longer wanted to be told what it wanted. The market wanted to be told what it was about to want. The market wanted a prophet, not a salesman.

Bigend had been a prophet. Bigend had always been a prophet. He had just needed the right firm to prophesy through.

The screen on his wall showed him a piece of footage from Moscow. The footage was a girl walking across a plaza. Bigend had seen this footage a thousand times. He had seen this footage in slow-motion, in fast-forward, in reverse, in every-direction. He had seen this footage and tried to figure out what it was about.

Rising Action

He had not figured it out. He suspected he would never figure it out. The footage was not the kind of thing that could be figured out. The footage was a substrate-emergence, and substrate-emergences were not figured out; they were waited-out.

Bigend was good at waiting. Bigend was the best waiter he knew.

The door opened. Cayce Pollard came in. She was wearing her usual-allergic-wardrobe, the kind of wardrobe that Bigend had specifically-requested for this-job. The wardrobe that avoided all the corporate-logos, all the brand-signs, all the markers that Cayce's body-substrate could not tolerate. The wardrobe that was a kind of armor against the brand-substrate that Bigend himself was so good at exploiting.

"Mr. Bigend," she said.

"Cayce. Sit. Tell me about Moscow."

She sat. She told him about Moscow. She told him about Voytek's-people. She told him about the cold. She told him about the faces of the people she had met. She told him about the piece of footage that she had found, the piece that might have been the source of the original-footage or might have been another-piece of footage entirely.

Midpoint Reversal

Bigend listened. He did not interrupt. He had been told, early in his career, that the best way to get information was to let the person talking believe that you were interested. He had been told this by a man whose name he no longer remembered. The man had been wrong. The best way to get information was to be interested. The interest itself was the methodology.

Cayce was interested, in her own allergic way. Cayce was interested in things that made her sick. Cayce was interested in patterns that emerged from the brand-substrate and then disappeared back into it. Cayce was the perfect-coolhunter for a world that no longer wanted to be sold to, but that still wanted to be understood.

"The footage," Bigend said, when Cayce had finished. "Did you find what you were looking for?"

"No."

"Did you find anything you weren't looking for?"

"Maybe."

Climax

"What was it?"

"I don't know yet. I think I need to watch it a few more times before I can say."

Bigend nodded. This was the right answer. This was the only answer. Cayce understood, instinctively, that the footage was not a thing with a destination. The footage was a substrate-emergence, and substrate-emergences were not arrived-at; they were stayed-in.

"Take the rest of the week," Bigend said. "Watch the footage. Watch it in your apartment. Watch it on the train. Watch it in cafés. Watch it until you can see what it is without thinking about what it is."

"What if I can't see what it is?"

"Then we'll find someone else who can."

Resolution

"And if no one can see what it is?"

"Then the footage is not a substrate-emergence. Then it's just footage. Then we move on."

Cayce nodded. She stood up. She walked to the door. She paused.

"Mr. Bigend."

"Yes."

"Why me? Why am I the coolhunter for this?"

"Because you can see what makes you sick. Because you can see what the brand-substrate is doing before anyone else can see it. Because you are allergic to the thing that everyone else is addicted to. Because your body is a pattern-recognition-machine that nobody else has."

Cayce looked at him for a long moment. Then she left.

Bigend watched the door close. Then he went back to looking at the wall-that-wasn't-a-wall, and the footage of the girl walking across the plaza, and waited.

"Coolhunting was not about finding cool. Coolhunting was about finding the thing-before-cool. Bigend's methodology was not about selling. It was about seeing the future before it arrived."
