Gabriel Hounds in Tokyo

1521 wordsBlue Ant Trilogy

"The Hounds weren't a brand. The Hounds were a way of seeing. Bigend didn't understand that. Bigend understood *markets*. He didn't understand *vision*."

— Zero History, the courier's internal monologue

Introduction

The Ginza district at 3:00 AM smelled of rain and money. The rain smelled of oil. The money smelled of leather. I stood beneath the awning of a closed department store, my Muji jacket shedding water — Muji sheds water like waxed canvas, because Muji is waxed canvas in another life — and waited.

The package was small. Black velvet. Latched. The latch was unbranded. The package was unbranded. The courier was unbranded — Muji jacket, Muji cap, hands scarred from eleven years of courier work, no labels anywhere. The labels were the absence.

My phone buzzed. A text, no sender ID.

Tokyo. Harajuku. Cat Street. The Hounds are here. Come alone.

Bigend's syntax. Terse. Imperative. The syntax of a man who bought companies the way other men bought coffee. The text was Bigend's text. The text was the only warm thing I'd touched in hours.

I didn't reply. I hailed a taxi. The driver smelled of cigarette smoke and something floral — air freshener, maybe, or the ghost of his wife's perfume. He didn't ask where. He just drove. The taxi smelled of cigarettes the way the cab smelled of cigarettes in 1990.

Come alone meant don't escape. The Hounds were Bigend's latest acquisition target. The Hounds were the trap. The trap had been set in motion when Bigend had received the first pair from London. The trap was a year old. The trap was the trip.

Rising Action

Cat Street was a narrow artery between Harajuku and Shibuya. By day: vintage shops, sneaker boutiques, the careful curation of youth culture. By night: shadows, locked shutters, the occasional late-bar spilling jazz onto wet pavement. The wet pavement smelled of the rain that had fallen at 3:00 AM. The rain still hadn't stopped.

I found the shop at 3:47 AM. No sign. No lights. Just a door with a brass handle worn smooth by decades of hands — the hands of people who had been looking for the Hounds before the Hounds were Hounds. The Hounds' Tokyo showroom. I knew it existed — I'd delivered to it twice before, once in 2007, once in 2009. The Hounds didn't advertise. The Hounds didn't need to advertise. The Hounds were found by the people who needed to find them.

The door opened silently. Inside: darkness shaped by the faint glow of a single display case. Leather boots. Raw denim. A jacket in a fabric that seemed to absorb light. The darkness was the absence of the Tokyo neon outside. The neon was the absence of the Hounds' booth.

A man's voice came from behind the case. Japanese. English. The accent was neither. The voice smelled of green tea and unbranded cotton. The voice smelled like the man standing behind the case.

"Ms. Pollard will be here later. Mr. Bigend sent you in advance." The voice knew I was the courier. Couriers are always the advance.

I set the package on the unbranded counter. The counter was oak. The oak smelled of nothing. Nothing was expensive in this showroom.

"This is the Tokyo pair?" I asked.

"This is the Tokyo pair." The man's fingers hovered over the case, not touching. "Three pairs produced. One in London. One in New York. This one — here. In Tokyo."

"Why Tokyo?"

The man smiled. It was a small smile. The kind that didn't reach the eyes. The smile smelled of exhaustion. The exhaustion was the exhaustion of a man who had made boots by hand for forty years. "Because Tokyo is where the counterfeiters are best. If the Hounds can survive Tokyo, they can survive anywhere."

Climax

The man opened the case. The boots sat on black velvet. Raw leather. Hand-stitched. The sole was a composite I couldn't identify — some material that hadn't been invented when I'd last studied footwear construction. The construction was the smell of horses. The horses were the absence of a tannery.

"This pair," I said, picking up the left boot for the courier's inspection — couriers inspect, that's what we do — "is alive."

The boot was warm. Warmer than boots should be. Warmer than the velvet. Warmer than the showroom. Warmer than the Tokyo rain outside. The warmth was the warmth of something that had been worn.

The man nodded. "The composite remembers every foot that has worn it. The memory is the warmth. The warmth is the ghost."

"The ghost of the maker's breath?"

"The ghost of the wearer's foot. The boots were made by hand. The boots have been worn by hand. The hands have been hands. The hands are what Bigend doesn't understand. Bigend understands markets. Bigend doesn't understand hands."

The darkness behind the case shifted. A second figure emerged. Smaller. Younger. A woman in a vintage Yohji Yamamoto coat, her hair cut in a blunt fringe that framed eyes the color of wet stone. The wet stone was a stone on a Kyoto temple path. The path was the absence of the Tokyo street.

"This pair," the woman said, her English precise and unaccented. The unaccent was the absence of an accent. The accent was the absence of the Courier. "is mine."

She held out her hand. I handed her the boot. Her hand was warm. My hand was warm. The boot was warm. The warmth was the absence of the cold that Bigend had been trying to sell to the cold.

"The Hounds are not for sale," she said. "Bigend believes the Hounds are a brand. The Hounds are a standard. Standards don't scale. Standards endure."

Resolution

I left the showroom. The package had been delivered. The package was unbranded. The package was the absence of the Hounds. The Hounds had stayed with the woman in the Yamamoto coat.

Cat Street smelled of wet pavement and the absence of neon. The neon had come back up. The absence of neon was the absence of a single street between Harajuku and Shibuya. The absence was a small cold in the larger city.

My phone buzzed again. Bigend.

Well?

I typed with my thumbs, the screen's glow reflected in the rain-streaked window.

The Hounds aren't for sale. The Hounds are a standard. Standards don't scale. Standards endure.

The reply came instantly.

Everything scales. Everything has a price. You of all people should know that.

I stared at the screen. The Mitsubishi logo on a salaryman's umbrella flashed in my memory. The Mitsubishi logo was the absence of a price. The salaryman didn't know I was a courier. The salaryman thought I was another salaryman.

Not the Hounds. Not this time.

I pocketed the phone. The taxi turned onto the expressway. Tokyo's neon bled into the night — a virtual city projected onto the real one, the real one projected onto the virtual. The difference was the Hounds. The difference was the standard. The difference was the boots on the woman's feet, scuffed and creased and real.

The difference was the warmth of a boot that remembered.

I closed my eyes. The Gabriel Hounds tag on my jeans was the only brand I could wear. The only brand that didn't make my hands itch. The hands had been carrying things for eleven years. The hands had learned what wasn't a brand.

Brandless objects. The only things that tell the truth.

Somewhere in Tokyo, a woman in Yohji Yamamoto walked the wet streets in boots that had chosen her. Somewhere in London, a cabinet minister's daughter marched in boots that had chosen her. Somewhere in New York, a hedge fund manager's son posed in boots that had chosen him.

Three pairs. Three standards. Zero history.

Bigend would hate it. Bigend would try to buy it. Bigend would fail.

I smiled. The smile was unbranded. The smile was the absence of a smile. The smile was the small cold that couriers carry when couriers have carried too much.

The taxi crossed the Rainbow Bridge. The real bridge. The one with the cables and the traffic and the salt wind. The cables smelled of salt. The salt smelled of Tokyo Bay. The bay smelled of nowhere.

The virtual city faded. The real city remained.
