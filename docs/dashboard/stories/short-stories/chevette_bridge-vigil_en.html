Chevette: Bridge Vigil

970 wordsBridge Trilogy

"The bridge at night was a different country. Day had its tourists, its Lost-Norter day-laborers, its Armenian-cart vendors, its bike-couriers in liquidmesh. Night had Chevette."

The fog came in off the Bay at twenty-three hundred, low-rolling, smelling of salt and rust and the particular-chemical-tang the SF-side refineries leaked at night when nobody was checking scrubbers. Chevette sat on the bridge-deck with her back against a lamppost nobody had lit in three years, her jacket zipped to her chin, her thermos between her knees.

Skinner was asleep three deck-rings west, in the cubby they'd built out of corrugated plastic and salvaged road-signs. She could hear him snoring — that wet-snore that meant his nasal-passages were narrowing from the cold — through the dark.

The bridge at night had sounds she trusted: the wind in the cabling, the distant-rumble of trucks on the I-280 ramp three-quarters-of-a-mile east, the periodic-bang of cooling metal contracting. These were the bridge's own sounds. She'd learned to read them at thirteen, when she'd first started sleeping-out here.

The bridge at night also had sounds she didn't trust. Tonight she was hearing the second category.

A distant-voice, low-male, the cadence of a deal. Then a second voice, higher, that might have been a woman's or a boy's. Then footsteps on the catwalk that ran under the deck at the next-bay west, moving fast.

Chevette uncrossed her ankles. Her hands — she had learned this from Skinner, who had learned it from someone he'd never named — stayed in her jacket pockets, fingers loosely curled around nothing. Empty-hands on the bridge meant honest. Fists meant trouble. Hands-in-the-air meant you were about to die.

She waited. The footsteps stopped. The voices stopped. The bridge creaked.

Then: a small sound, almost-too-small, the sound of glass-on-glass. The tinkle of a bottle being set down. Whoever it was had decided to do business, not run. The deal had been transacted, or was being transacted. Chevette's job — the job she'd inherited from Skinner at fifteen, when he'd been sleeping-off a beat-down from the Black Travelers and she'd covered the deck alone for forty-eight hours — was to make sure the deal didn't turn violent.

She stood up. The thermos clattered against the lamppost. She picked it up and put it in her bag. Then she walked toward the sound, the way she'd been taught: slowly, making-noise, not-startling, her boots loud on the deck-planking.

When she came around the corner of the next-bay, she saw two figures. A man in a long-coat — Black Travelers by the cut of it, that vaguely-midnight-tribe-aesthetic — and a kid, maybe-sixteen, in a hoodie so big the kid's hands were invisible inside the sleeves.

The Travelers-man had a bottle of something clear. The kid had a bag of something Chevette didn't want to think about. Neither of them was holding a weapon that she could see.

"Tuesday," she said. That was the bridge-word for: I see you, I am here, this is my deck.

The Travelers-man nodded. "Tuesday, sister."

The kid just stood there.

Chevette walked past them, on the upwind side, her hands still in her pockets. She stopped three paces beyond, her back to them, and looked out over the rail at the fog. She listened: the soft sound of the bottle being passed, the kid's throat working, the man's lighter clicking.

Good. A deal, not a death.

She stayed there for another minute, until the Travelers-man walked past her, touching his brow with one finger as a kind of salute, and the kid trailed after him. Then they were gone, and the deck was quiet again.

Chevette walked back to her lamppost and sat down. She poured herself another cup from the thermos — black coffee, lukewarm, the kind Skinner drank — and waited for the next sound.

The bridge at night was a different country. But Chevette was its border-patrol. She knew every border, every checkpoint, every rule-of-thumb that wasn't a rule but a habit that had hardened into a rule over fifty-odd years of squatters and refugees and Lost-Norters and the people who fell through the cracks of the city below.

She had learned the borders from Skinner. He had learned them from someone else. He would learn them from Chevette, eventually, when he was old enough — the new-Skinner, whoever that was, the kid or the woman who would inherit the night-shift.

But that was years away. Tonight it was just Chevette, and the fog, and the bridge-creak, and the distant-Red-And-White lights of the Bay Bridge to the north, the lights of a city that had never quite fallen down but had never quite recovered, either, the lights of a place where everyone was always between-something, always between-things, always on a bridge.

The thermos was empty by four AM. Skinner would be awake soon, his snore turning into a snort turning into a grunt turning into the slow-stir of a man his age who had slept too-cold. Chevette would make a joke about her vigil. He would pretend not to have slept through it. They would go down to the next-deck-ring and get breakfast from the Armenians.

But for now, she sat on the bridge in the fog and listened to the bridge being itself. It was a kind of prayer. It was a kind of practice. It was a kind of work that nobody paid her for and nobody would ever thank her for and nobody in the city below would ever know happened.

But the bridge knew. And the bridge was what she was.

"She was the bridge's body, the way Skinner was the bridge's mind. She was its sleep, its breath, its slow-watching-eye. She was what the bridge became when the tourists went home and the deals got done and the violence didn't."
