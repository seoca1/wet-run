Novice

Warren's Meadow

1611 wordsHardwired (Walter Jon Williams)

Setup

The placarding wheel is the part that always goes first.

Warren turns the pump housing over in his hands, feeling the rotor through the casing with the pads of his fingers, the way a doctor feels a child's chest for the murmur that means the heart is leaking. He has the engine of an old Pratt and Whitney spread across the workbench like an autopsy, every gleaming chamber drilled into his hands under the cold bulb of the lamp. Outside the window of the hangar, the meadow holds its breath in a long Colorado silence. The mountains are brown with the dried grass of summer. He has not heard a plane in three days. He does not expect to hear one for another three.

He is seventy-one years old. He does not look seventy. The DNA holds — he paid for that, long ago, in cryo credits earned by a decade of quiet, useful sabotage — but the eyes remember the soot of Vandenberg and the eyes are tired. He sets the borescope down and looks at his two handreaders instead, the worn pneumatic gloves he wired himself ten years before to feel what the borescope feels, to know what the chambers look like in the moment when the chambers are hot. The handreaders are off. He wears them on his belt when he sleeps. He can hear the green hiss of the synthetic trachea that he keeps wrapped around his throat, the cold of the cylinder that feeds him whenever the body starts to forget to breathe.

He is thinking about the deltas again.

He is always thinking about the deltas. The first delta is called Midnight Sun, after a name Cowboy chose when he was nineteen — Cowboy gave the delta up after three years, the morning after the Rock War, when Cowboy understood that the jocks had been thirty-three kinds of fool. The second is Pony Express, which still flies when Warren can keep the bottles inside steel. The third is called The Last Free American, which is what Cowboy calls his life, the pretension which is the only thing that has kept Cowboy's hand steady on the throttle and on the wheel of every cargo that is, every cargo that has come down the Alley.

The console on the bench ticks once. Then ticks again. The console is an old thing — Orbital surplus, a square grey slab of neural-bound chip and chrome, the kind of console the thirdmen had used as their wetware consoles on Vandenberg before the war. Warren had wired it himself, years ago, to listen for the small transponder codes his three birds are supposed to send back at midnight, every midnight, no matter where they sit. The console ticks when it cannot hear them. Tonight the console is ticking.

Development

He looks out at the meadow again.

The meadow is a small green rectangle in the middle of a small brown corridor, a small brown corridor in the middle of a small green state. The state is a small green state in the middle of a small brown country, and the country is a small brown country in the middle of a small blue planet. The planet is a small blue planet in the middle of a small black sky. The sky is what Cowboy is in love with. Warren is in love with the meadow.

The meadow is what Warren bought with the credits that Cowboy paid him for the deltas, the meadow he bought with the credits the thirdmen paid him for the parts, the meadow he bought with the credits the Orbital blocs paid him for the service. The thirdmen are running out of credits. The blocs are running out of patience. Cowboy is planning his own run for next Wednesday.

A knock comes at the hangar door.

The knock is the knock the thirdmen use, three raps then two, and Warren sets the housing down without thinking and walks to the door. He opens it. The man outside is young — younger than Warren's daughter, who is dead — and the young man is wearing the brown jacket the Orbital couriers wear when they have come down through the well, and the young man is holding a small black case in his glove.

"Warden," the courier says.

"Warden died at Vandenberg," Warren says. "I am his ghost."

The courier does not smile. The courier holds out the case.

Inside the case is a small wetware module wrapped in black felt, the kind of chip that the Orbital recruiters used to slide under the scalp of every jock on the morning of the Rock War. The chip glows faintly, a small white light the color of a star seen through the chrome of a cockpit. Warren knows what the chip is. Warren knows why they have sent it.

"This is a recall," Warren says.

"This is a recall," the courier agrees.

Warren does not take the chip. Warren does not close the case. He stands in the door of his own hangar, on his own meadow, looking down at the wetware module the Orbital has sent him on the morning of his seventy-first year, and he thinks of Cowboy, who is not here yet but who will be here, who is always here, who keeps the runway lit with the white stones he carries in the pocket of his flying jacket and lays out by hand the night before he flies.

"Tell them," Warren says, very quietly, "that Warren is not a jock anymore."

The courier nods. The courier does not speak. The courier turns and walks back across the apron toward the road, where a small grey Orbital car waits with its engine running, the screen on the dashboard pulsing the small clean rhythm of an Orbital subroutine that Warren could read once and no longer can.

Warren closes the door.

Climax

He goes back to the bench. He lifts the rotor housing to his ear and shakes it.

Inside, the bearings chatter like a child's teeth in the cold.

He knows what the noise is. The bearings are worn. The bearings are worn because nobody owns them and nobody has owned them since the thirdmen decided that the deltas were more like them than like the panzers. The bearings are worn because the deltas were built for the battle that Warren won on the morning of the Rock War and Warren lost on the afternoon of the Rock War and Warren has been replaying the morning on the afternoon ever since.

The bearing is not dangerous. The bearing is not a problem. The bearing is a sound that Warren listens to, the way a sailor listens to the hull of his boat when the sailor is moved to the middle of the sea. The bearing is the sound of a memory that will not let go of his hand. The bearing is the sound of his own wetware running its own little subroutine in the back of his chrome skull, the subroutine the Orbits had installed when Warren came down from Vandenberg on the morning after, when the chair had burned his face the color of a wetware warning and the chair had left the subroutine running for thirty-one years.

He sets the housing on the bench. He turns on the lathe.

The bearing is what Cowboy loves. The bearing is what Warren keeps.

He is going to make the bearing. He is going to make the bearing the way Cowboy makes the run — making the bearing with the steady hand that Cowboy steadied with the steady nerve that Cowboy earned on the morning of the Rock War and Cowboy earned on the afternoon of the Rock War and Cowboy has been earning on every morning and every afternoon ever since.

Resolution

Outside, the meadow is silent. The stars are silent. The mountains are silent.

On the road, the small grey Orbital car is still waiting. The screen on the dashboard still pulses. The wetware module is still in the black case in Warren's back pocket, where he has put it without thinking, the way he puts every chip and module the Orbits send him into the back pocket of his overalls — into the back pocket where the chips will go to sleep until the morning when Cowboy climbs out of the bronze machine and asks Warren, as he always asks, whether the runway will hold him this time, and Warren will say yes.

The console on the bench ticks once more, then goes still. The chip has stopped glowing. The deltas are sleeping in their cradles in the dark.

The bearing begins to sing.

Warren listens to the bearing sing. He listens the way a father listens to the breath of a child through a wall of the house at night. He listens because the bearing is what Cowboy needs, and Cowboy is what the meadow needs, and the meadow is what Warren bought with the credits he earned being the man who keeps things alive — the man who keeps the deltas airworthy, the man who keeps the thirdmen supplied, the man who keeps the Orbital from taking back the little wetware it thinks it owns.

He turns off the lathe. He sets the bearing, still warm, into the rotor housing and rolls the housing in his palm. The bearing is round. The bearing is smooth. The bearing is, in the long quiet of the morning, the only thing that is, tonight, his.

Outside, the meadow is silent.

The stars have decided.
