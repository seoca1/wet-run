The Dodger's Office

1590 wordsHardwired (Walter Jon Williams)

Setup

The Dodger kept the office in Albuquerque because Albuquerque kept the office in shadows. The street outside the door ran north at a different angle than every other street in the city, and the angle hid the marquee of the Baptist church across the way from the small high window that looked out above his desk. The marquee was new. The marquee was a long bar of painted plywood mounted on steel rungs of its own, and the marquee carried, in a faithful hand, the name of the sermon of the coming Sunday. The marquee had been there for three years. The Dodger had seen every sermon and remembered none of them.

The sermon this morning was EARN THE THINGS YOU LOVE.

The Dodger did not love the things he had earned.

The portfolio, for instance. The portfolio was a square of leather carrying a small green bar of credit-bond-issued paper, and the paper carried, in a smaller hand, the name of the man whose credit had earned the paper. The man's name was Cowboy. The man's name was the name of every run Cowboy had flown across Damnation Alley for the last nine years. The Dodger held the paper between his fingers and turned it over in his hands, the way he turned the cut plug of tobacco in the cut plug of his mouth. The paper was warm.

The console on the wall of the office was an old Orbital console, the small chrome slab that the Wetworkers had once used, on Vandenberg, as the wetware deck of every chip they had slipped under the scalps of the jocks on the morning of the war. The small green light on the chrome pulsed, very slowly, in the small clean rhythm of a subroutine the Dodger had been watching for nine years without ever asking what the subroutine did. The Dodger did not know what the subroutine did. The Dodger did not love the console. The Dodger did not hate the console. The Dodger kept the console because the console was what Cowboy had given him, on the morning of the first run, when Cowboy had walked into the office with a wetware module in his pocket and a set of codes on his tongue.

Cowboy had been at the station.

This was the name Cowboy gave to the cut of the highway that ran north of Albuquerque on the cut of the road that ran east of the Sangre de Cristos, the cut of the mountain that Cowboy had flown over for the year. Cowboy had called the cut the station because Cowboy had been at the cut. Cowboy had flown the cut. Cowboy had earned the cut.

Cowboy had not earned the paper.

Development

The Dodger turned the paper over and looked at the paper. The paper was a small green bar, a few of credit-bond-issued paper in the cut of the day, a few of the paper that Cowboy had cut with the steady nerve that Cowboy had earned on the morning of the Rock War and Cowboy had earned on the afternoon of the Rock War and Cowboy had been earning on every morning and every afternoon ever since.

The paper was what Cowboy loved. The paper was what the Dodger kept.

He set the paper down on the desk. The desk was a small square of cut steel, the seat of the steady nerve that the Dodger had earned on the morning of the Rock War and the Dodger had earned on the afternoon of the Rock War and the Dodger had been earning on every morning and every afternoon ever since.

The desk held the codes that opened the portfolio. The codes were a small cut of numbers, a cut of numbers the Dodger kept on a small cut of paper. The Dodger did not love the codes. The Dodger did not hate the codes. The Dodger had been holding the codes for nine years, and the codes had been the only thing that had kept Cowboy's hand steady on the throttle and on the wheel of every cargo that was, every cargo that has come down the Alley.

The morning of the run, the codes sat on the desk. The codes sat on the desk the way Cowboy sat on the throne. The codes sat on the desk the way the bread sat on the altar at the church across the way. The codes sat on the desk the way the names of the sermon sat on the marquee.

The marquee, this morning, read EARN THE THINGS YOU LOVE.

The Dodger did not love the codes. The Dodger loved the man. The man had been the cut of his brother's cut. The man had been the cut of every cut of the cowboy's run. The man had been the cut of the Alley, the cut of the mountain, the cut of the office.

A knock came at the office door.

The knock was the knock Cowboy used — two raps, then one. The Dodger set the cigarette down on the chrome of the desk. The Dodger did not stand. The Dodger did not move.

"Come in, Cowboy," the Dodger said.

The door opened. Cowboy walked in.

Climax

Cowboy had not shaved. Cowboy had the small chrome of a wetware patch over the place behind his ear where the small black console of his chrome skull had been humming since the morning the Orbital bloc had paid to slip the chip under the scalp. Cowboy was in the tan jacket Cowboy always wore to fly. Cowboy was in the small brown boots Cowboy had bought at the Free Zone the year Cowboy had won the gold at the Alley championship.

Cowboy sat in the chair that faced the desk. Cowboy did not speak.

The Dodger did not speak.

The small green light on the console pulsed twice.

"You have the codes," Cowboy said, finally.

"I have the codes," the Dodger said.

"You will not give them to me."

"I will give them to you," the Dodger said. "After."

The Dodger stood. The Dodger walked around the desk. The Dodger took the small square of leather from the desk and held it out in his hand. The Dodger did not hold the codes. The Dodger held the paper.

"Tell me what the portfolio is for," the Dodger said. "Tell me why the Orbital man in the brown suit came down through the well last Tuesday and told me to keep this paper close until today."

Cowboy looked at the paper. Cowboy looked at the paper the way he looked at the chrome of a console before he jacked in. Cowboy looked at the paper a long time.

"The Orbital is not buying me," Cowboy said. "The Orbital is buying the city under the Alley. The Orbital is buying the dirtground the Alley is laid on. The Orbital is buying the right to flood the Alley and let the thirdmen settle the bill."

"And the codes," the Dodger said.

"The codes are the only thing that opens the bank that pays for the right," Cowboy said.

The Dodger did not speak.

"Give me the codes, brother," Cowboy said.

The Dodger put the paper back on the desk.

"I will give you the codes," the Dodger said, very quietly, "in the office, after the run, when you have not earned the paper from the Orbital man in the brown suit."

"You are asking me to refuse the run."

"I am asking you to refuse the paper," the Dodger said.

Cowboy did not speak. Cowboy looked at the paper. Cowboy looked at the codes. Cowboy looked at the Dodger.

Cowboy stood.

Resolution

Cowboy walked to the door. Cowboy opened the door. Cowboy did not look back. Cowboy walked out into the small dry morning of Albuquerque, into the small white light of the small white marquee of the small white church across the way. Cowboy walked toward the small grey Borrego where the small grey chrome of the cargo had been waiting all morning for the run.

The Dodger watched Cowboy from the high window.

The codes sat on the desk. The paper sat on the desk. The console pulsed. The marquee, across the way, in the long small white hand of the preacher, read EARN THE THINGS YOU LOVE.

The Dodger picked up the cigarette and lit it.

He did not bring the codes to the window. He did not hold the codes to the light. He set the codes down on the desk and let the codes sit where the codes had been sitting.

He was going to make the run.

He was going to make the run the way Cowboy made the run — making the run with the steady hand that Cowboy steadied with the steady nerve that Cowboy earned on the morning of the Rock War and Cowboy earned on the afternoon of the Rock War and Cowboy has been earning on every morning and every afternoon ever since.

He went out the door. The street ran north at the angle. The marquee ran east at the angle. The office ran south at the angle. The Dodger ran east at the angle of his own cut.

The portfolio stayed on the desk. The codes stayed on the desk. The names stayed on the desk.

He was going to make the run.

He was going to earn the things he loved.
