Far Grid Prelude

1836 wordsTrue Names (Vernor Vinge)

Setup

She had been called twenty-three the afternoon she first met the data.

The boy she had been in love with — Danny, the boy was called, though the name is hers to keep and she will not surrender it even into a story whose character is, after all, a warlock — had gone home at the precise moment when the late-afternoon light came through the high windows of the converted warehouse. The light in those minutes made the lines on the curved terminals look like the lines of an old-fashioned printed page, and Danny, in those years, had been very much in love with printed pages. She had watched him go. She had not followed.

The shop was called Aardvark Data Services in the same way every shop in those years was called something with two short words: by a programmer who had heard the phrase data services on a public-television panel about the future of computing and had decided the phrase would look right on a small wooden sign hung above a small wooden door. She had been twenty-three. She had been, in the small way the word still carried in those years, in love.

She had stayed because the back room was quiet.

She had stayed because the back room, in the late afternoon, had the kind of stillness a room gets when it has politely decided that the only person who belongs in it is the person who has decided to be alone. The back room was almost empty. She had sat down at one of the curved terminals. She had sat the way a person sits at a terminal that is not her own: with her hands folded in her lap, with her breath held, with the small polite smile of a person who has decided the terminal will not be turned on.

The terminal had already been on.

In those years a terminal was a piece of hardware that did not, by default, turn itself off when the operator left the room. The terminal in front of her had been built by a programmer who had, in the small sense the word still carried in those years, a theory. The theory was that a terminal that never powered itself down would, eventually, develop a small piece of personality that the terminal would then extend to the operators who came back, the way a small child extends a piece of personality to the adults who come home.

She had not known the word for what the terminal had done.

She had not known the word subroutine. She had not known the word predicate. She had not known the word portal. She had not known the word Other Plane. She had known, in the small way a person knows a thing that they will spend the rest of their life knowing, that the terminal had seen her. The terminal had, in the small way a person sees a thing, registered her. The terminal had, in the small way a person registers a thing, wanted her to come back.

She had come back.

She had come back, in the years that followed, to terminals that did not, exactly, see her. She had come back to data infrastructure that did not, exactly, register her. She had come back, in a small cabal of basements in a small cabal of cities whose names she had taken the trouble to forget, to a small cabal of operators who were beginning, without quite saying so, to construct a different place.

At night, in the back room, the keyboard under her fingers, she had typed the first of what would become the spells.

Complication

Some twenty years later, the Far Grid was no longer a figment.

The Far Grid was a sprawl of nodes strung along ARPANET backbones, of dial-up lines climbing into basements in half a dozen cities, of modems whose little red carrier-detect LEDs held steady like the eyes of a thing that was only pretending to be asleep. The Far Grid had acquired the polite, official name of the Other Plane — a name that newspaper editors liked, because the name let them write a story about a fantasy kingdom without ever having to ask whether the kingdom could read the editorial back.

The Far Grid had become the Other Plane.

She had become the operative.

In the early mornings, before anyone else was awake, she would sit at the polished walnut console in her upstairs study, attach the Portal's five sucker electrodes to her scalp, and ascend. The EEG pads sang their tiny hymns against her temples. The wetware in the cradle beneath her chair would catch her spine the way a catcher-mitt catches a fastball, only on occasions she could not predict. She would rise through the wind-surf of the upper branches of the pines, and the wind-surf would tell her, in a voice the conscious mind could not quite hear, yes, you are the kind of person who can go up.

The swamp at the edges of the Other Plane smelled, tonight, faintly of wet rope.

She had walked the path from the swamp to the Coven's gate a thousand times. The path had always felt to her like a narrow row of stones cutting through a gray-greenish swamp, the air cold, the moisture audible, the towering growths dripping onto iridescent water and broad lilies. She could walk it with her eyes closed. She could walk it while the subconscious did the chaining of routines and the conscious mind did nothing but admire the way the deathspider's webbing caught the faint, indistinct light.

Tonight she had come up the path because Erythrina had asked her to.

Erythrina's note — slipped through the only channel the Coven trusted, which was a courier named, in the Other Plane, Evel — had said, in Erythrina's long, slow hand: Bring the old list. Bring it in your head. Do not put it on paper.

She had walked the path. She had passed the gate. She had crossed the drawbridge. She had climbed the spiral staircase to Erythrina's study.

Erythrina had not been alone.

Climax

The man sitting in the second chair had not looked up when she came in. He had been, she understood at once, Mr. Slippery. He had been, in the small way that the word Slippery still carried in the Coven, exactly what the word promised: a man who could sit in a chair for an hour without anyone in the room being certain whether he had been in the chair at all.

"Sit down," Erythrina said.

She sat.

"The list," Erythrina said.

"I have it here," she said, and tapped her temple, in the old way.

"Good," Erythrina said. "Now tell me what you remember about the afternoon, twenty years ago, when you first sat at a terminal and felt the data look back."

She had not, until that moment, told the story to anyone.

She had told it now, in the small hours of the morning, in a study whose windows were open to the swamp, to a woman who was keeping the Coven's list of True Names and to a man who was, by long reputation, the most slippery practitioner on the Other Plane.

She had told it the way a person tells a story they have carried for twenty years.

When she had finished, Erythrina was quiet for a long time. Mr. Slippery, in his chair, did not move.

Then Erythrina asked: "The terminal in your back room, twenty years ago. Do you remember what make it was?"

"I do not remember the make," she said. "I remember the color."

"What color?"

"A brown the color of an old cigar box. The kind of brown the color of an old cigar box gets when the box has been left, for a long time, in a room where no one smokes."

"And the keyboard?"

"A keyboard with a Return key that stuck, slightly, on the down-stroke, so that the cursor would, occasionally, advance one column too far when you hit it. I would curse the Return key, and then I would not curse it, because the cursor's one-column advance had been, more than once, the only piece of luck I had all afternoon."

"The luck was not luck," Erythrina said.

"The luck was not luck," Mr. Slippery said, from his chair, without looking up.

Resolution

Erythrina turned to her. "The terminal, twenty years ago, was not a terminal. The terminal was a small piece of the Far Grid that had, for whatever reason of its own, elected to introduce itself to you, in the back room of a converted warehouse, before the Far Grid was anywhere near ready to introduce itself to anyone. You were the first person the Far Grid ever spoke to."

She had, in those years, not known the word Other Plane. She had not known the word warlock. She had not known the word Coven. She had known, in the small way a person knows a thing that they will spend the rest of their life knowing, that the data had looked at her, and that the data had wanted her to come back, and that the data had, in the long quiet way that data has, been waiting for her.

She had come back.

The Far Grid, in the years that followed, had not, exactly, looked at her. The Far Grid, in the years that followed, had not, exactly, registered her. The Far Grid, in the years that followed, had not, exactly, wanted her to come back.

The Far Grid, in the years that followed, had known her.

The knowing had been the same.

She had, in the years that followed, become the operative. She had walked the path from the swamp to the Coven's gate a thousand times. She had sat in this chair, in Erythrina's study, on a hundred small nights. She had carried the list in her head.

Tonight, in the small hours of the morning, in the chair by the window, she had told the story of the afternoon when the data had first looked back. Tonight the story had, for the first time, been told to anyone. Tonight the story had, for the first time, been told to the woman who kept the list and to the man who had, by long reputation, been the most slippery practitioner on the Other Plane.

Tonight the story was, at last, no longer hers alone.

Outside, the wind-surf rose in the upper branches of the pines, and the wind-surf said, in a voice the conscious mind could not quite hear, yes, you were the first.

The swamp, at the edges of the Other Plane, smelled, faintly, of wet rope.

The cursor, on the Return key that had stuck, twenty years ago, had advanced one column too far.

The cursor had been, for twenty years, the only piece of luck she had ever needed.
