Maiden's First Run

1484 wordsHardwired (Walter Jon Williams)

The first thing I learned on the interstate was that the interstate does not announce itself.

I was nineteen the night I climbed the dorsal hatch of my boyfriend's old panzer. He was still nineteen too — for the few minutes it took him to stud the jack into my right temple and his own, and lie down next to me in the g-suit rack, and tell me about the Alley. He had done the run twice before, both times with an older brother who'd quit after the second and stayed home in Tampa, working the console screen for the thirdmen. The brother had told him the Alley wasn't a road. The Alley was a lie about a road.

"He says it's a nervous-system highway," my boyfriend said. "It runs on the chip."

"Whose chip?"

"Ours. It reads our wetware. So the line you take is the line it learns you can stop being afraid at."

I thought that sounded like something his brother had made up. I said so.

My boyfriend laughed. He was a boy. He laughed the way boys laugh. Then he was dead.

The Alley killed him on his third run.

I was not in the panzer that night. I was in the terminal warehouse in Tampa, watching the small data screen on the back wall, watching the small blinking dot of his panzer travel north along the line that the thirdmen had drawn for him. He had made it past the privateer corridor at Hillsborough. He had made it past the Law's outer screen at Lakeland. He had made it past Kissimmee.

He had not made it past Orlando.

The Law rolled him at a turn off the 4, somewhere south of the old Disney World sprawl, the place where the chrome of the Orbital blockade sits heaviest, where the wetware of the panzer is most likely to fail. He found it last in the way that the Alley finds it last for everyone, which is to say, with the engines on fire and the screen showing the small, perfect number that the Alley had learned was the number at which his nervous system would give up.

He was nineteen. His name was Marek. The panzer was not paid for.

I have spent the year since then making sure that next time, my panzer will be paid for. The way you do that is you run the Alley yourself.

The morning I climbed the dorsal hatch, the Alley had not yet announced itself.

I climbed the dorsal hatch the way a girl climbs the dorsal hatch of a panzer she does not yet own — slowly, with the g-suit heavy on my shoulders, with the stud still cold against my right temple, with the data screen at the back of the cabin showing me only my own breathing. I had a different brother tonight. I had a different boyfriend. I had a different panzer. I had the cargo in the back: chloramphenildorphin, in two thick foam blocks marked CODE RED HANDLE WITH NETWORKS, which is what the thirdmen write on everything they want you to be careful of.

I had the cargo.

I had the nerve.

I sat down in the cabin. I closed the dorsal hatch. I let the small screen come up.

The screen scrolled a single line of data —

WETWARE: NOMINAL. INTERLOCK: COMPATIBLE. INTERSTATE: WAITING.

— and I understood, for the first time, what Marek's brother had meant.

The Alley was not a road.

The Alley was a chip.

The Alley read what the chip had read.

I told the screen my name. The screen did not blink. The screen said, in a small, patient voice,

WETWARE: NOMINAL. RUN WHEN READY.

I was not ready. I had not been ready since Marek died. I had been the girl who would never run the Alley, the way a girl who has lost a hand to a buzz-saw will never run the buzz-saw. I had been the girl who would work the small console in the warehouse for the thirdmen, who would watch other girls' panzers travel north along the small blinking line of their data, who would pour coffee and answer the portable when it rang and never, ever, climb the dorsal hatch.

I had been nineteen for a long time.

I was nineteen this morning.

I ran the Alley.

The Alley is three hundred miles long. The Alley is not three hundred miles wide. The Alley is the narrow, perfect line that a nervous system can hold at three hundred without slipping off the wetware.

I drove the Alley for three hours. I drove the Alley with my nerve. I drove the Alley with the steady nerve I had earned the morning Marek died, and the steady nerve I had earned the afternoon Marek died, and the steady nerve I had earned every morning since, working the small console, watching the small screen, drinking the small coffee, waiting for the morning when I would climb the dorsal hatch of my own panzer.

I drove the Alley at three hundred. The panzer's chrome sang. The chip inside the g-suit hummed with the patience the Way is patient with girls who have lost a boyfriend to it.

The Way did not announce the Law. The Way did not announce the privateer corridor at Hillsborough. The Way did not announce the screen at Lakeland. The Way did not announce Kissimmee. The Way did not announce the morning of Marek's death or the afternoon of Marek's death or the morning of every run since.

The Way waited for me to announce it.

I did not announce it.

I drove the turn at three hundred.

The turn was the place where Marek had lost his nerve. The turn was the place where Marek's panzer had stopped being his. The turn was the place where the small data screen had stopped showing the small blinking dot.

The turn was the place where the Law had rolled him, because the Law rolls you when your data starts to lose its small blinking.

My data did not lose its small blinking.

My chip did not slip.

My wetware did not fail.

I drove the turn the way Marek had not driven the turn. I drove the turn at three hundred. I drove the turn the way a girl drives the turn of a panzer she has earned, with the cargo in the back and the stud in her temple and the nerve in her pocket and the morning of Marek's death folded into the small space where the thirdmen keep their small patience.

I drove the turn, and the chrome sang, and the Alley did not speak.

I arrived at the warehouse at midnight.

The warehouse was not the warehouse in Tampa. The warehouse was the warehouse in Orlando, where the thirdmen's screen said the cargo would have to be, where the Law was waiting for the cargo, where the thirdmen's runners in their dark suits had brought them up from the line so that the cargo could find its new home.

The Law was at the warehouse.

The Law had been waiting for me at the warehouse the way the Law had been waiting for Marek at the warehouse the way the Law had been waiting for every girl who had tried the Alley since.

The Law was not the chrome of the Orbital blockade. The Law was not the chrome of the screen.

The Law was two men in dark suits with small chrome sockets on their wrists and ankles, the kind of sockets the thirdmen used before the Rock War ended, the kind that read the wetware of the chip in your temple and decided whether the cargo was a cargo or a body.

"ID," one of them said, holding out his small portable.

"Maiden," I said.

"Marek's girl?"

"Marek's girl," I said.

The Law looked at me the way the Law looked at the Alley. The Law did not speak for a long moment. The Law was patient.

Then the Law said, very quietly,

"You're the first girl to make the run."

I did not reply.

I climbed down from the cabin the way a girl climbs down from the cabin of a panzer she does not yet own — slowly, with the g-suit heavy on my shoulders, with the stud still cold against my right temple, with the cargo in the back, with the small data screen on the cabin wall showing me only my own breathing.

The cargo was waiting for me.

The Law was waiting for me.

The Alley, far behind me on the small line of data between Tampa and Orlando, was waiting for me.

I was the first girl to make the run.

I was the first girl to arrive at the warehouse.
