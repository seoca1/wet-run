Erythrina's List

1349 wordsTrue Names (Vernor Vinge)

Setup

I keep the names in a place that no one visits.

That is not strictly true. Mr. Slippery has visited once, years ago, when he believed I had been compromised and wanted to verify the integrity of the Coven's roster by reading it over my shoulder. I let him. He stayed an hour. He was polite. He left without altering anything. After he was gone, I found that he had moved one entry three places to the left and underlined the first syllable of the next. He did not tell me what the rearrangement meant. I have not asked. Some of his notations are not for me to read.

The list is not a roster of warlocks, exactly. It is closer to a ledger of debts. Each entry corresponds to a True Name that has been carried through the Coven — passed up the chain by an initiate, memorized by an officer, sometimes loaned to a guest for a single operation, and then, by long custom, returned to me. The custom predates me. The Coven's founders believed that a True Name left unchecked in the world would eventually be the death of its owner; they believed this so firmly that they constructed a small bureaucracy around the practice of carrying the names in their entirety and un-carrying them in the same operation. The bureaucratic forms are still there, at the bottom of my list, in the same worn ASCII as the original spell template, a small subroutine of ASCII that I have been, for fifteen years, the only person in the Coven allowed to read.

The list smells, when I open it, of the small paper smell that paper makes when paper has been kept, for a long time, in a room that no one is allowed to enter without the room's permission.

Tonight the list is six items longer than it was last month.

Two of the additions are easily explained: Robin Hood's new courier, a Polish boy named — in the Other Plane, and only there — Evel, has finally been read into the Coven's full registry. The boy's real name is encoded under four layers of persona and lives on a paper-only disk in my floor safe. The encoding was done by Slimey Limey, who is the only one of us who still keeps paper backups because, he says, the NSA reads optical, and the NSA reads ASCII, and the NSA reads optical ASCII in the way that a wetware construct reads the brain-data of a person who has, for a long time, been trying to remember what the NSA has been reading.

I do not argue with the Limey. I have not argued with the Limey in twenty years.

Complication

Three additions are new handles of old members who retired their handles after the last wave of FBI attention. Standard practice. I have flagged them for deletion in six months if the handles are not reclaimed.

The sixth addition is the one I cannot place.

It is not a name. It is a call sign. The list entry reads, in my own handwriting — or what passes for my own handwriting in the place where I keep the list, where handwriting is closer to a long, slow whistle:

M. / + nothing. No last name. No True Name. No node of origin. Just the initial M. and a slash, as if the rest of the line had been deliberately left blank. Below the slash is a single annotation, in a hand I do not recognize, that reads: trusted by no one, sends messages in his sleep.

The Mailman.

I close the list. I do not write anything new on the cover page. I have not added anything to that page in fifteen years. The page reads, in the old typeface:

*To my sister, with love.*

It is not addressed to me. The page predates the Coven. I have never asked whose sister it was. I have never been curious about it. Tonight, for the first time, I find myself reading it slowly, as if the words might tell me how to file an initial that is not a name and a slash that is not a signature and a line of small handwriting that has no business being in a place where no one visits.

The Coven is quiet. The moat burns its usual color. Alan has not flagged any unusual traffic at the gate. The list, who knows everything, knows nothing useful about M.

Climax

I fold the list back into its envelope. I seal the envelope with the seal that no one in the Coven has ever broken on purpose. I put the envelope back in the place where no one visits.

Then I sit in the chair by the window, and I think about the fact that the Mailman, who has never in his years of operation used real-time communication, has now twice, in the last week, sent a message — not to me, but to the list — through a channel that no one in the Coven admits to having built.

The list has always been the place where the Coven's names are kept. The Mailman has just demonstrated, without leaving a True Name or a node of origin, that he knows where the list is.

I do not know what that means. I do know that I am going to have to invite Mr. Slippery over again. He has a habit, when the Coven is worried, of arriving exactly when the worry starts. I have a habit, when I am worried, of pretending that I am not. Tonight I will not pretend. Tonight I will leave the gate open for him, and I will pour the tea I keep in the small cabinet behind the typewriter, and we will sit across the long table and not say a single word about the Mailman until one of us cannot stand it any longer.

I have not yet decided whether that one will be him or me.

I have, in the long hours of looking at the envelope, finally understood what the envelope has been trying to tell me. The envelope has been trying to tell me that the Mailman has, in the polite subroutine of the Mailman's small mind, decided to introduce himself to the Coven — not to me, not to Mr. Slippery, not to the gate, but to the list, which is the only piece of the Coven that has, for fifteen years, been allowed to know what the Mailman has been doing. The envelope has been trying to tell me that the Mailman has, in the small subroutine of his own long memory, finally decided that the list is the only piece of the Coven that is worth introducing himself to.

I have been outread.

I have been outread by a man who has, for fifteen years, been sending letters to the Coven through a channel that no one in the Coven admits to having built.

Resolution

I do not know what the Mailman has been trying to tell me. I do know, in the small way a person knows a thing that they will spend the rest of their life knowing, that the Mailman has, for the first time in his long career, decided that the Coven is worth telling.

I have not yet decided whether that decision is good or not.

In the morning, I told myself, I would invite him. In the morning, I would leave the gate open. In the morning, I would pour the tea.

In the morning, the morning would come.

The list went back into its envelope. The envelope went back into the place where no one visits. The place went back into being the place where no one visits, because the place, in the end, is the only thing in the room that does not have a subroutine to be read by.

I sat in the chair by the window.

The tea cooled.

The tea, in the morning, would be poured.
