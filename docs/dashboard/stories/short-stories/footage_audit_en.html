Footage Audit

1153 wordsBlue Ant Trilogy

"The footage was the only thing that was real. Everything else was interpretation."

— Spook Country, Cayce Pollard's internal monologue

Introduction

The apartment in Soho smelled of dust and old paper. Cayce Pollard stood at the window, watching the morning light catch the edge of a Gabriel Hounds tag on a passerby's jacket. The tag was authentic. She knew because her skin didn't crawl.

The footage sat on the external drive beside her laptop, the drive warm against the desk because three terabytes of authenticated POV archive generate a small amount of constant heat that registers against the wooden surface like a sleeping cat the size of a paperback book and weight of a small confession. Three terabytes. The Brown operation's complete archive. Tito had delivered it in a shielded case, his expression the same careful blankness he wore when he was being followed, the same expression he wore when he was following someone else, the expression that was an absence of any expression at all and the absence was what Cayce hired him for.

"Cayce." Milgrim's voice from the doorway. He'd stopped trembling six months ago. The anti-addiction protocol had rewired something, the wiring invisible to anyone who hadn't watched Milgrim's hands at forty-eight-hour intervals across the half-year that had followed Zurich, or maybe he'd just run out of things to fear because the supply of fear in Milgrim's body had been exhausted by the protocol and the protocol had replaced the fear with the absence of the fear and the absence of the fear was what Milgrim had become. "The client called. Bigend. He wants the audit by noon."

Cayce didn't turn. "Bigend always wants it by noon. Bigend wants it before he asks."

"Bigend wants to know if the footage is real." Milgrim stepped closer. The light caught the edge of his jaw. "All of it. The locative art. The shipping containers. The thing in the container."

Rising Action

The footage was authenticated POV records. Every frame signed, timestamped, geolocated. The Brown operation had been thorough — or paranoid, which in their line of work amounted to the same thing.

Cayce opened the first file. Havana. A street corner. The locative art piece manifested through her phone's camera: a ghostly sculpture hovering above the pavement, visible only through the lens. The artist's signature embedded in the metadata. Alberto. 2007. Havana.

The second file. A shipping container in a port she couldn't identify. The camera moved inside. Crates. Pallets. Something wrapped in gray tarpaulin. The footage didn't explain. Footage never explained. Footage was.

The third file. Tito's face, close to the lens. His eyes reflected the container's interior lights. He was saying something. The audio was stripped — Brown operation protocol. But Cayce read the movement of his lips: This is it. This is what they're moving.

She scrolled. Forty-seven files. Forty-seven locations. Havana. Miami. Rotterdam. Vancouver. A container terminal in Busan. Each file a piece of a puzzle the Brown operation had assembled and then abandoned.

Or been forced to abandon.

"Brown's dead." Milgrim said it quietly. "Holland's in the wind. Windgate's cooperating with someone. Probably not the people he says."

Cayce's fingers paused on the trackpad. "The footage doesn't care who's cooperating. The footage is."

"Bigend cares who's cooperating. Bigend cares what's in the container."

Cayce looked at him. Milgrim had learned to read her silence. That was new. Or maybe she'd just stopped hiding it.

"What's in the container, Cayce?"

She opened file twenty-three. The Busan terminal. Night. The container's doors opening from the inside. No — being opened from the outside. Figures in hazmat suits. The gray tarpaulin pulled back.

The footage showed: not weapons. Not drugs. Not currency.

Servers. Racks of them. Blade servers, custom cooling, fiber bundles thicker than her wrist. The labels were in Korean and English. Hosaka. Maas Biolabs. Kombinat.

And beneath the servers, in a recessed compartment: a single Ono-Sendai unit. Modified. The casing scored with something that might have been tally marks. Or coordinates.

Climax

Cayce felt the familiar cold clarity. The pattern recognizing itself.

The Brown operation hadn't been tracking a shipment. They'd been tracking a node. A nodal point in a network that spanned Havana locative art, Busan server farms, and whatever Holland had been running in Vancouver.

The footage was the only thing that was real. Everything else was interpretation.

But interpretation was what Bigend paid for.

"Milgrim." She didn't look up. "The locative art in Havana. The artist — Alberto. Run his signature against the Idoru project's fan-forum metadata. 2005 to 2007."

Milgrim's phone was already out. "On it."

"Tito's lip movements in file three. Reconstruct the audio from the visual phonemes. Cross-reference with Windgate's known vocabulary from the Holland intercepts."

"Done."

"And the Ono-Sendai in the container. The tally marks. Map them against known nodal-point coordinates from Laney's Slitscan archives."

Milgrim paused. "Laney's archives are proprietary. Slitscan —"

"Bigend owns Slitscan's nodal-point data. He bought it in 2004. Through a shell company in the Caymans. You know this. You wrote the briefing."

Milgrim's expression didn't change. But his fingers moved faster.

The pattern was organizing itself. The data wanted, the data grew.

Havana locative art → Idoru fan forums → nodal perception → Brown operation → Busan servers → Ono-Sendai with coordinates.

The footage was the only thing that was real. But the pattern made the footage real. The pattern was what the footage meant.

Resolution

Bigend's reply came at 11:47. Thirteen minutes before noon.

Accepted. Invoice at usual rate. The container is in Vancouver. You're on the next flight.

Cayce closed the laptop. The external drive went into the shielded case. The Gabriel Hounds tag on the passerby's jacket was still authentic. Her skin still didn't crawl.

"Vancouver." Milgrim was already packing. "The container in Vancouver."

"Bigend wants it opened. Bigend wants to know what's on the servers."

"And if it's a trap?"

Cayce picked up her jacket. The fabric was a vintage Barbour, waxed cotton, no synthetic fibers. No tags to make her skin crawl.

"Then the footage will show it. The footage is the only thing that's real."

She walked out of the Soho apartment. The morning light was brighter now. The dust motes danced in it, each one a data point in a pattern she was learning to read.

Somewhere the servers were humming. Somewhere the footage was waiting.

Somewhere Cayce was not.
