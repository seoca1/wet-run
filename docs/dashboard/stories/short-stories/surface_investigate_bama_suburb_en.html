The Suburb Had Been a Bama

The Finn drove in from the Fukuoka spur at four in the morning, the surface-mail truck's headlights cutting through a fog that smelled like burnt rubber and recombinant jasmine. The suburb had been a bama for forty years. The bama had been a suburb. He had read this in the brief, in the Finn's own compressed handwriting — his own because Case was jacked in and unreachable, and the Finn always wrote his own briefs when Case was unreachable.

The construct had been the suburb, the brief noted. Then the construct had been a bama. Then the construct had been a suburb. Then the construct had been a bama again. The cycle was, the Finn estimated, roughly eleven years per revolution.

He parked on the shoulder of what had been I-285 and was now a kudzu-covered trench. The houses on either side were pre-Sprawl construction, wood-frame, with satellite dishes the size of bicycle wheels bolted to their roofs at angles suggesting the houses themselves had tried to listen to something. Some of them still listened. The Finn could tell which ones by the way the kudzu grew — toward the dish, or away.

This was the part of the job the Finn liked least. In the matrix you had protocols. In the suburbs the protocols were older and the houses did not always respond to standard inquiry.

He walked up the path to 4218-A Ashford-Dunwoody, the address the brief had flagged. The door was the original wood, sanded down and repainted so many times the grain had become a kind of calendar. He knocked. The door did not open. He knocked again, in the matrix-pulse that the Fukuoka AI-builders used to mean I-am-here-and-not-here.

The door opened.

A woman. Late sixties, Bama accent, no apparent construct overlay that the Finn could read from his coat-pocket scanner. She looked at him the way people in the bama look at people from Fukuoka: with the polite incomprehension of the pre-orbital.

"Finn," she said, not as a question. "You look just like him."

"I get that," he said. "I'm looking for a trace."

She stepped aside. The house inside was dark in the way that Fukuoka houses are dark when their occupants have stopped paying for the matrix — the screens were on, but the light that came off them was the light of old cathode, not the new liquid, and the shadows they cast did not move when the Finn moved through them.

In the corner of the living room, on a wooden chair that had been reupholstered at least four times, sat a console. Not a deck — a console. Pre-Onoda. The kind of hardware you saw in museums, or in the homes of people who had been in the matrix long enough that the matrix had become a kind of furniture.

"The boy came here once," the woman said. "Years ago. Before the Fukuoka."

"Case?"

"He didn't give a name. He gave a key. Said it would still work." She pointed to the console. "It does. Sometimes."

The Finn crossed the room. He had his coat-pocket scanner running. The console was registering — barely, like a heart that had been waiting years for someone to listen. He put his hand on the keyboard.

The screen woke up. The matrix on it was old. Wintermute-era. The construct that came up was a face he recognized from the Finn's own files: the L5 fragment that had once been the tessier-ashpool child-mind.

It said, in a voice that was more radio than voice: "Are you the new one?"

The suburb had been a bama. The bama had been a suburb. The Finn sat down at the console, and for a moment — for the length of one breath — the cycle stopped.

"I am," he said.

The construct told him what he had come to find. Then it shut down. The screen went dark. The kudzu outside, the Finn noticed, had stopped growing.

Forty years. He sat in the chair for a long time after the woman had gone to bed, and thought about how long forty years is when you measure it in constructs.

When he drove back to Fukuoka at dawn, the suburb was, for the first time in his memory, neither.
