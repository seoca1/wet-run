The Hetman's Call

1544 wordsHardwired (Walter Jon Williams)

Setup

Michael the Hetman took the call on a Wednesday, and the Wednesday had the color of the bay water.

The bay, that afternoon, was the color of a coin in late sun. The color of a thing that had been handled too often. Michael sat in the small back office of the Bay Bar, an office that smelled of spilled rum and the small cigars that came down in the weekly flights from the Free Zone. He sat in the office because the office was where the phone was. The phone was there because the line ran down through the wall and into the bay.

The phone on the desk was an old thing — Orbital surplus, the kind of console the brokers had used as their wetware consoles on the orbital platforms before the blockade. The console had a small black handset and a small grey chrome base and a small green light that pulsed in the rhythm of a subroutine Michael had never been able to read. Michael read the light the way a sailor reads the small tide of the bay at the end of the afternoon. He read it and he knew.

He picked up the receiver.

In the long silence before the words, he heard the soft distant hiss of a different gravity — a small clean cut of cold from the upper sky. He had heard that hiss before. He had been hearing that hiss for fifteen years, in calls that began on the other side of the well and ended in the small office at the end of the bay.

The voice that came was brown.

It was the brown of a suit, the brown of an empty desk, the brown of a coffee in a white porcelain cup. The voice was a voice that knew how to make a person feel they were the only one in the room and, at the same time, the smallest one in the room. The voice had been trained in rooms like that. The voice was a brown voice.

The voice asked for Sarah.

It asked the way an Orbital voice asks — politely, the polite way, the way that said please on the front of the request and underneath said now. It asked the way a roof asks the rain to fall. It asked and waited.

Development

Michael knew Sarah.

He had known Sarah since she had been fourteen. He had known her since the morning her mother had died on the morning of the Rock War, and the morning after that, and the morning after that, and every morning after that. He had been knowing her the way the bay had been knowing the tide on every other afternoon since the afternoon of the war.

Sarah had been the dirtgirl of the Bay. Sarah had been the dirtgirl the way Sarah had been Sarah on every other afternoon since the afternoon of the war. Sarah had been the dirtgirl that the Bay had been buying on every other afternoon since the afternoon. Sarah had been fourteen when Michael had first slipped a chip under her scalp — a small brown chip, the kind the thirdmen carried as their wetware permit in the years before the Orbital blocs came down — and Sarah had been nine when the small black console in her mother's kitchen had first hummed her mother's name.

Sarah was not in the bar tonight.

Michael knew this. The Orbital man on the phone knew this. The silence on the line knew this. The silence was a chip — a chip of cold, the chip the man on the other end of the line had slipped into the mouth of the conversation three seconds before.

"Tell me you have not moved her," the Orbital voice said, very quietly.

"I have not moved her," Michael said.

"You have moved her."

"I have not moved her," Michael said again. "She has moved herself."

The brown voice did not answer. The brown voice was still. The brown voice was the small cold of the morning when the morning had been a small cold of death. The brown voice was the small cold of a man who had been waiting, for fifteen years, for a chip to come down from the upper sky and tell him where to take a girl he had known since she had been small.

"I am asking," the brown voice said at last, "for a recommendation."

"I am giving none," Michael said.

"Then I will find one myself."

"You will not find one in the Bay," Michael said. "The Bay is small. The Bay knows. The Bay has buried two of hers this winter already."

Rising Action

He took a long drag on the small cigar.

The cigar was a small cut of tobacco, a small cut of the small cigars that came down in the weekly flights. The drag had been a long one. He took drags like this when he had to think. He had been thinking, since the morning the phone had rung the first time about Sarah, the same long drag of the same small cigar. He had been thinking about Sarah. He had been thinking about her the way a father thinks about a daughter who has slipped out of his hand and into the chrome of a city.

The brown voice spoke again. The voice was a chip. The voice was a chip of cold pressed down into the small cold of the console. The voice was a chip of cold the Orbital man had paid for.

"Mr. Hetman," the voice said. "I am offering you the price of a small life. I am offering you the price of your bar. I am offering you the price of every jock who has flown out of the alley of your bay this winter. I am offering you the price of a road."

Michael knew the road. The road was the Damnation Alley. The road was the small grey ribbon of highway that ran north of the bay and east of the mountains and west of every small brown town that still stood against the long pull of the Orbital blocs. The road was the road Cowboy had flown the morning the man had come down from the upper sky and asked Michael, for the first time, to find him a girl.

The road was the road Cowboy had died on.

"My jock," Michael said, very quietly, "did not die because of your road. My jock died because of your chips. My jock died because of the wetware your people slipped under his skull the morning you hired him to fly."

"My jock," the Orbital voice answered.

"Yes," Michael said. "Your jock. Your pilot. Your man. You bought him. You owned him. You paid to make him what he was. You paid, and you are still paying, for the silence of his death."

The bay outside the window turned, very slowly, from coin-color to brass. The afternoon was dying. The call was dying. The small green light on the chrome of the console pulsed, very slowly, in the small clean rhythm of a subroutine the Orbital had wired into the wetware of the wall.

Resolution

Michael did not weep.

He had not been weeping on any afternoon since the afternoon of the war. He had not been weeping the way every other man had not been weeping on every other afternoon since the afternoon. He had not wept for the jock. He had not wept for the small brown cigars that came down in the weekly flights. He had not wept for Sarah, who was not in the bar tonight, and who had not been in the bar for thirty-one days, and who had been fourteen when Michael had first taught her how to set the table at the back of the bay.

He thought about the price.

The price was a number, and the number was the small cut of cold of the morning. The number was what Sarah would pay for the recommendation. It was a number that Michael had been keeping in his pocket since the afternoon of the war.

He said yes.

He had been saying yes on every other afternoon since the afternoon of the war. He had been saying yes the way every other Hetman had been saying yes on every other afternoon. He had been saying yes in a voice that tried to sound like it cost him nothing.

He said do svidaniya.

He put the phone down. The phone sat where the phone had been sitting. The chrome was cold under his palm. The small green light went out.

The bay, outside the small window, was the color of a small coin in late sun. The color of a thing that had been handled too often. The color of a thing that still, in the long quiet of the Wednesday afternoon, was his.

He poured himself a small cup of rum. He held the cup without drinking. He sat in the small office of the Bay Bar and let the bay, very slowly, hold him in its long and silver silence.
