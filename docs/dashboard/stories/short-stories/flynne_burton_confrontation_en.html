Money on the Counter: Flynne Confronts Burton

Origin: Flynne confronting Burton about the money

759 wordsPeripheral

There were seven thousand dollars in hundreds on the kitchen counter, weighted down with a coffee mug that said "Maqsood's Quality Meats" in script the color of dried blood. Burton had put the mug there an hour ago and walked out to the porch without saying anything. I had been standing in the kitchen doorway for nine minutes, watching the coffee mug hold down the money, watching the money hold the coffee mug, watching the whole arrangement not explain itself.

I picked up the mug. The coffee inside was cold and had a skin on it the color of a contact lens.

I walked out to the porch.

Burton was sitting in the plastic chair with the cracked arm. The plastic chair had been on this porch since I was nine. He was looking out at the lot across the road where the Wilsons used to park their truck, except the Wilsons were gone and the lot was just a square of cracked asphalt holding down some milkweed.

"Seven thousand," I said.

"Yeah."

"Seven thousand dollars."

"Yeah."

"On our counter."

"Yeah."

I sat down on the porch step. The wood was the temperature of creek water. The sun had gone behind the hill on the other side of the road and the shade of our trailer was starting to stretch itself out across the cracked asphalt in the shape of a second lot, parallel and slightly off-register from the first.

"Where did it come from, Burton."

"You know where it came from."

"I know where you said it came from."

"It's the same place."

"It's not the same place. You said you were testing a headset. You didn't say the headset was plugged into a job."

He didn't answer. He picked up a piece of milkweed stem from between the porch boards and started pulling it apart into smaller pieces. The milkweed had gone to fluff. The fluff was the color of clean cotton.

"Burton."

"Flynne."

"Where did the money come from."

"It came from the sessions. They paid for the sessions. You did the sessions. The money came from the sessions."

"Burton, no one pays seven thousand dollars for someone to sit in a chair and play a video game."

"You weren't playing a video game."

"Then what was I doing."

He looked at me then, finally, with the squint he got when he was trying to decide whether to tell me something or whether telling me would make me do the thing he didn't want me to do. I had seen the squint before. The squint was the most reliable weather vane on this porch.

"You were making good money," he said.

I put the mug with the cold coffee down on the porch step between us. The Maqsood's Quality Meats script was facing away from me. The skin on the coffee wobbled.

"Burton."

"Flynne."

"I have been making money at the print shop for nine years and the most I have ever made in a week is four hundred and sixty dollars. I have been making money at the print shop for nine years and my half of this trailer is paid up through October and my half of this trailer is paid up through October because of those four-hundred-and-sixty-dollar weeks, and I have made every single one of them myself."

"You have."

"So if there is seven thousand dollars sitting on our kitchen counter under a mug that says Maqsood's Quality Meats on it, that money came from somewhere. And the somewhere is not the print shop. And the somewhere is not Houdini's. And the somewhere is not the medicated cream I am putting on Mom's shoulder three times a day."

He pulled another piece of fluff off the milkweed stem. The fluff drifted. The fluff caught on my knee.

"The somewhere is the headset," he said.

"The somewhere is the headset."

"The headset paid for the sessions."

"And the sessions paid for the headset."

"Yes."

"Burton, no one pays seven thousand dollars for someone to sit in a chair and play a video game. You said that already. I am saying it again because I am still waiting for the answer to where the seven thousand dollars came from."

He looked out at the milkweed lot again.

"It came from London," he said.

I sat with that.

The shade of the trailer had reached the far edge of the Wilsons' lot. The milkweed was in the shade. The fluff on my knee was in the shade.

"London England," I said.

"London future," he said.
