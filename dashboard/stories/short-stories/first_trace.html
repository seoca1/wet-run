The Finn's Client

Introduction

*"The sky above the port was the color of television, tuned to a dead channel."*

— Gibson, Neuromancer, Chapter 1

The Finn's office smelled like burnt synth-coffee and old RAM. I'd been jacked in for six hours straight, burning my eyes against the pale geometry of Chiba's corporate sublayers, when he finally slid a credstick across the desk.

The Ono-Sendai electrodes had left their usual red circles on my temples—the kind that took an hour to fade, even with the good cold cream from the head shop on the third level. I hadn't slept in two days. The good juice was down to my last three vials, and the dealer on the corner was starting to look at me the way dealers look at people who can no longer afford the asking price.

"Trace job," he said. His voice was the color of money—green and cold. "Someone's been talking to the wrong people."

I looked at the stick. Fifty thousand. Enough to buy me six months of the good juice, the stuff that made my nervous system feel like it was full of warm honey instead of static. Enough to forget, for a little while, that I'd once been the best cowboy in Chiba, before the good surgeon in Minneapolis had scooped out the part of me that made the matrix sing.

"Who's the target?"

"Name's Yoshida. Mid-level icebreaker for T-A subsidiary in Fukuoka. He's been moving data to a drop point in Istanbul." The Finn's eyes were flat black screens, reflecting nothing. "I need to know who he's meeting when he jacks in."

"That's a trace," I said. "Not a crack. You're not paying crack rates for a trace."

"I'm paying for speed." He pushed a second stick across. "Three days. Maybe less."

I took both sticks. The weight of them felt like a promise—or a chain. The Finn had a way of making every job feel like the first step toward something larger, something I couldn't see yet. The kind of job where the money was the least interesting part.

"Why me?" I asked, because the Finn never asked me directly. He always went through Linda Lee, or that young hustler from the Chat—, or sometimes just a dead drop in a noodle shop. "You don't even like me."

"I don't like anyone, Case. You know that." He lit a cigarette that wasn't a cigarette—some kind of neural prep, the kind that makes the time before a jack-in feel like warm water. "I like cowboys who can think. Most of them can't. They jack in, they see the ice, they get scared. You get scared and you get smart at the same time. That's rare."

I didn't know what to say to that, so I took another drink from the cold coffee he'd poured me an hour before. It tasted like the bottom of a dumpster in the rain, which was how all of his coffee tasted, and which was probably intentional.

The target was ghost in the matrix. Yoshida moved through corporate ice like a fish through dark water, leaving almost no signature. I spent two days just mapping his patterns, watching him jack in from his apartment in Fukuoka at exactly 3 AM local, stay for four hours, jack out.

He was consistent. Professional. And that consistency was the first thing that told me something was wrong.

A good icebreaker doesn't keep the same schedule. A good icebreaker knows that patterns are how you get caught. Patterns are the noise you make when you forget nobody else is making any.

On the third night, I followed him into the deep architecture. We went past the corporate ice, past the public sublayers, past the tourist traps where kids jack in for the cheap thrills of artificial zero-g. Down into the black space where the older systems lived—the pre-corporate architecture that nobody bothered to maintain anymore because there was no money in it. The kind of space where the matrix gets thin, like worn velvet, and you can see the seams.

I was halfway down the third sublayer when I saw the first sign that Yoshida wasn't alone in the black space. A second signature, faint, the color of a dead leaf, moving in parallel. Not a real person. A construct, maybe. Or a very clever kind of ice.

Yoshida stopped at a node I didn't recognize. It wasn't T-A architecture. It wasn't any architecture I'd ever seen. It was older than anything I'd ever been paid to crack, and it hummed with a kind of cold patience, the way some animals wait for prey they know is coming.

And then I saw what he was doing.

He wasn't just moving data. He was building something—a kind of dead drop that existed in the space between systems, invisible to normal tracing. It was clever work, elegant in a way that reminded me of Molly's knife, precise and brutal. The data went in, and the system closed over it like water, and there was nothing to see, nothing to trace, nothing to find unless you were already standing inside the dead drop's own architecture looking back at the people who had built it.

I traced the connection. Istanbul, just like the Finn said. But there was a second node, buried deeper, routing through three different corporate systems before landing at a final address. Each hop used a different protocol, a different kind of ice, a different corporate signature, and each one was old—older than I was, older than the matrix, older than the Sprawl itself.

L5.

Tessier-Ashpool's orbital habitat. The place where everything in the Sprawl ultimately traced back to, like blood returning to the heart. The place where the rich went to die, and the AI went to be born, and where, the old stories said, the future itself was kept in a vault nobody had ever opened.

I pulled out. My hands were shaking. The good juice was wearing off and the cold cream was wearing off and the Ono-Sendai electrodes had left circles on my temples that would take a week to fade. The Finn had known. He'd known what Yoshida was doing, known about the dead drop, known about L5. This wasn't a trace job.

This was a test.

When I jacked out for the last time, the sun was coming up over the Sprawl, painting the perpetual smog in shades of orange and gray. The kind of color that meant nothing, that came from nothing, that was just the world pretending to be a postcard for people who still had postcards to send.

The Finn was waiting in his office, same as before. The credsticks were gone from the desk, replaced by a single cup of cold coffee and a small black notebook I had never seen him use in front of another person.

"You found it," he said. Not a question.

"L5," I said. "What the hell is going on?"

He smiled. It was the first time I'd ever seen him do that. The smile was old. The smile had been in a lot of rooms before mine. "You passed, Case. The Finn doesn't hire cowboys who can't think. He hires ones who can see the shape of what's coming before it gets here."

"What is coming?"

"I don't know yet." The Finn's eyes were still black screens, but now they seemed to hold something else—a reflection of the matrix itself, infinite and cold, the color of a TV tuned to a channel that hadn't broadcast in fifty years. "But when I figure it out, I'll need someone who can trace it back to the source. And I don't mean L5. I mean whatever is in L5, pretending to wait."

He slid another credstick across the desk. This one was heavier than the first two combined, and it was the wrong color—matte black, like the kind of stick that doesn't leave a paper trail in any bank that wants to stay in business.

"Consider it an advance," he said. "On the next job. And there will be a next job, Case. There's always a next job, until there isn't."

I took it. The weight felt different now—not like a chain, but like a compass pointing somewhere I couldn't see yet. The kind of compass that points to a place the compass itself is afraid of.

Somewhere in L5, or beyond it.

Somewhere where the game was bigger than any of us had imagined, and where the only way to know what was at the end of the game was to play until you couldn't play anymore.

Somewhere where 3Jane Tessier-Ashpool was waiting, in a family vault of her own making, for the next cowboy foolish enough to jack in and find out.

I left the office. The rain had started. The rain was the color of television, tuned to a dead channel. The rain is always the color of television, in Chiba. The rain is the only honest thing in the city, and the city knows it, and the city is afraid of it, and the city waits, every morning, for the rain to be a different color.

The rain has not been a different color in twenty years.

*First Trace* is an original short story set in William Gibson's Sprawl universe. It takes place after the events of *Neuromancer* and serves as a bridge between Case's recovery and the larger conspiracy that will define his future.

Epilogue (The Color of Rain)

At 0300, I jack out. At 0300, the rain is the color of television, tuned to a dead channel. The color has not changed. The color will not change. The matrix is a country I do not want to visit, but Chiba is a country I cannot leave.

I walk into the rain. The rain is the same color. The rain has always been the same color. The rain is the only honest thing in Chiba.

Tomorrow I will jack back in. Tomorrow the buffer will be waiting. Tomorrow the construct will be waiting. Tomorrow the matrix will be waiting.

Tomorrow the rain will still be the color of television, tuned to a dead channel.

The dead channel is the only channel that does not lie.
