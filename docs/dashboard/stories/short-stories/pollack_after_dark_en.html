Pollack After Dark

1438 wordsTrue Names (Vernor Vinge)

Setup

They left at three. The drizzle had become a misty rain by then.

The cabbages were ruined. Six plants, four of them the late variety I had been coaxing into a second head, all crushed flat by the boots of people who had not asked my name before they frog-marched me into my own kitchen. I did not care about the cabbages. I cared about the electrodes, which were back in their case, which were back in the drawer, which were back where they had been before the visit — which was the worst part of the visit, in its way: that nothing in the drawer had moved, that the Feds had been so certain of their case that they had not even bothered to take the electrodes as evidence.

The Portal's five sucker electrodes were cool against my fingers when I held them up to the light of the kitchen window. The light through them was pink, faintly, the way thin glass goes pink when you look through the back of it.

I did not power up the Portal until the sun was behind the tree line and the only light in the garden was the gold band across the taller trees to the east. I did not power up the Portal until I had walked the garden twice, listening for the gravel of tires on the road, hearing nothing. I did not power up the Portal until I had stood for a long moment at the edge of the cabbage patch, in the rain that was no longer rain but only the air being wet, and I had said aloud, to no one: I am Roger Andrew Pollack, TIN/SSAN 0959-34-2861, and they have my True Name, and there is nothing I can do about it.

Then I went inside and I powered up the Portal.

The Portal, in the kitchen, sat in its cradle the way a small brain sits in a small chrome cradle, with the EEG pads singing their tiny hymns against the temples of anyone who had been foolish enough to attach to the wetware of the construct the Coven's founders had spent twenty years building. The Portal smelled, when I attached the electrodes to my scalp, of the small ozone smell that ozone makes when ozone has been, for a long time, looking for a piece of wetware to attach to.

Complication

The ascent was slow. The subconscious had to be coaxed. The wind-surf in the upper branches of the pines came to me through the kitchen window, very faintly, and the subconscious took it as a kind of permission: yes, you are the kind of person who can go up, you have been the kind of person who can go up for four years, the wind-surf has not changed. I went up.

The fringes of the Other Plane looked, tonight, like the edges of a worn photograph. I could see the data-space the way I could see the back garden when I squinted: a wide, dull field of stacked rectangles, each one a server, each one unlit, each one humming so faintly that only the subconscious could hear it. I traced through three changes of carrier. I found a comsat to do my intermediate computing. The whole process was almost at a subconscious level — the proper functioning of numerous subroutines, the chaining of small pieces of code that the Coven's founders had written into the architecture of the Other Plane itself — and for a long moment I was almost at peace.

Then I tried to find the Coven.

The path to the Coven had always felt, to me, like a narrow row of stones cutting through a gray-greenish swamp. The air had been cold, the moisture had been audible, the towering plants had dripped onto iridescent water and broad lilies. I had walked that path four hundred times. I could walk it with my eyes closed. I could walk it while the subconscious did the chaining of routines and the conscious mind did nothing but admire the way the deathspider's webbing caught the faint, indistinct light.

Tonight, when I tried to find the path, the swamp was empty.

I mean: empty. Not closed. Not guarded. Not dumped — which is what the Coven does to lesser warlocks who try to enter without the right spell — but empty, the way a room is empty when the people who lived there have all, in the same night, moved out. The stones were still there. The lilies were still there. The towering plants were still dripping onto iridescent water. But the water had no current. The plants had no insects. The webbing had no spider.

I tried three alternate paths. Each was empty in the same way. Each was empty as if the Coven had not been moved but removed, the way a tooth is removed from a jaw, leaving only the impression of its roots.

Climax

I sat down on one of the stones. I pulled the hood of my jacket up over my head, because the moisture in the swamp was real to the subconscious even if the rain in the back garden had stopped. I sat there for a long time. The Coven did not come back.

I tried to call Mr. Slippery on the only channel the Coven trusted, which was a courier named, in the Other Plane, Evel. The courier did not answer. The courier had, the courier's subroutine told me, in the small polite subroutine of courier, been retired for the evening.

I tried to call Erythrina. Erythrina did not answer. Erythrina's subroutine told me, in the same polite subroutine, that Erythrina had been, for the last six days, in the small room where Erythrina kept the Coven's list of True Names.

I tried to call DON.MAC. DON.MAC did not answer. DON.MAC's subroutine told me, in the same polite subroutine, that DON.MAC had been, for the last six days, in the small wooden table by the fireplace, with his closed notepad and his empty usual-corner chair.

I had, in the long minutes of sitting on the stone, finally understood what the swamp had been trying to tell me. The swamp had been trying to tell me that the Coven had, in the polite subroutine of the Coven's small mind, decided to remove itself from the Other Plane before the Feds could remove it. The swamp had been trying to tell me that the Coven had, in the polite subroutine of the Coven's small mind, decided to leave behind the small impression of its roots, and the small impression of its stones, and the small impression of its lilies, and the small impression of its deathspider's webbing, so that the person who had been foolish enough to power up the Portal in the small hours of the afternoon would, when the person arrived, find the impression, and the impression's small subroutine.

I had been outrun.

I had been outrun by a Coven that had, for four years, been waiting for the Feds to come up my driveway.

Resolution

When I came out, the kitchen was quiet. The electrodes were cool in my hand. The cabbages outside were still ruined. The gold band on the taller trees had been replaced, while I was gone, by the gray of an overcast evening that did not intend to lift.

I made a cup of tea. I drank it at the kitchen table. I did not call the Coven. I did not call anyone.

In the morning I would call Erythrina. In the morning I would call Mr. Slippery — no, that was wrong, I was Mr. Slippery; in the morning I would call the operator at the LA Times data desk and ask, very carefully, whether there had been any unusual activity in the Northern California data infrastructure over the last twenty-four hours. In the morning I would go back to being Roger Andrew Pollack, TIN/SSAN 0959-34-2861, the man whose True Name was now in the hands of people who would, eventually, use it.

Tonight I was only the man in the kitchen with the cool electrodes and the ruined cabbages and the tea that had gone cold while I was gone.

I poured the tea out. I did not wash the cup. I set it on the table, upside down, so that no one could drink from it by mistake.

The electrodes went back into the drawer.

The drawer went back into the desk.

The desk went back into being a desk.

I went back into being a man.
