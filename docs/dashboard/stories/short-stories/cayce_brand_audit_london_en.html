Brand Audit: Cayce in London

Setup: The Trigger

6000 wordsBlue Ant Trilogy

"The future is there, looking back at us. Trying to make sense of the fiction we will have become."

— *Pattern Recognition*, opening epigraph

Phase 1: Opening Hook — The Logo in the Lift

Cayce's allergies fired before the lift doors closed.

It was not the brass plaque. Brass she could stand. It was the small enamel pin worn by the receptionist at the third-floor desk of the building on Wigmore Place — a pin she had not seen when she had walked past her thirty seconds earlier, when she had been looking at the brass plaque, when she had been thinking about the contractual small-print of her brief. The pin was the size of a thumbprint. The pin was the corporate-mark of a multinational Cayce had audited before, a household-appliance manufacturer that had spent three fiscal years rebranding itself into a "lifestyle-emotional-ecosystem," a phrase Cayce had seen printed on the back of a press-release she had memorised because she could not throw it away, because it was the kind of language that her allergies had been designed to catch.

The pin's enamel was white. The pin's enamel had a single grey letter at its centre. The pin was exactly the kind of pin that Cayce's allergies considered a hostile object, because the pin was not the pin. The pin was the brand. The brand was the pin. The pin was the corporate-strategy pinned to a stranger's lapel, the strategy reduced to a thumbprint of enamel, the enamel readable at the lift-bank, the lift-bank inside a corporate-welcome-suite, the suite leased by the very company whose rebrand Cayce had been hired to evaluate.

Then the lift doors closed. The lift was a small metal-room with a mirror-back wall and a polished-steel floor. Cayce saw herself reflected in the mirror. She was wearing a charcoal Uniqlo jacket she had bought in Tokyo seven months ago, and a black T-shirt, and a pair of new school-shoes she had not yet broken in. She was carrying a brown paper-folder that contained the brief and a printout of the client's rebrand-moodboard. The brown paper-folder was the kind of folder Cayce preferred to a leather-briefcase because the brown paper-folder did not announce itself, because the brown paper-folder was a generic-object, because the brown paper-folder could be carried into any boardroom in any city without firing a single allergy.

The lift ascended. The lift was, Cayce noted, the kind of lift that announces its own ascent via a soft-tonal chime at every third floor. The chimes were designed. The chimes were audible only above the threshold of hearing for a healthy adult. The chimes were the building's brand-audible. The chimes were everywhere.

Cayce put her hand on the metal railing inside the lift. The railing was cold. The railing's cold was the kind of cold that made Cayce's fingers stop noticing where they ended, a kind of cold that was useful in the first ten seconds of a corporate-engagement because it pushed the rest of the body's complaints into the background, the way a small cut on the hand can briefly eclipse a headache.

She pressed the small enamel pin into her mind. She could not unsee it. She could not forget that the receptionist had been wearing it. The receptionist's job-title, Cayce had read on the laminated card next to the brass plaque, was Reception Coordinator. The receptionist's name was Annika. The receptionist was the first surface of the brand that any visitor encountered. The receptionist was a surface. The surface was branded. The branding had fired Cayce.

The lift doors opened onto the fourth floor. The fourth floor was the executive-suite. The fourth floor was the floor where Cayce would be spending the next four hours. The next four hours would be spent looking at the client's brand-strategy and telling the client which parts of the brand-strategy were not yet branded, which parts of the brand-strategy were trying too hard to be branded, and which parts of the brand-strategy had no business being a brand-strategy at all.

Cayce stepped out of the lift. She walked past a wall of framed magazine-covers. The magazine-covers were framed in identical chrome. The chrome was, Cayce noted, an inch-thick. The chrome was the brand's preferred frame-width across all of its European offices. Cayce had seen the same chrome in the Paris office in March and in the Berlin office in May. The chrome was a corporate-constant. The corporate-constant was an allergy.

She walked into the meeting-room. The meeting-room was at the end of a corridor lined with a grey carpet. The grey carpet had a low-pile. The low-pile was specified in the brand-guidelines, section 4.7, "Floor-coverings: pile-height and acoustic-performances." Cayce had read section 4.7. Cayce had memorised section 4.7 because she had been paid to memorise section 4.7.

The meeting-room had a long oak table. The oak table had no centre-piece. The oak table had eight chairs, all of the same model. The chairs were upholstered in a grey wool that matched the carpet. The wool was the same grey as the chrome that framed the magazine-covers. The grey was not accidental. The grey was a brand-instruction.

Cayce sat down. She placed the brown paper-folder on the table in front of her. She opened the folder. She looked at the brief. The brief was a single sheet of paper. The single sheet of paper contained four paragraphs and a list of seven deliverables. The single sheet of paper was the kind of brief that Cayce preferred, because the single sheet of paper could be read in four minutes, and because the single sheet of paper's brevity was a sign that the client understood what they had hired her to do.

She waited. The lift behind her made another chime. The lift's chime was the building's chime. The chime was the brand-audible.

Phase 2: Rising Action — The Audit Begins

The client arrived at eleven. The client was three people. The first person was a man in a dark suit whose name was Marcus, who was the Director of Brand Strategy. The second person was a woman in a grey blazer whose name was Sienna, who was the Head of Creative. The third person was a man in a rollneck whose name was Otto, who was the agency's external consultant. The three people sat down around the oak table in three specific chairs. Cayce had already memorised the chair-arrangement from the meeting-room's overhead schematic, which had been emailed to her the night before. Cayce preferred to know the chair-arrangement in advance because the chair-arrangement was a brand-statement. The chair-arrangement said: I am in the centre. You are not.

Marcus opened his folder. The folder was a leather-binder. The leather was brown. The brown was a deep oxblood. The leather was monogrammed in gold at the lower right corner. The monogram said "M.H." in a serif typeface. The serif was the kind of serif that Cayce's allergies had been trained to ignore, because the serif was a private-mark, not a corporate-mark, and Cayce's allergies were strictly corporate.

"Thanks for coming in," Marcus said.

"Thanks for the brief," Cayce said.

"How was your flight?" Sienna asked.

"Long," Cayce said. She did not elaborate. Elaborating on flights was a corporate-ritual she preferred to abbreviate.

Marcus placed a USB-stick on the table. The USB-stick was silver. The silver was the same silver as the magazine-cover frames. The silver was the brand's silver. The silver was a corporate-constant.

"We've consolidated the new visual-language into a deck," Marcus said. "Twelve slides. Eight minutes. Then we'd love your read."

Cayce took the USB-stick. She plugged it into her laptop. The laptop was a ThinkPad she had owned for three years. The ThinkPad was not branded with a sticker. The ThinkPad's chassis had a single scratch at the lower right corner. The scratch was accidental. The scratch was the only accident she allowed on the laptop's surface.

The deck opened. The deck's title-slide read: Memorable — 2026 Brand Refresh: An Emotional-Ecosystem for the Post-Lifestyle-Consumer. Cayce read the title-slide twice. The title-slide was the kind of title-slide that her allergies would have fired on if she had been in any other profession. She was, however, a coolhunter. A coolhunter's allergies were, paradoxically, calibrated. A coolhunter's allergies fired on logos and brand-strategies that were already present in the market. A coolhunter's allergies did not fire on logos and brand-strategies that were not yet present in the market, because a logo that was not yet present in the market was not yet a logo. It was a prototype. A prototype was a thing Cayce's allergies treated as a kind of raw-material, the way a botanist might look at an unclassified plant without sneezing.

The deck was a prototype.

She opened slide two. Slide two was a colour-palette. The palette was a gradient from soft-mint to deeper-mint to a green that Cayce could not quite name, a green that was not the green of grass or the green of industry, a green that was a kind of clinical-green, the kind of green that hospitals used in their waiting-rooms, the kind of green that suggested we are not going to hurt you.

"This mint," Cayce said.

"Yes," Marcus said.

"It's a hospital-mint."

Sienna's eyebrows went up.

"It's not," Sienna said. "It's a wellness-mint. It's the colour of post-clinical-comfort. We've tested it in three markets."

"It's a hospital-mint," Cayce said. "The lighting-test is the test that matters, not the focus-group. Hospital-mint says: we are doctors. Wellness-mint says: we are not doctors. The difference is the lighting. Hospital-mint is lit from above. Wellness-mint is lit from the side. Your samples are all lit from above."

Sienna looked at her own deck. Sienna looked at Marcus.

"That's a really useful observation," Marcus said.

Cayce nodded. The useful-observation was the deliverable. The deliverable was a small observation. The small observation was a small correction. The small correction was the kind of correction that saved a brand-millions, because the small correction meant that the brand would not accidentally say we are doctors to a generation that had stopped trusting doctors in 2020 and had not started trusting them again.

Slide three was the typography. The typography was a custom-serif designed by a Berlin type-foundry Cayce had never heard of. The custom-serif was, Cayce noted, almost-but-not-quite a Bodoni. The almost-but-not-quite was the allergy.

"This typeface," Cayce said.

"Custom," Otto said.

"It's a Bodoni-derivative. The differentiation is in the terminals. The terminals have a slight curve, a slight counter-clockwise curl. The curl is, in your word, memorability. In my word, the curl is a wink. The curl says: we know we're trying to be a Bodoni. The wink is, in 2026, an allergy."

"A wink is an allergy," Otto repeated.

"A wink is a wink is an allergy," Cayce said. "A Bodoni is a Bodoni is a Bodoni. A Bodoni-derivative is a brand-strategy. The brand-strategy is what's failing. The consumer is past the wink."

Otto wrote something in a notebook. Otto's notebook was a Moleskine. The Moleskine was black. The black was a corporate-constant. The black was an allergy.

Cayce continued. She went through slides four, five, and six. She noted that the photography was over-stylised. The photography was the kind of over-stylised that a brand-strategy deployed when it had not yet decided what its product was. The over-stylised was a mask. The mask was a strategy. The strategy was, in Cayce's reading, a kind of panic.

She noted that the brand-voice was inconsistent. The brand-voice oscillated between clinical-precise and aspirational-poetic, an oscillation that Cayce had seen in a hundred brands in the last decade, an oscillation that signalled the brand-strategy was a compromise between two warring internal factions, the warring factions being the marketing-team and the design-team. The warring factions were at lunch in another part of the building. Cayce could feel them at lunch because the warring factions had left a kind of psychic-stain on the deck, a psychic-stain that Cayce could read through the typography and the photography and the colour-palette, the way a forensic-scientist could read a crime-scene through the dust on a tabletop.

She noted that the brand-strategy contained no mention of the client's competitors. The brand-strategy contained no mention of the client's product. The brand-strategy contained no mention of the client's actual customer-base. The brand-strategy was, Cayce realised, a brand-strategy about a brand-strategy. The brand-strategy was a deck about a deck. The deck-about-a-deck was the most common failure-mode of corporate rebrand-attempts in the post-2020 period, because the post-2020 period had made brand-strategies an end in themselves, because the post-2020 period had confused the brand for the brand-strategy, because the post-2020 period was, in Cayce's reading, allergic to its own products.

"This," she said, after she had gone through the deck, "is a deck about a deck."

The three clients looked at each other.

"It is a deck about a deck," Cayce repeated. "It is not a deck about your product. It is not a deck about your customer. It is not a deck about your competitor. It is a deck about a hypothetical-deck, a hypothetical-deck that exists only in your brand-strategy. The hypothetical-deck is, at the moment, a brand-strategy for a brand-strategy, a brand-strategy for an idea-of-an-idea-of-a-brand."

Sienna's face went flat.

"The deck," Marcus said, "is a starting-point."

"The deck," Cayce said, "is the ceiling."

The ceiling was, Cayce noted, a suspended-tile ceiling. The suspended-tile ceiling was the kind of suspended-tile ceiling that you found in 1970s office-blocks. The suspended-tile ceiling was the client's inherited architecture. The inherited architecture was not, Cayce noted, an allergy. The inherited architecture was a fact. The fact was the substrate. The substrate was the brand's actual starting-point.

She stood up. She walked to the window. The window was a tall single-pane that looked out over Wigmore Place. The Wigmore Place was a small square lined with Georgian town-houses. The town-houses had white stucco. The white stucco was the kind of white stucco that was no longer fashionable. The white stucco was, in Cayce's reading, the substrate of the entire neighbourhood. The substrate was a fact. The fact was the brand's actual ceiling.

"I want to come back tomorrow," she said. "I want to walk the building. I want to walk the neighbourhood. I want to walk five streets in five directions. I want to take photographs. I want to bring my own photographs back here. Then I want to look at your deck. Then I want to tell you what's missing."

"What's missing," Otto said.

"Everything that is not in the deck," Cayce said. "Specifically. The street. The customers. The product. The product in the customer's hand. The customer walking out of your shop with the product under their arm. The customer walking into another shop. The customer walking home. The customer opening the product in their kitchen. The product on the kitchen counter. The product next to the customer's coffee. The customer's hand on the product. The customer not thinking about the product. The customer thinking about something else. The customer not thinking. That is what's missing."

Otto wrote in his Moleskine.

Sienna looked at Marcus.

Marcus looked at the window.

Cayce sat back down. The chair was warm now. The chair's warmth was the kind of warmth that came from a person who had been sitting in it for more than twenty minutes. Cayce had been sitting in the chair for more than twenty minutes. Cayce's own warmth was in the chair. Cayce's own warmth was an allergy.

Then the lift behind her chimed again. The lift's chime was the brand-audible. The brand-audible was a chime. The chime was a single tone. The tone was a soft-E. The soft-E was the brand's preferred tonal-interval. The soft-E was an allergy.

She looked at the small enamel pin in her mind. The pin was the corporate-mark. The corporate-mark had fired her. The pin had been the first surface of the brand. The pin had been a kind of warning-shot, a kind of detonation, a kind of trigger.

The trigger had been, Cayce realised, the entire morning. The trigger had been the lift's chime. The trigger had been the chrome of the magazine-frames. The trigger had been the grey wool of the chairs. The trigger had been the Moleskine notebook. The trigger had been the oak table. The trigger had been the suspended-tile ceiling. The trigger had been the white stucco of the town-houses outside the window.

The trigger had been, in short, an entire corporate-environment. The corporate-environment was designed. The corporate-environment was meant to be invisible. The corporate-environment was meant to be the substrate. The substrate was, in Cayce's reading, the brand. The brand was the substrate. The substrate was the trigger.

She looked at her own brown paper-folder. The brown paper-folder was not branded. The brown paper-folder was a generic-object. The brown paper-folder was a kind of counter-substrate. The counter-substrate was, in Cayce's reading, the only thing she had brought into the meeting-room that was not the brand.

She took a breath. The breath was, she noted, slightly mint-scented. The mint-scented was the deck's residual-evaporation. The residual-evaporation was the brand's after-life.

Phase 3: Midpoint Reversal — The Trigger Re-Fires

Then, at lunch, the brand fired her again.

The client took Cayce to a private-dining-room on the seventh floor. The seventh floor was the executive-restaurant. The executive-restaurant was a single room with a marble-floor and a single long-table and a single chandelier. The chandelier was a Murano-glass chandelier. The Murano-glass was the colour of the deck's deeper-mint. The deeper-mint was, Cayce realised, not an accident. The deeper-mint was a corporate-constant. The corporate-constant was a Murano-glass chandelier.

The lunch was three courses. The lunch was the kind of lunch that Cayce had eaten in fifty different cities. The lunch began with an amuse-bouche. The amuse-bouche was a single teaspoon of pea-puree. The pea-puree was served on a small white plate. The plate had a corporate-mark on its underside. The plate's corporate-mark was, Cayce noted, a tiny blue-T. The tiny blue-T was the client's primary-mark. The tiny blue-T was an allergy.

She set the plate down. The plate's underside clinked against the marble-table. The clink was a small metallic-sound. The small metallic-sound was, Cayce noted, the brand's audible. The audible was a clink.

"How do you find the deck, really?" Sienna asked, with the kind of voice that Cayce had learned to hear as the voice of someone who had been told by her boss to ask a difficult question at lunch.

"The deck," Cayce said, "is the deck-about-a-deck I described this morning."

"Yes, but how do you find it, really?"

"Really," Cayce said, "I find it the way your receptionist finds it. From the lift-bank. From the lift's chime. From the chrome of the frames in the corridor. From the pin on her lapel."

Sienna looked at Cayce. Sienna did not understand.

"Your receptionist is wearing a pin," Cayce said. "The pin is the size of a thumbprint. The pin is white. The pin has a single grey-letter at its centre. The pin is the corporate-mark. The pin is what a visitor sees first. The pin is what a visitor carries out of the building. The pin is what a visitor carries home on the lift. The pin is what a visitor carries into the lift-bank at the end of the visit. The pin is, in your corporate-environment, the entire brand. The pin is the substrate."

Sienna was looking at Cayce. Sienna was, Cayce realised, not hearing her. Sienna was hearing her own voice in her own head, the voice that had asked the question, the voice that had been told by Marcus to ask the question, the voice that had been told that Cayce would be difficult, the voice that had been told to expect this kind of answer.

"The pin," Cayce repeated.

"Yes," Sienna said.

"Have you ever looked at the pin?" Cayce asked.

Sienna looked at Cayce.

"Of course I've looked at the pin," Sienna said.

"What does the pin look like?" Cayce asked.

"It looks like a pin," Sienna said.

"Describe it," Cayce said.

Sienna paused.

"It's a thumbprint," Sienna said. "White enamel. A single grey-letter."

"What is the letter?" Cayce asked.

Sienna paused.

"T," Sienna said.

"What does the T look like?" Cayce asked.

"It's a T," Sienna said.

"Describe the T," Cayce said.

"It's a serif-T," Sienna said.

"What kind of serif?" Cayce asked.

"A humanist-serif," Sienna said.

"What is the angle of the crossbar?" Cayce asked.

"I don't know," Sienna said.

"The angle of the crossbar is, in your design-system, 92 degrees," Cayce said. "The 92-degree crossbar is a corporate-constant. The corporate-constant is in your design-guidelines. The design-guidelines are in section 6.2 of the brand-book. The brand-book is on your intranet. The intranet is hosted on the same server as the deck. The deck is hosted on the server. The server is in a basement in Frankfurt. The Frankfurt-basement is owned by a company called, I believe, Brand-Stack GmbH. The brand-stack has a corporate-mark of its own. The corporate-mark is a kind of yellow-stack. The yellow-stack is, on its own, an allergy."

Sienna stopped looking at Cayce. Sienna looked at her plate. Sienna's plate had a tiny blue-T on its underside. The tiny blue-T was, Cayce noted, exactly the same tiny blue-T as the receptionist's pin. The tiny blue-T was, Cayce realised, the entire corporate-environment reduced to a thumbprint of enamel.

The corporate-environment was not, Cayce suddenly realised, a corporate-environment. The corporate-environment was a kind of autopsy. The corporate-environment was the brand laid out on a slab. The brand was, on the slab, exactly the same as the pin. The pin was the brand. The brand was the pin. The pin was the autopsy. The autopsy was the brand.

The brand was, Cayce realised, dead.

She set down her fork. The fork was a silver Laguiole. The Laguiole was the brand's preferred flatware. The Laguiole had a small bee on its handle. The bee was, Cayce noted, an allergy. The bee was the brand's preferred flatware-emblem. The bee was an entire corporate-strategy pinned to a fork, the strategy reduced to a thumbprint of silver, the silver readable from across the table, the table inside an executive-restaurant, the restaurant leased by the very company whose rebrand Cayce had been hired to evaluate.

"How long have you been in this building?" Cayce asked Marcus.

"Eleven years," Marcus said.

"Eleven years," Cayce said. "In eleven years, the pin has not changed. In eleven years, the crossbar-angle has not changed. In eleven years, the chrome of the frames has not changed. In eleven years, the grey wool of the chairs has not changed. In eleven years, the Laguiole-fork's bee has not changed. In eleven years, the lift's chime has not changed. In eleven years, the building's substrate has not changed."

"No," Marcus said.

"The substrate is not the brand," Cayce said. "The substrate is the substrate. The brand is what you change. The brand is what you are trying to change. The brand is what the deck is trying to change. The brand is what the deck-about-a-deck is trying to change. The brand is, however, the substrate. The substrate is, however, the brand. The substrate has not changed in eleven years. The brand has not changed in eleven years. The deck-about-a-deck is trying to change something that has not changed in eleven years. The deck-about-a-deck is, in this sense, an impossible-object. The impossible-object is an allergy."

She took a sip of water. The water was, Cayce noted, served in a small glass. The small glass had a tiny blue-T on its underside. The tiny blue-T was, she noted, the same tiny blue-T as the plate. The tiny blue-T was, she noted, the same tiny blue-T as the pin. The tiny blue-T was an entire corporate-environment reduced to a thumbprint of glass.

"The pin," Cayce said.

"Yes," Marcus said.

"The pin is not the brand," Cayce said. "The pin is the substrate. The substrate is not the brand. The brand is what is not in the substrate. The brand is what is missing. The brand is what is, in your corporate-environment, exactly the same as the pin. The brand is, in your corporate-environment, the missing-thing."

She stood up.

"I need to leave the building," she said.

Marcus looked at her. Marcus did not understand. Marcus was, Cayce realised, the substrate. The substrate was the substrate. The substrate could not hear her.

"I need to leave the building," she repeated.

"Yes," Marcus said.

She walked out. The lift's chime fired. The chime was a soft-E. The soft-E was the brand-audible. The brand-audible was, Cayce realised, not the brand. The brand-audible was the substrate. The substrate was not the brand. The brand was, she realised, outside the building. The brand was, she realised, on Wigmore Place. The brand was, she realised, in the white stucco of the Georgian town-houses. The white stucco was not branded. The white stucco was, in Cayce's reading, the substrate of the entire neighbourhood. The substrate was not the brand. The brand was what was not in the substrate. The brand was what was missing. The brand was, she realised, the missing-thing in the substrate. The missing-thing was, she realised, the entire neighbourhood. The entire neighbourhood was not branded. The entire neighbourhood was a kind of counter-substrate. The counter-substrate was, in Cayce's reading, the only thing the building had not yet absorbed.

She stepped out onto Wigmore Place. The Wigmore Place was quiet. The Wigmore Place was the kind of quiet that Cayce preferred, the kind of quiet that was not the brand's quiet, the kind of quiet that was the substrate's quiet, the kind of quiet that was the kind of quiet that was the kind of quiet that Cayce's allergies could not fire on, because the kind of quiet was not the brand. The kind of quiet was the counter-substrate.

She stood on Wigmore Place for eleven minutes. She did not count the minutes. The minutes were, Cayce realised, the substrate. The minutes were not the brand. The brand was what was not in the minutes. The brand was what was missing from the minutes. The brand was, she realised, the missing-thing in the minutes. The missing-thing was, she realised, the entire eleven minutes. The eleven minutes were not branded. The eleven minutes were a kind of counter-substrate.

She took a photograph. The photograph was of a door. The door was a Georgian door. The door was painted black. The black was not the brand's black. The brand's black was on Moleskine notebooks. The brand's black was on the chrome of the magazine-frames. The brand's black was an allergy. This black was not an allergy. This black was, in Cayce's reading, the substrate.

She took a second photograph. The second photograph was of a window. The window had a small pot of basil on the sill. The basil was not branded. The basil was, in Cayce's reading, the substrate.

She took a third photograph. The third photograph was of a cat. The cat was sitting on a doorstep. The cat was, Cayce noted, not wearing a corporate-mark. The cat was, in Cayce's reading, the substrate.

She stood on Wigmore Place for eleven minutes and she did not count the minutes. The minutes were not the brand. The minutes were the substrate. The substrate was not the brand. The brand was what was missing from the minutes. The brand was what was missing from Wigmore Place. The brand was what was missing from the cat. The brand was what was missing from the basil. The brand was what was missing from the door.

She went back to the building. The lift's chime fired. The lift's chime was the brand-audible. The brand-audible was the substrate. The substrate was, Cayce realised, not the brand. The brand was, she realised, the missing-thing in the substrate. The missing-thing was, she realised, the entire building. The entire building was, she realised, the substrate.

She sat down in the meeting-room. The chair was warm. The chair's warmth was the substrate. The substrate was, she realised, not the brand. The brand was the missing-thing.

Then the lift chimed again. The lift's chime was a soft-E. The soft-E was the brand-audible. The brand-audible was the substrate. The substrate was not the brand. The brand was the missing-thing.

She opened her laptop. She opened the deck. She looked at slide two. Slide two was a colour-palette. The palette was a gradient from soft-mint to deeper-mint to a green that Cayce could not quite name. The green was not the brand's green. The green was, she realised, the substrate. The substrate was not the brand. The brand was the missing-thing.

She closed the laptop.

She wrote, in the brown paper-folder, the words the brand is the missing-thing in the substrate. The words were handwritten. The words were not branded. The words were, in Cayce's reading, the substrate.

She stood up. She walked out of the meeting-room. She walked past the wall of framed magazine-covers. The chrome of the frames was, she noted, an inch-thick. The chrome was the brand's preferred frame-width across all of its European offices. The chrome was an allergy. The chrome was, she realised, the substrate. The substrate was not the brand. The brand was the missing-thing.

She walked into the lift. The lift's chime fired. The chime was a soft-E. The soft-E was the brand-audible. The brand-audible was the substrate. The substrate was not the brand.

She pressed the ground-floor button.

Phase 4: Climax — The Lunch

She went back to the seventh floor at half past one. The seventh floor was the executive-restaurant. The executive-restaurant was a single room with a marble-floor and a single long-table and a single chandelier. The chandelier was a Murano-glass chandelier. The Murano-glass was the colour of the deck's deeper-mint. The deeper-mint was, Cayce realised, not an accident. The deeper-mint was a corporate-constant. The corporate-constant was a Murano-glass chandelier.

The three clients were already at the table. The three clients were sitting in three specific chairs. Cayce had memorised the chair-arrangement. Cayce preferred to know the chair-arrangement in advance.

Marcus poured her a glass of water. The water was, Cayce noted, served in a small glass. The small glass had a tiny blue-T on its underside. The tiny blue-T was, she noted, the same tiny blue-T as the plate. The tiny blue-T was, she noted, the same tiny blue-T as the pin.

"The pin," Cayce said.

"Yes," Marcus said.

"I need to ask you a question," Cayce said.

"Please," Marcus said.

"When did you last look at the pin?" Cayce asked.

Marcus paused.

"I don't look at the pin," Marcus said. "I see the pin every day. I don't look at it."

"What does the pin look like?" Cayce asked.

"It's a pin," Marcus said.

"Describe the pin," Cayce said.

Marcus looked at the table.

"It's a thumbprint," Marcus said. "White enamel. A single grey-letter."

"What is the letter?" Cayce asked.

"T," Marcus said.

"What does the T look like?" Cayce asked.

"It's a T," Marcus said.

"Describe the T," Cayce said.

"It's a serif-T," Marcus said.

"What kind of serif?" Cayce asked.
