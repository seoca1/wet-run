"I'm allergic to brands. Literally. I get nauseous. That's why I'm good at what I do."

Introduction

I do not get bored. When I am bored, I cannot refuse, so boredom is a gift.

August 2002, New York. It was too hot. Manhattan concrete reflected the heat. Reflection was refusal. Shade was acceptance. I looked for shade. A corner café off 14th Street — a Blue Ant client meeting.

My name is Cayce Pollard. Coolhunter. I am allergic to brands. Literally. A logo and I feel nauseous. That is why I am good at this.

"Cayce." Hubertus Bigend spoke. Founder of Blue Ant. Six foot six from Canberra. Northern European vowels. Blue Ant is a guerrilla marketing firm. Headquartered in SoHo. It captures the drift of culture. Bigend trades in cultural options. The underlying asset is desire.

"I need your reading," Bigend said. "I need your allergy."

"What am I to read?" I asked.

"The Footage." Bigend said. "Video fragments circulating online. Seventy seconds apiece. No music, no dialogue, no narration. Only objects and spaces and gestures and moods. Images that precisely capture the longing and the anticipation of the viewer."

My allergy rang. Just thinking of the footage made me feel I might be sick. Therefore, the footage was culture. Culture was refusal.

Rising Action

"The footage is not one work," Bigend said. "It is the work of several makers. In different eras and different places. They share a common pattern."

"A common pattern?" I asked.

"The fragments differ by era. Fragments from the 1950s capture the 1950s. Fragments from the 1990s capture the 1990s. Each fragment is an emotional seismograph of its own time."

"So emotion is the pattern," I said. "Emotion repeats. It is re-presented. It is proven."

"Your father started the footage," Bigend said.

My father. Oh.

Andrew Pollard. A security specialist. A Cold War-era security specialist. The father of patterns was the father of security. Security protected the patterns. Patterns made the security. Father made me. I read father.

"Your father did not make the footage," Bigend said. "Your father discovered it. Discovery is greater than making. Discovery is culture."

"Discovery is me," I said. Therefore, discovery was culture.

Climax

"Cayce." Damien Vorcees spoke. A former Blue Ant employee. He investigated the footage and then vanished. Vanishing was culture.

"How far did you track the footage?" I asked.

"Moscow," Damien said. "Year 2000. There is a Russian artist. He made a replica of the footage. The replica was greater than the original. That was evidence of greatness."

"Why did you vanish?" I asked.

"The original footage protects me. The replica exposes me. Exposure is refusal. Refusal is culture."

"What is the difference between the original and the replica?" I asked.

"Both are viewed. The difference is culture."

"Your vanishing is culture," I said. "Your vanishing is our vanishing."

"Cayce." Damien said. "Do not give up your allergy. The allergy is your pattern. Pattern lets you read. Reading saves you."

But I could not give up my allergy. Giving up was refusal. Refusal was culture.

Resolution

My allergy differs by era. In the 1950s I was allergic to certain patterns. In the 1990s I was allergic to different patterns. In the 2020s I will be allergic to still other patterns.

Therefore, allergy is eternal. Eternity is culture. Culture is me.

Epilogue

The footage still circulates. Seventy seconds apiece. No music, no dialogue, no narration. Watching continues. Watching is culture.

My allergy still rings. Ringing is culture.
