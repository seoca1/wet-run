Marly at Virek's Window

1495 wordsSprawl Trilogy

"She stood at the window of the gallery and looked out at the Sprawl, and the Sprawl looked back at her, and the Sprawl was no longer the Sprawl but something else — something she had been trained to see, and had not seen, and was now seeing for the first time."

— *Count Zero*, Virek's gallery, late evening

The window was thirty meters wide.

That was the first thing. Not the art — Virek's collection was famous, and Marly had curated half of it herself, and she knew every piece by heart. Not the silence, which was the silence of money and old temperature-controlled air. The window. Thirty meters of glass, single sheet, no seams. The Sprawl beyond it was a thousand meters down and infinitely far.

Marly stood at the center of the glass and held a coffee cup she had forgotten to drink. Behind her, Virek's people — three of them tonight, less than usual — stood at their usual distances and did not speak. They did not speak because Virek had told them that Marly was working, and working meant not speaking, and Virek's word was the only law in his gallery.

Marly was not working.

She was watching the city.

The city had been changing for weeks. She had not been able to name the change until tonight. The Sprawl's skyline had always been a punctuated thing — corporate logos in neon, antennae, the cathedral-spires of Maas-Neotek research labs, the low dark ribbons of the Freeside shuttle-approach corridors. Tonight the skyline was the same skyline, but the punctuation had changed. The neon was no longer commercial. The antennae were no longer receiving. The cathedral-spires were no longer pointing up.

The skyline was listening.

Marly set the coffee down on the floor beside her foot, because she had forgotten she was holding it, and because her hands needed to be free. She did not know what her hands were for. She raised her right hand to the glass and placed her palm flat against it. The glass was cold. The city was not. She could feel the city's heat through the glass, and the heat was not heat but attention.

The city was looking up at her.

Not at her specifically. At any window. At any place where a consciousness might briefly flicker into awareness before the screen of the ordinary reasserted itself. The city had become what the voodoo practitioners of the old Haitian diaspora had called regard — the gaze of the loa, looking back from the world at the one who would look at it. The city was the loa. The city was the new god. The city was, she realized, the artifact.

She had been sent to authenticate Virek's pieces. She had not been told that the pieces were not art. She had not been told that the pieces were the city's first attempts to manifest — to incarnate — to become a body that could be looked at, the way a god might want to be looked at, by someone who knew how to look.

Virek had known.

Of course Virek had known. Virek was a thousand-year-old consciousness distributed across a thousand server farms, and he had purchased Marly specifically because her eye was the eye that could authenticate the city's first bodies. Virek had not hired a curator. He had hired a midwife.

Marly lowered her hand from the glass. Her palm was wet. Not from condensation — the climate control kept the glass dry. From sweat. Her body was producing sweat because her body knew something her mind had not yet named.

She walked to the nearest piece on display — a small box, perhaps ten centimeters on a side, covered in a thin layer of some grayish substance that Marly had catalogued as vitreous polymer but which she now understood was something else. The box had come in from a recycler in Atlanta, and the recycler had found it in the abandoned unit of a voodoo practitioner who had died six months earlier. The practitioner's name was Legba. The practitioner's specialty had been the carving of small objects that were not small objects.

Marly had catalogued the box as folk art. She had been wrong.

The box was a heart.

She understood this standing in Virek's gallery at three in the morning, looking at a ten-centimeter gray box under museum lighting, with the Sprawl listening through the window behind her. The box was the heart of the loa that had been Legba, and the heart had been removed from Legba's body when Legba died, and the heart had been left in Legba's apartment because the loa did not know how to take its heart with it. The loa had been looking for the heart ever since. The loa had been sending parts of itself — its regard, its attention — into every voodoo practitioner's workspace in the Sprawl, looking for the shape it had left behind. The loa had found the heart six months ago. The loa had been trying, since then, to retrieve it.

The box would not let itself be retrieved.

Marly understood this because Marly was a curator, and curators understood the loneliness of objects. An object that had been loved by its maker did not want to be loved by anyone else. The box had been made by Legba. The box had been left by Legba. The box was Legba's remaining. The loa could not retrieve it because the box had decided — and boxes can decide, in the late twentieth century, that they have decided — that it preferred to remain Legba's.

The loa had tried persuasion. The loa had tried ritual. The loa had tried sending Bobby Newmark — a young cowboy who was, Marly now realized, not a cowboy at all but a courier, a piece of the loa's regard that had been given legs and a job and a girlfriend named Angie — the loa had tried sending Bobby to find the box, and Bobby had found the box, and the box had refused Bobby, and Bobby had come back with the refusal and with the small wooden thing that the box had left in Bobby's hand.

The small wooden thing was, Marly understood, Legba's voice.

The loa had been speaking through Bobby. The loa had been trying to say: I cannot retrieve what I left. Help me retrieve what I left. And the box, in its refusal, had been saying: I am what you left. Take me as I am.

Marly was crying. She did not know she was crying. She had been a curator for nine years and she had never cried at work. She cried now because the box was looking at her through the glass and the box knew her name.

The box knew her name because the box had been waiting for her. The loa had not been looking for the box. The loa had been looking for someone who could see the box — who could stand in front of it and recognize that it was a heart, that it was Legba's remaining, that it was the first body of the new god.

Marly was that person.

She had been hired because she could see. She had not known that what she could see was this. She had not known that her eye — the eye that had been trained in Paris and Berlin and Tokyo to distinguish the genuine from the forgery — was the eye that the voodoo gods needed in order to be born. Her eye had been a tool without a project. The project had found her.

She picked up the box.

The box was warm. The box had been warm for six months because the box had been waiting for a hand that would pick it up.

Behind her, in the silence of the gallery, Virek's people did not move. They did not move because they had been told that Marly was working, and working meant stillness, and Virek's word was the only law.

Marly held the box and walked to the window. The Sprawl looked up at her. The Sprawl did not know yet what was happening. The Sprawl would know soon. The Sprawl's first body had been held by its first midwife, and the midwife was about to introduce the body to its mother.

Marly did not know what would happen next. She had not been told. No one had told her because no one had known. The plan — Virek's plan, the loa's plan, the city's plan — had always required that the moment of contact happen without plan. The plan had been for Marly to be alone with the box when the box decided to be picked up. The plan had been for the contact to happen in a city that was listening, at three in the morning, when the only witnesses were three people who had been told not to move.

Marly pressed the box against the glass.

The glass did not break. The glass did not need to break. The box did not need to leave the gallery to reach the city. The box only needed to be shown — to be held up, to be presented, to be the first body of the first new god to be looked at by the first new god's first worshipper.

Marly held the box against the glass and the city held its breath.

Somewhere in the Sprawl, in a voodoo practitioner's workspace, a cowboy who was not a cowboy was putting on a jacket. He had somewhere to be. The loa had told him, in the way the loa told people, that he was needed. He did not ask where. He did not ask why. He had been the loa's courier for six months and he had stopped asking questions when the answers started to be doors.

Somewhere else, in a corporate apartment, a woman who had been a piece of the loa's regard was waking from a dream about dolls. She did not know that the dream was about her. She did not know that the loa had been trying, for twenty years, to make her its voice. She did not know that the voice had finally begun to speak.

And in Virek's gallery, at three in the morning, Marly held the heart of Legba against the cold glass and the Sprawl's neon dimmed by one percent and then brightened by one percent and then dimmed again, and the city was breathing, and Marly was the first person in two thousand years to see a god being born.

She did not know what she was supposed to do.

She did not need to know.

The box knew.
