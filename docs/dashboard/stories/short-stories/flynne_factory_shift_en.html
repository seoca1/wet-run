Before the Polis: A Shift at the Print Shop

Origin: Flynne at Houdini's Foods before everything changes

1286 wordsPeripheral

The fabricator hummed the way it always hummed at six in the morning, which was a hum I had come to associate with the inside of my own skull. Six in the morning at Houdini's Foods was the hum and the smell of polymer pellets in the hopper and the soft wheeze of the dehumidifier trying to keep the moisture content of the air low enough that the printer wouldn't jam. Six in the morning at Houdini's Foods was the back door being unlocked by a hand that wasn't mine and a faint pulse of cold air that came in under the loading dock and settled on the floor like a stain.

"Earl," I said.

"Morning, Flynne."

Earl was the assistant manager. Earl had been the assistant manager of Houdini's Foods since before I was hired. Earl had a beard that he kept trimmed to a length that is, in the absolute culture of beards, neither here nor there. Earl had a wife in Asheville and a daughter at WVU and a truck that he kept parked in the lot on the east side of the building because the lot on the west side filled up with snow in the winter and the snowplow would push the snow against the truck and freeze it in.

"Earl, is the load in."

"The show is in."

"The load."

"The load is in. Two pallets. One of synthetic protein substrate and one of the actual protein powder that gets mixed in with the synthetic. The protein powder is the one from Wisconsin. The protein substrate is from the facility in Knoxville."

"The facility in Knoxville."

"That's what the pallet says."

I walked to the loading dock. The two pallets were sitting on the concrete at the height that pallets are always at, which is the height that makes you bend your back in a particular way. The first pallet had "PROTEIN SUBSTRATE / SYNTHETIC / KNOXVILLE" stenciled on the side in stencil ink that was already wearing off because the pallet had been used many times. The second pallet had "PROTEIN POWDER / DAIRY / WISCONSIN" stenciled on the side in the same stencil ink wearing off in the same places.

I picked up a box from the Wisconsin pallet. The box was heavy. The box had the weight of a thing that had come a long way to be heavy in this particular loading dock.

"Flynne."

"Earl."

"Why do you do that."

"Do what."

"Pick up a box and stand there holding it before you move it. You do that every morning. You pick up a box and you stand there with it for a few seconds. Why do you do that too."

"I don't know, Earl."

"You don't know why you hold the box."

"I don't know why I hold the box. I hold the box because I pick up the box. I pick up the box because the box is there."

"The box is there because you pick up the box. The cycle is recursive."

"The cycle is recursive, Earl. The cycle has been recursive my entire life. I pick up a box at six in the morning at Houdini's Foods and the box is heavy and the box is from Wisconsin and the box contains protein powder that is going to be mixed with protein substrate that is going to be fabricated into something that is going to be sold to someone who is going to eat it. The cycle is recursive. The cycle is also my life."

I put the box on the conveyor.

The conveyor carried the box into the back of the fabricator.

The fabricator hummed.

Earl had gone back to his office. Earl's office was a glass-fronted box at the front of the building that had a window into the production floor and a window into the parking lot. Earl sat in the glass-fronted box and looked at both windows in alternation.

I held the next box.

The next box was from Knoxville. The next box contained synthetic protein substrate. The next box was the same weight as the first box, or close enough that I couldn't tell the difference without looking at the number on the side, and I wasn't going to look at the number on the side because looking at the number on the side would not change the weight of the box.

"Flynne."

"Earl."

"There's a guy out front."

I looked up.

Earl was at his office window, looking out at the parking lot.

I walked to the front window.

There was a guy out front. He was standing next to a black SUV that I had not seen in the parking lot before. The SUV had tinted windows. The guy was wearing a dark suit that fit him in a way that suggested he had bought the suit at a place that did not sell suits to people who worked at Houdini's Foods.

"Flynne."

"Earl."

"The guy is asking for you."

"By name."

"By name. He's asking for Fisher. He's asking whether Fisher is on shift. He's asking whether Fisher is the one who does the print jobs."

"Earl."

"Yes, Flynne."

"The fabricator is going to run out of substrate in twenty minutes if I don't keep loading it."

"The fabricator is going to run out of substrate in twenty minutes if you don't keep loading it."

"Yes."

"The guy is out front."

"The guy is out front."

"Flynne."

"Earl."

"I have worked at Houdini's Foods for nineteen years. I have been the assistant manager of Houdini's Foods for fourteen years. I have seen a lot of guys out front in this parking lot. I have not seen a guy out front in this parking lot asking for Fisher by name."

"No, Earl."

"No, Earl what."

"No, Earl, you have not seen a guy out front in this parking lot asking for Fisher by name. You have not seen a guy out front in this parking lot asking for anyone by name. People come to Houdini's Foods to buy food. People do not come to Houdini's Foods to ask for people by name."

"No, Flynne."

"No, Flynne what."

"No, Flynne, people do not come to Houdini's Foods to ask for people by name. People are not coming to Houdini's Foods to ask for Fisher by name. There is a guy out front in a black SUV asking for Fisher by name. Something is happening that is not the cycle."

"Something is happening that is not the cycle, Earl."

"Yes, Flynne."

"I'm going to load the next box."

"I'm going to load the next box."

"Then I'm going to see what the guy wants."

"Then I'm going to see what the guy wants."

I picked up the box from Knoxville. I held the box from Knoxville. I put the box from Knoxville on the conveyor.

I walked to the front door.

The door had a window in it. Through the window I could see the parking lot. Through the window I could see the guy in the dark suit next to the black SUV. The guy was looking at his phone. The phone was the kind of phone that Earl did not have but that everyone in the parking lot except Earl had been talking about.

The door opened when I pushed on it. The air outside was cold and smelled like the air outside always smelled at six in the morning in Clanton, which was the air of a town that had gotten up early and not yet decided what it was going to do with the day.

I stepped out.

The guy looked up.

"Flynne Fisher," he said.

"Yes," I said.

And the cycle changed.
