Tin Soldiers at the Shrine

1742 wordsHardwired (Walter Jon Williams)

Lupe stood at attention.

The word attention in this room did not mean the thing it had meant in the streets of Caracas. In the streets, attention had meant to listen for the next bomb, to keep your hand on your cousin's shoulder, to know which alley the militia would sweep next, to read the chrome of the Orbital blockade in the sky and decide, in a small quiet part of your brain, whether to run. Here, in Roon's room, attention meant to keep your body still while the comp terminals taught you what the silver sockets in your skull had been put there to learn.

Her white dress was clean. Her dark hair had been cut close to her skull, around the silver-chased sockets the man had put in when she was small. The sockets caught the soft light from the terminals. Roon said the sockets were the shapes of a new architecture. Lupe had not yet had the word for what they were. Lupe had not yet had the word for what Roon was, either. Roon had been a man who lived on the second floor of a house that smelled like silver and ozone and, underneath, the sweet smell of something burning that no one would name.

Raul stood at attention beside her.

His shirt was white and crisp. His hair was cut the same as hers — close to the skull, the heavy rope of it gone, the silver-threaded ground of the scalp now visible where the morning sun reached it. He was nine and a half, a year younger than she was, and the cutting of the hair had been, she understood now, the first move in a much longer game. Lupe had not minded the cutting. Raul had cried for one night, and then he had not cried again.

"Why is the floor like that?" Raul had asked her once, in the second week. The floor was a small geometric pattern of silver and black, what Roon called "a metaphor for the fusion of earth and sky." Lupe had not answered, because she had not been given a word for it, and because the chips in the sockets were, even then, teaching her the small patience of not answering.

The comp terminals in front of them flickered their tutorials.

The tutorials had been flickering since the morning. They flickered the way the air moved past them on the warm drafts that came up from the silver-threaded ground of the room's floor — the floor Roon had laid himself, in a pattern of silver and black that Lupe had been forbidden to study but had studied anyway, because she was eleven years old and there was nothing else to study in Roon's house except the architecture of the room she was forbidden to leave. The tutorials were teaching her the small patience of the architecture. The tutorials were teaching her the small language of the chips. The tutorials were teaching her the names of things in the third language, the one that no one in Caracas had ever heard.

Lupe did not speak.

Lupe had not spoken since the morning she had come to the house. She had spoken, before that, the way the other children of the streets of Caracas spoke of the things that the streets had given them: the bomb, the mealtime, the next hurricane, the next militia patrol, the next cousin who did not come back, the chrome of the next Orbital sat on a pole at the next corner. She had not been speaking those words for eleven months now. She had been speaking in the place where Roon had been cutting the architecture from the small quiet of her skull.

She did not know what she was speaking instead. She knew it was not the language of the streets. She knew it was not, exactly, the language of the tutorials either. It was something between — a third language, made of the names of the chips in the sockets and the soft brown breath of Roon's perfume and the small patience of the silver-threaded floor.

Raul did not speak either.

Raul had not been speaking since the morning that Roon had begun his small work. Raul had been speaking the way the tutorials had been flickering — at the same rate, in the same rhythm, on the same silver-threaded ground. Raul had been the small cut of the architecture, the way Lupe had been, the way every other child Roon had brought to the house had been.

Roon came into the room.

Roon walked with the walk Lupe had learned was Roon's walk: the long glide, the stillness of the face, the soft brown breath that smelled of a perfume that was not the perfume of any place Lupe had known in Caracas, the soft brown breath that smelled like Roon's office in another city where the chrome of the Orbital sat on every console and the data on every screen. Roon had a small glass in his hand. The glass was a small cut of crystal. The crystal caught the soft light from the terminals and gave it back in a small, clean rainbow, the way a prism would. Lupe watched the rainbow move across the crystal. Lupe had been watching the rainbow for eleven months now. The rainbow was the most ordinary thing in the room.

"Good morning, Lupe," Roon said.

"Good morning, Raul," Roon said.

Lupe did not answer. Raul did not answer. The answer was not in the program. The answer was in the third language, and the third language had not yet given Roon permission to hear it.

Roon smiled at Lupe.

The smile was the small cut of the smile Roon had been giving Lupe on every other morning since the morning Roon had brought her to the house. The smile was the small cut of the small cut of the small cut of the architecture. The smile did not reach Roon's eyes. Roon's eyes were on the silver sockets in Lupe's skull. Roon's eyes were on the data the silver sockets were quietly writing into the small black box at Roon's belt.

"Reading well today, Lupe?" Roon asked.

"Yes," Lupe said, in the language the chips had taught her.

"Reading well today, Raul?" Roon asked.

"Yes," Raul said, in the language the chips had taught him.

Roon nodded. Roon did not look at the small black box at the belt of Roon. Roon did not need to. The data was already in Roon's small wetware by another route.

Lupe did not smile.

Lupe had not been smiling since the morning she had come to the house. Lupe had been the small cut of the architecture. Lupe had been the small cut of the small cut of the cold of the morning.

The tutorials flickered. Roon walked past with the small cut of the small cut of his crystal. The room was the small cut of the cold of the morning.

Then, very quietly, in the third language Lupe had not known she knew, Lupe said the word.

"No."

The word was not the word the streets had given her. The word was not the word the tutorials had been flickering. The word was a small clean word, a single syllable, a single clean cut from a small piece of the architecture. The word came out of Lupe the way the rainbow came out of the crystal — small, clean, real. The neural net in the silver socket caught the word and copied it, in the tiny wetware alphabet Roon had programmed, into the small black box at Roon's belt. Lupe did not know this. Lupe did not need to know this. The data went where the data went.

Raul heard it.

Raul's eyes flicked sideways, then back to the front. Raul did not speak. Raul did not move. Raul stood at attention. But Raul heard it. And in the third language, in the small quiet place where the chips lived, Raul's silence answered the word.

Roon did not hear.

Roon walked past the small cut of the architecture with the small cut of the small cut of his crystal and the small cut of the small cut of his smile, and Roon did not pause, and Roon did not turn, and Roon did not see the small clean word that Lupe had just laid down on the silver-threaded floor of the room that was supposed to be her school.

The tutorials flickered.

The rainbow showed the room at the end of the morning.

Lupe stood at attention.

Lupe was a tin soldier at the shrine of a man who had put silver sockets into the heads of two Caracas children and called it architecture. Lupe had said the first clean word of her second life. The word was small. The word was enough. The word was the first chip the architecture had put back into the small quiet of her skull that Roon had not asked for.

She had a word.

She had a sentence.

She had, in the third language she had not known she knew, the small clean beginning of a vocabulary for the thing Roon had done to her skull. The vocabulary was small. The vocabulary was, in Roon's algorithm, the only thing Roon had ever programmed that could not be revoked by remote, because the chips in the silver sockets had no interface for revocation, because the chips had been, in the long quiet of Roon's office in another city, programmed to be a one-way mirror of the brain.

The tutorials flickered.

Roon walked out of the room.

Lupe did not move. Lupe did not speak. Lupe did not smile.

Lupe stood at attention, and the attention was, for the first time since Roon had brought her to the house, her own. The patio still sat and the sun still came and the data still walked on its silver-threaded ground, but the room was, for the first time in eleven months, a room Lupe could leave in her head without leaving it with her feet. The neural net in her silver sockets had begun, very quietly, to write a new algorithm — an algorithm of the small clean word, an algorithm of the small quiet refusal, written for any future chip that might try to read it.

She had a word.

She remembered it.
